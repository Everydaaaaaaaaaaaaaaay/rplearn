### B组（第4—7题）

#### A Different Beat

The club forms at my new school covered half the noticeboard. I had always liked making music, and the drum group was there among the choices. Before I could write my name, I heard several classmates praising the video club. Nearly everyone beside me seemed to be choosing it. I hesitated with my pen above the forms.

At my old school I had learned a steady rhythm (节奏) and earned a small performance badge (徽章). Drumming had made me excited about practising, not just about standing on a stage. In this school, however, the video club seemed to be the place where friendships began. I wanted to fit in (融入), so I followed the others. I hoped sharing their choice would make the first weeks easier.

The video meeting soon showed me how little I knew about the work. Members discussed editing controls and camera angles, while I struggled to follow the conversation. I felt lost. I had chosen a place beside popular pupils, but that did not give me their interest or experience. I began wishing that the hour were a drum practice instead.

I asked for a short break and passed the music room. The drum leader was explaining how two different patterns fitted together. The familiar discussion felt like finding the right path after taking a wrong turn. I listened outside for a moment, then entered. I was nervous about being late to ask, but more certain about what I wanted to do.

‘Is there still a way for me to join the drum group?’ I asked Mr Bell. He said the regular team was full, but I could prepare as a reserve player. If someone missed a performance, I would be ready to take that place. I would have to learn both the higher and lower drum parts. I agreed at once, pleased to have a chance to work with those sounds again.

The video leader approved my request to switch (调换). Every regular drum player attended every performance, so I never went on stage as a replacement that season. Yet I was glad I had moved. I enjoyed preparing for a role I valued. Looking fashionable had seemed important when I signed the first form; choosing work that suited me mattered more by the end.

### C组（第8—11题）

When Finn cannot decide how to begin a school announcement, he opens a chat tool. In this fictional school network, he also uses it to plan a game and talk through a disappointing afternoon. He describes it as a digital companion (伙伴), although he continues to spend time with friends in person. His example shows why these tools are discussed as more than a way of checking homework.

A fictional survey involved 1080 pupils. From that same total, 810 said they had tried a companion tool, and 360 reported social interactions (互动) with one. Another question found that 540 doubted the advice, while 360 had discussed a serious matter with a tool instead of a person. These counts describe different questions, so the groups may overlap. A pupil could doubt a reply and still return to the conversation later.

A school coordinator said the tools were rapidly (迅速地) becoming **woven into** ordinary routines. Pupils were opening them for plans, feelings and conversations, not only for isolated tasks. The figures are self-reports and do not prove that using a tool causes a change in social skills. They also do not show that all users prefer digital conversations to meeting other people.

Several features may explain the attraction. A tool can be available when a classmate is busy, let a pupil return to the same question and respond without showing impatience. Those features are increasingly (越来越) familiar in the tools described by the report. Some pupils find the exchange convenient. Convenience, however, does not establish that the tool has a person’s understanding or that the suggested answer is dependable.

The coordinator raised a concern about relying (依赖) on a tool whenever someone disagrees with us. If pupils avoid every difficult conversation with people, they may have less practice explaining a view, listening to another one and working through a misunderstanding. A confident digital reply can also contain mistakes. These are possible risks, not outcomes the survey proved had happened to each pupil who used a companion tool.

The report sets possible benefits beside possible risks. It asks readers to consider what a tool offers while keeping time for real conversations. Some replies may be useful, but agreement on a screen is not a guarantee of sound advice or an answer to every friendship difficulty. Choosing a sensible role for a digital companion requires examining its limits as well as noticing its convenience.
