### 全组回填

Helping at my aunt's bakery, I suggested a numbered-token system. Customers would pay, take a wooden token and wait while their bread was wrapped. The arrangement would organise the queue and prevent double service.

Then a customer handed me token seventeen, although I had just called eighteen. I assumed he had missed his turn. A delivery trolley near the window had drowned out my voice, he explained. I pointed to the number on the wall instead of listening to his explanation.

My aunt stepped forward and wrapped his order. Afterwards, she asked why I had designed the system. “To make service fair,” I answered. “Then fairness must include people who cannot hear every call,” she replied. I had taken a useful tool and treated it as if it could replace all judgement.

We placed a large numbered card beside each wrapped order on a tray facing the waiting area. An uncollected order stayed there, so a customer could see whether it was ready. We tested the arrangement rather than assuming it would suit everyone. By lunchtime, fewer people were asking us to repeat the numbers.

As we closed, I counted thirty-nine paid orders but found forty tokens in the return box. I worried that the revised system had introduced another error. My aunt suggested checking the box before blaming the method. At the bottom lay a token from a different shop, round rather than square. A child had dropped it in while his mother collected her bread. The extra object had been mistaken for one of our own because I had counted without looking closely.

I laughed, then felt embarrassed. Twice in one morning, I had relied on a rule while overlooking the details in front of me. The system had not become useless; my confidence in it had become too absolute.

The next Saturday, I kept the tokens, used the tray and checked each returned piece. I asked customers whether the display was clear. Their replies prompted a final adjustment: we moved the tray away from the reflection on the glass. The same man returned and found his order easily. He praised the bread, not the system. For once, I did not need my clever idea to receive the credit.

A routine should make good service easier, not make people conform to the routine. I still value efficiency but leave room to adjust methods. When an unexpected case appears, I try to regard it as information rather than an inconvenience. Some cases reveal an exception; others reveal a careless assumption. The important thing is to find out which before reaching a conclusion. A wooden token can organise a queue. It cannot excuse me from paying attention.
