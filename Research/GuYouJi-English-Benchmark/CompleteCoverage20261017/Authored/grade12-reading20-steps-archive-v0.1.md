### A组（第1—5题）

#### A1

A small school exhibition does not become informative simply by placing many old objects behind glass. Visitors need to know why an object is present and what it can tell them. In this fictional project, pupils are preparing a display about everyday journeys. Their four-step method aims to make labels useful while leaving the limits of the evidence visible. It is intended for a modest collection, where a careful question may be more valuable than an impressive claim unsupported by records.

1. Select an object that can support a specific question. An old ticket might help ask how someone paid for a journey, but it cannot tell every story about the transport system. Check that the object is connected to the question you plan to display. Choosing the most attractive thing first and inventing a grand explanation afterwards can leave the visitor admiring an object without learning why it matters. A plain item with a clear connection may support a stronger label.
2. Separate what the object shows from what you infer. You may be able to read a printed date directly, while the identity of the passenger remains unknown. Write these as different kinds of information. “Issued in March” and “perhaps kept after a family visit” should not sound equally certain. If a useful record is missing, state the gap instead of filling it with an appealing story. An honest uncertainty can invite investigation rather than spoil the display.
3. Test the label with someone outside the project. Ask that person to explain what they think it says, then listen for misunderstandings before defending your wording. If “return” is understood as returning the ticket to the office rather than travelling back, the label needs attention. This small reading trial checks communication, not the historical truth of the ticket. A reader understanding a sentence perfectly does not make an unsupported claim within it accurate.
4. Preserve the reason for each revision. Keep the earlier wording, the evidence that prompted a change and the date. A later pupil may discover a related timetable and need to know why you described the route cautiously. Without a record, a careful revision can look like a typing accident and be undone. The final label should be concise, but the project notes need enough detail for someone else to trace its decisions and continue the inquiry.

### B组（第6—10题）

#### B1

Dear Mira,

You wrote that your club's first public reading evening did not attract a crowd. Before you decide it failed, I want to tell you about a single empty seat that mattered to our family. There was nothing grand about the hall where it stood, and no newspaper reported what happened. I remember the evening because somebody noticed who had not yet found a place.

My mother had just started working late shifts and could rarely reach the town reading group before its first story. Miss Rowan saved a seat for my mother beside the door, where arriving late would not interrupt the reader. When we entered together, I saw my mother's shoulders relax. She could listen without first explaining her work to a room of strangers. After the meeting she thanked Miss Rowan, who asked only that another late arrival receive the same welcome.

In the years that followed, my mother helped run the hall. She checked the entrance and left a few seats within easy reach rather than packing every row for a better photograph. It occasionally meant rearranging chairs after the reading had begun. I watched her accept that inconvenience as part of the purpose of the meeting, not as proof that the plan had gone wrong.

I met your grandmother when she volunteered to read a story there. She did not rush through the final lines while a late visitor was finding a chair; she paused and then continued without drawing attention to him. That quiet care first made me want to know her better. Much later, your grandmother and I started an evening reading circle at our own workplace, leaving room for people who could only attend part of a session.

Your aunt organised a mobile reading table when the old hall closed. Visitors could sit for one poem or stay for several stories. At first I missed the familiar rows of chairs, but she reminded me what they had been for. A seat is useful because somebody can take it, not because it stays empty for us. The arrangement changed; the welcome remained.

Ask your visitors what would make another evening easier to attend, Mira. The size of an audience is worth noticing, but it is not the only sign that someone has found a place. You can inherit the care without inheriting our hall.

All my love,
Grandad

### C组（第11—15题）

#### C1

A narrow sea crossing creates a distinctive challenge for birds migrating through the invented Varo coast. The birds in this fictional report breed north of the strait during summer and travel south for winter. Researchers wanted to know how the monitored birds timed their crossings and how a change in seasonal winds might affect suitable crossing periods. The project joins field observations to a conditional model, rather than presenting the future as an extension already recorded by the tracking devices.

Teams worked at several breeding sites over six summers. They caught 80 different birds and obtained feather samples from 66 of them for comparisons between breeding areas. The caught birds received tracking equipment, but only 51 yielded usable records covering a complete migration. Reaching exposed nesting places required boats, walking and repeated weather checks. The report lists totals for the whole project, so the 66 sampled individuals cannot be read as 66 birds newly sampled in each summer.

The 51 tracked individuals supplied 153 completed flights in total. Repeated journeys by a bird counted as additional flights, not additional individuals. Several birds crossed near the same headland in more than one season, yet they did not all cross on the same date. Autumn journeys lasted between 15 and 37 days. Birds from separate breeding areas often approached the coast along different routes before reaching the strait. These are descriptions of the recorded sample, not a rule that every bird must follow one path.

A wind model then examined the number of days likely to offer favourable crossing conditions under one selected climate pathway. If the modelled wind pattern develops and the birds continue to favour the same crossing conditions, the period offering favourable crossings might become shorter. This could make the timing of arrival more important. The model did not establish a certain loss of the population, and it did not account for every possible change in bird behaviour. A different pathway or a different response by the birds could change the result.

The report treats past crossing records as evidence of current patterns and the wind calculation as an exploration of one possible future. Keeping that distinction visible allows readers to see both why the strait matters and why further observation remains necessary.

### D组（第16—20题）

#### D1

Feeling capable of organising tomorrow's study is useful, but it does not answer every question about whether life as a whole is satisfying. A fictional report in the Meren area begins with this distinction. It compares an adult life survey with a school confidence survey, warning that their shared positive vocabulary should not disguise differences in the questions, the respondents and the scales.

The Meren Life Survey asked residents to rate overall satisfaction with life on a scale from 0 to 10. Respondents separately described emotions experienced the previous day. The report kept those answers separate instead of treating each good mood as a point added to life satisfaction. For its age comparison, it used samples aged 18–30 and samples aged 60–74. These age bands refer to the resident survey, not to the pupils in the later school study.

The younger residents' average was 7.4 at the first date, compared with 7.1 for the older band. In new samples three years later, the respective means were 6.9 and 7.2. The ordering of the two means had reversed: the younger band was now below the older one. The figures describe repeated samples, not individual records of ageing or proof that every member of a generation changed in the same direction. Neither band can be declared the most satisfied of all ages from this two-band comparison alone.

A separate Study Confidence Survey asked pupils about confidence in managing their study routines, using a scale from 1 to 3. Year 10 samples averaged 2.80 and then 2.45; Year 12 samples averaged 2.65 and then 2.50. Both means declined, with a larger numerical fall in Year 10 on the school measure. This paragraph adds a distinct educational trend, not a conversion between confidence and life satisfaction. For example, dividing or adding scores would not make the two surveys measure the same thing. The pupils, questions and score ranges all differ.

Among the later Life Survey respondents, longer unplanned online sessions were linked with lower life-satisfaction scores. No experiment assigned those sessions. Less satisfied people might seek distraction online, while irregular working hours might influence both routine and satisfaction. The researchers want later work to investigate these possibilities. The observed relationship is a reason for inquiry, not evidence that one change to online behaviour will solve every respondent's difficulties.
