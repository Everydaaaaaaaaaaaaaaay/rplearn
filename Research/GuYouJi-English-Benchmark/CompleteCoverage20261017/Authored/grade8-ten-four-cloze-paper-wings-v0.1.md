### 全组回填

My cousin Finn visited us during the spring holiday. He could turn an ordinary sheet of paper into an aeroplane with sharp, neat wings. Mine looked like folded shopping lists. Whenever my uncle praised Finn's work, I quietly pushed my own planes under the table, onto the floor between its legs, hidden by the long tablecloth.

Finn was not unkind. He offered to show me his folds, but I heard the offer as another reminder that I was less skilled. I said I was too busy and went upstairs. Later, I found myself copying his design from memory. My plane still looked different, and I tore it up.

The next morning, our little neighbour asked us to make planes for her toy passengers. Finn produced a beautiful one. Mine was rougher, but its middle was wide enough for a small paper figure. I expected the girl to choose Finn's. Instead, she put a passenger in mine and asked for another.

"His looks better," I said, pointing to my cousin. "But yours has a seat," she replied. "My passenger needs somewhere to sit."

Finn picked up my plane and studied it. For once, I did not hide it from him. He asked how I had made the wide middle. I showed him. Then he suggested a way to make the wings more even without closing the space for the passenger.

We made several together. Some dropped immediately; others flew across the room. Each time, we changed just one fold so we could see what made a difference. There was no prize and no reason to decide who was better.

By lunchtime, the floor was covered in paper. We both helped clear it up. My uncle asked which plane belonged to whom. Finn answered, "We made the last ones together."

I still admired his neat work, but I no longer treated it as proof that my own ideas were worthless. When he offered help again, I accepted. Before he went home, I gave him a plane with a passenger inside. He gave me one of his best designs. Neither was perfect, and that no longer seemed to matter.
