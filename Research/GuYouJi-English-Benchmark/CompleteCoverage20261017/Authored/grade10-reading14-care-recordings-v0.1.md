## 学生题面

### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Willow Community House

Willow Community House needs volunteers for its indoor garden room, where adults with learning disabilities grow small plants and plan displays. We welcome applicants from varied backgrounds who are interested in supporting other people’s decisions. A member of staff will introduce volunteers to the room before they begin working with visitors.

#### Position

Garden-room Companion (No pay; free meals during each duty period).

#### Place

Eastford

#### Hours

Part time. Two short weekday shifts, with times agreed in advance.

#### Role

You will help visitors choose materials, develop simple gardening skills and take part in shared tasks. You will explain agreed routines, protect visitors’ rights and help them look after their belongings. You will be able to ask staff for support when a task is unfamiliar. Our first concern is that visitors feel valued as people whose decisions matter.

#### Skills and Experience Required

Applicants must be honest and patient, listen carefully, hold a current driving licence, and communicate fluently in both written and spoken English. Listening is needed to understand visitors’ choices; a current driving licence and fluent English are needed for collecting materials and communicating with the team. Previous experience accompanying adults in a similar supported activity is preferred, but newcomers who meet the essential requirements are welcome to apply.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Maya reached the school recording room with ten minutes left before the lunchtime radio play. She had spent the walk imagining the clay bowl she hoped to finish after school. At the doorway, a student assistant told her that the sound files had been renamed. Maya heard the words, but her thoughts were still on the curve of the bowl, and she did not ask which names had changed.

When she opened the sound list, several familiar labels had disappeared. She felt particularly uneasy because she had prepared the sound order and promised the actors she would check every entrance. A mistake at an earlier rehearsal had made a quiet kitchen scene begin with a train whistle. She did not want the actors to face that confusion again during the broadcast.

Her first plan was to ask the student assistant for the old list. The assistant had already gone to a lesson, and the shared folder contained only the new version. Maya looked at the labels, unable to reconstruct their old order. Then she remembered the librarian’s advice about an unfamiliar catalogue: check what an item contains before deciding where it belongs.

She put on headphones and opened the files one at a time. The clip called “Morning” contained birds, “Arrival” contained a bicycle bell and “Inside” contained running water. She wrote these descriptions beside the new names and matched them to the three scenes in the play. Before the broadcast, she checked the order with the actors and marked the exact words at which each clip should begin.

The play went ahead using Maya’s annotated list. The bicycle bell followed the visitor’s entrance, and the tap began only when the kitchen scene reached the washing-up. When the assistant returned, Maya shared the descriptions so that the next operator would not have to guess. That afternoon she went to the art room, pleased that she could finally give her full attention to the bowl.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

A drawer in the neighbourhood parcel room offers reusable wrapping paper **at no cost**. Anyone may take sheets without paying, registering or leaving paper first. Residents who have suitable spare paper may add it to the drawer. The project began when a visitor wanted to wrap a birthday present and discovered that the room’s last large envelope was too small.

During the first month, a university student brought paper saved from a family celebration. A shopkeeper later took two sheets for a parcel. A delivery rider added unused rolls from home, while a retired nurse chose a small piece for a neighbour’s gift. No one was asked to state an occupation, but these visitors mentioned theirs while talking with the volunteers who kept the drawer tidy.

The paper does not have to look new. Helpers remove private names and addresses, and sheets must be large enough to use again. A small tear near an edge is acceptable; paper covered in glue is not. These conditions apply to what may be added, not to who may take a usable sheet. Volunteers do not try to judge a visitor’s financial situation from a coat or a shopping bag.

The organisers want sharing spare materials to feel as ordinary as borrowing a pen. They hope the drawer will make residents readier to offer a small kindness and to accept one when they need it. The room does not sell wrapping services, and the project has no target for turning occasional visitors into regular customers.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

A classroom message can now be prepared on a tablet and sent to a printer without anyone picking up a pen. This convenience has changed how often students need to write messages by hand. It also raises a question about lessons: if the final message is digital, is there still a reason to practise composing it on paper? A fictional research report explores the question through two limited activities.

The report makes a distinction between the appearance of a message and the decisions behind it. A page may look smooth even when its words do not express the writer’s intention. Equally, an untidy draft may contain a carefully chosen explanation. The researchers therefore examined what students produced and how they selected words, rather than awarding success for neatness alone.

In the first activity, thirty students prepared directions for a new class member, using paper for one version and a keyboard for the other. The order of the tools varied across students. The paper drafts contained more stated landmarks in this activity, while the digital drafts took less time to rearrange. The researchers recorded both findings and did not claim that the same pattern would occur in every kind of writing.

The second activity dealt with spelling choices. Students explained an intended message and then selected among similar word forms offered on a screen. Students with stronger spelling knowledge more often selected a form that fitted their explanation. The activity connected knowledge of word forms with the accurate expression of ideas. A suggested word was not necessarily the right one merely because it appeared in the list.

One teacher described a word forming on the **mental sketchpad** before it reached the page. A student starts with a meaning, imagines a possible arrangement of letters and only then writes or types those letters. No physical sketchpad is placed on the desk; the student can rehearse a word while looking away from the paper. This pause may reveal a letter pattern that needs checking before it is entered.

The report supports continued work on spelling and meaningful composition by hand alongside digital editing. A checking tool can present several possible forms, but the writer still needs knowledge to decide which fits the intended message. The activities described here are fictional and do not establish a general learning advantage for a particular tool, age group or teaching programme.
