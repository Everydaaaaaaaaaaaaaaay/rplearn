### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Reedbank Habitat Group — Habitat Crew Leader

The fictional Reedbank Habitat Group offers a paid placement leading young people in conservation work. The crew observes seasonal changes and helps maintain an outdoor learning area. Leaders coordinate daily work with site staff and make sure the student group can understand the purpose of each agreed task.

#### Key duties

Leaders guide 10–15 high-school students in teamwork, record keeping and straightforward site-maintenance tasks. They prepare the equipment list, keep attendance records and pass observations to the site team. Supporting a safe working routine and including quieter members are part of the role.

#### Schedule

The placement has two phases: spring (March–May 2027), Tuesday to Saturday; summer (June–August 2027), Monday to Friday. Leaders work 40 hours per week in either phase. The change of season changes the regular working days, not the weekly hours.

#### Location

Reedbank Outdoor Learning Site, near the fictional town of Alden.

#### Qualifications

Applicants must be at least 21 on the first working day and hold a current driving licence held for at least 3 years; exactly three years satisfies that minimum. Applicants must be able to lift 40 pounds and work outdoors. Current CPR and outdoor first-aid certificates are required before field duties; training to obtain them is available before the placement. Previous conservation work and project leadership are preferred, not compulsory.

#### Compensation

$750 per week, paid every 2 weeks. The payment for two ordinary weeks is $1500 before deductions. The placement includes staff training; this is a fictional notice, not an actual offer of employment.

#### Work challenges

Field sessions may involve heat, rain, insects and uneven surfaces. Some routes require carrying a field pack for several miles. Leaders need to adapt a plan to site conditions and work constructively with students from varied backgrounds. Staff support is available, but applicants should expect physical outdoor work, not solely office administration.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

The first repaired object at a fictional Alden community event was not a machine but a loose chair cover. A resident who knew sewing asked another visitor to hold the cloth while they compared the seams. A child wrote a label explaining which edge needed attention. By the time the cover was fitted again, several people who had arrived separately had begun working as a small group.

The Alden Mend-and-Meet session grew from a local appeal for residents willing to share practical skills. It now includes simple fabric repairs, a place to check small wooden objects and a desk for advice about finding specialist help. Visitors are not charged. Optional donations cover thread, labels and other basic materials; the event is not intended to compete with a professional repair service or promise that every item can be restored.

Tomas, who volunteers at the wooden-object table, says the event changed how he spent an otherwise quiet Saturday. He had skills from a former job but few reasons to use them with other people. Explaining a repair gave him a reason to listen as well as talk. He now recognises more neighbours outside the hall. The organisers present this as one volunteer's account of connection, without claiming a measured change in health or a result guaranteed for other participants.

The sessions are deliberately **inclusive**. Newcomers can begin by writing labels, comparing photographs or simply observing before joining a repair. Organisers place chairs so a visitor can sit beside a table without having to demonstrate a skill first. Younger members sometimes read a diagram for an older visitor, while experienced sewers show them how to examine a seam. There is more than one way to contribute.

At the end of a session, the organisers ask what people learned from one another as well as which objects were repaired. A finished chair cover matters, but so does the exchange that helped finish it. The project offers a meeting place around practical questions, making connections between generations that a request for a repair alone might never have created.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

When partners discuss housework, the final list of assigned jobs can hide a difference in responsibility. One couple may split tasks so that each partner owns particular jobs. Another may share the planning and completion of each regular chore. A fictional research report compares those arrangements, asking about reported fairness while recognising that a balanced total of work does not describe every part of a household routine.

The report compared 100 couples using shared responsibility with 100 using separate task ownership. In both groups, partners estimated roughly equal individual shares of their household's total work. 40 couples in the shared group and 20 in the separate-task group said their arrangements were fair. The proportions differed, even though the reported total shares were similar. The researchers had observed existing routines; they had not randomly assigned the couples to new ones.

A shared approach could make partners discuss the less visible requirements of a task. Yet the causal direction could run the other way: people with an established habit of cooperation might be more likely to choose shared responsibility. Other differences between the groups might also matter. The report sets out these alternatives rather than presenting the comparison as proof that one change in routine would produce a particular result.

An unfamiliar chore may also benefit from a “grass is greener” impression. A partner who rarely organises meals may see cooking as a short activity at the stove, overlooking the search for ingredients and the planning around other commitments. An outside researcher in the account comments, “Seeing the result of a chore does not necessarily reveal its full burden.” Taking a turn at the work could make its demands clearer. That is a possible explanation to investigate, not an additional result already established by the comparison.

The report's contribution is to make a pattern and the questions behind it visible. It asks why different arrangements might feel fair, and what further evidence would help distinguish explanations. It does not offer a universal rule for assigning chores, or conclude that the amount of work people do has no bearing on fairness.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

In a fictional design project, a six-fingered robotic hand can grip an object while attached to an arm and can travel across a tabletop after being released. Its designers are studying how grasping and movement might fit within one device. The account describes controlled trials with light objects. It does not describe a commercial machine already operating in unpredictable working environments.

“The useful question is what a hand can do when its design is allowed to change,” says the designer. Instead of limiting the fingers to the familiar movement towards one side of a palm, the team gave them joints that bend backwards as well. The hand can therefore use both sides for holding objects. This departure from a human outline is a design opportunity, rather than a claim that every ordinary hand movement has become unnecessary.

The researchers used a genetic algorithm to explore combinations of finger lengths and joint movement. Simulated candidates were tested against selected tasks, and better-performing combinations were carried into later rounds. Physical prototypes still had to be checked. A simulated grip could fail when a real surface slipped, so the search procedure did not certify practical success or replace observations of the built hand.

On the arm, the prototype could hold a light object against each side of its palm at the same time. Off the arm, it moved steadily with four supporting fingers while two held a small load. It could also use five for support and one for a lighter object. In both cases the roles added up to the same six fingers. The report distinguishes moving close to an object from actually securing it; a promising route across the table was not automatically a successful carrying trial.

One possible application is inspection inside narrow equipment spaces. The designers also discuss a future hand operated by a human user, but that would require research into control and the user's interpretation of unfamiliar movements. No such interface has been validated in these trials. The report welcomes the broader design possibilities while leaving the proposed human application dependent on further work.
