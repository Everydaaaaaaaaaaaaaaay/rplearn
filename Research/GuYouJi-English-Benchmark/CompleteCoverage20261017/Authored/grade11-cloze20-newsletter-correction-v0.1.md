# 高二二十空：印刷前撤下的标题

## 学生题面

On the school newspaper, I was known for writing lively headlines. When I heard that the old greenhouse might close, I thought I had found a story that would (1) ____ everyone's attention. A student said the gardener had called it “a waste of space”. I wrote the phrase at the top of my page without checking its (2) ____.

My editor, Nia, asked whether I had spoken to the gardener. I admitted that my information was (3) ____. She would not approve the page until I had heard his account. I thought she was being unnecessarily (4) ____. The printer's deadline was only two hours away.

The gardener looked puzzled when I repeated the words. He had been discussing a broken shelf inside the greenhouse. It blocked a doorway and could no longer hold plants. The remark referred to the shelf, not the (5) ____. As for closure, the building needed repairs, but no decision had been made. By removing the surrounding conversation, I had changed the (6) ____ of a perfectly ordinary complaint.

Back in the office, I deleted the headline. Nia did not say, “I told you so.” Instead, she helped me (7) ____ what we actually knew from what remained uncertain. We could report that repairs were being discussed. We could not report a closure as a (8) ____.

I worried that a careful article would be dull. Then we interviewed students who grew plants there and asked the gardener to explain the repairs. Their different perspectives made the story more (9) ____ without making it less accurate. The page went to the printer with five minutes to (10) ____.

The following day, the student who had repeated the remark thanked us for (11) ____ it before publication. He had heard only part of the conversation and had not intended to (12) ____ anyone. I had been ready to turn his incomplete memory into a confident (13) ____.

Our revised article received fewer comments than I had expected. Yet one reader said it helped her understand the issue rather than merely choose a (14) ____. That felt like a better (15) ____ of success. Since then, I have kept a note beside my keyboard: “What would make this account (16) ____?” It reminds me to seek information that might challenge my first impression, not just (17) ____ it. A deadline can require quick work, but it cannot (18) ____ a guess into a fact. I still enjoy a strong headline. Now I believe its strength should come from the (19) ____ beneath it, rather than the (20) ____ it creates.

### 答案与逐项依据

1. B。A：并非让大家注意力分散；B：catch attention表示吸引关注，符合追求抢眼标题；C：attention非待偿还款项；D：标题意在吸引而非逃过关注

2. A。A：后来证实省略谈话上下文改变意思；B：听到的内容不是音量核查任务；C：短语拼写无问题，错误来自谈话对象；D：不是核定该短语的所有权

3. D。A：没有保密承诺或秘密渠道；B：未提供统计数据；C：只听到片段，非详尽资料；D：由学生转述，没有直接问园丁

4. C。A：编辑记得需采访，没有忘事线索；B：要求核实恰是防粗心，不是认为她疏忽；C：要求先听本人解释，叙述者觉得过于谨慎；D：编辑在限制发表，不是赠予利益

5. D。A：架子挡门，但批评对象明确是架子而非门口；B：批评的是物件，不是园丁本人；C：谈话对象不涉及报纸截稿时间；D：shelf与整座greenhouse对比，非说整楼浪费空间

6. D。A：引语仍为英语，没有转换语言；B：原话仍为园丁所说，没有换发言人；C：省去上下文未改变抱怨发生次数，改变的是含义；D：省去上下文使普通抱怨的意思变成反对温室

7. C。A：并非为错误找借口，而是整理证据范围；B：protect A from B不表示已知未定的分类；C：separate known from uncertain把已知与未知分开；D：borrow A from B不能表达区分已知与未定

8. C。A：修理及未来关闭的不确定可作为可能性讨论，并未绝对禁止提及可能性；B：尚未决定可作待回答的问题，不是这里被否定的确定口吻；C：没有决定，不能把关闭写成确定事实；D：无决定不排除有人提议，不能拿proposal代表已定结论

9. D。A：不同视角增信息，不意味着结局更可预测；B：采访公开用于文章，非更保密；C：未增加费用或讨论成本；D：采访提供多角度具体信息，而非靠误导标题

10. D。A：分钟不是可发明的对象；B：没有欠别人五分钟的债务；C：赶截止并成功送印，不把剩余时间定义为浪费；D：with time to spare表示尚余五分钟

11. B。A：文章解释情况，并未隐藏该问题；B：发表前澄清话中所指，学生感谢避免错误；C：感谢的是澄清，不是再重复未经核实的话；D：不是宣传原误传话语，而是核清意思

14. B。A：没有选座或订票背景；B：读者理解争议，而非被推动简单选边站；C：讨论内容的理解，不是字体选取；D：文章并非供读者选日程

15. C。A：读者反馈已发生，非未来承诺；B：理解被认可不是成功的障碍；C：读者理解程度成为衡量成功的尺度；D：评价标准不构成费用

16. B。A：不是只找增加流行度的材料，而是检查真实可靠；B：下句主动找挑战初印象的信息，询问何种证据会显示报道不准确；C：强调证据与印象的冲突，非流行风格；D：没有报纸获利目标

19. B。A：标题下面并未靠装饰取得真实性；B：全文核实来源，强标题的基础应是证据；C：未经核实的传言是刚纠正的错误来源；D：截止压力不增加报道可信度

20. A。A：结尾对比证据基础与标题制造的兴奋效果；B：清楚表达也是正向目标，不是开头抢眼却误导的问题；C：作者追求准确，不能把accuracy作为要舍弃的煽动效果；D：读者理解正是新成功标准，非被否定的目的

### 全组回填

On the school newspaper, I was known for writing lively headlines. When I heard that the old greenhouse might close, I thought I had found a story that would catch everyone's attention. A student said the gardener had called it “a waste of space”. I wrote the phrase at the top of my page without checking its context.

My editor, Nia, asked whether I had spoken to the gardener. I admitted that my information was second-hand. She would not approve the page until I had heard his account. I thought she was being unnecessarily cautious. The printer's deadline was only two hours away.

The gardener looked puzzled when I repeated the words. He had been discussing a broken shelf inside the greenhouse. It blocked a doorway and could no longer hold plants. The remark referred to the shelf, not the building. As for closure, the building needed repairs, but no decision had been made. By removing the surrounding conversation, I had changed the meaning of a perfectly ordinary complaint.

Back in the office, I deleted the headline. Nia did not say, “I told you so.” Instead, she helped me separate what we actually knew from what remained uncertain. We could report that repairs were being discussed. We could not report a closure as a certainty.

I worried that a careful article would be dull. Then we interviewed students who grew plants there and asked the gardener to explain the repairs. Their different perspectives made the story more informative without making it less accurate. The page went to the printer with five minutes to spare.

The following day, the student who had repeated the remark thanked us for clarifying it before publication. He had heard only part of the conversation and had not intended to mislead anyone. I had been ready to turn his incomplete memory into a confident claim.

Our revised article received fewer comments than I had expected. Yet one reader said it helped her understand the issue rather than merely choose a side. That felt like a better measure of success. Since then, I have kept a note beside my keyboard: “What would make this account inaccurate?” It reminds me to seek information that might challenge my first impression, not just confirm it. A deadline can require quick work, but it cannot transform a guess into a fact. I still enjoy a strong headline. Now I believe its strength should come from the evidence beneath it, rather than the excitement it creates.
