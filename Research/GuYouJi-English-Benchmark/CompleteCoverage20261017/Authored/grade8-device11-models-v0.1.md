### B组（第4—7题）

#### The Club I Really Wanted

At my new school I stopped in front of the club noticeboard. There were groups for film, gardening and making things. The model (模型) club caught my attention at once. I liked planning small buildings from cardboard and explaining how their parts fitted together. Yet my hand moved towards the film-crew form.

At my old school I had earned a badge (徽章) for a model display and enjoyed every minute of preparing it. Here, however, many classmates called film-making exciting and model-building rather dull. I wanted to fit in (融入), so I put my name beside theirs. I told myself that being with the popular group would probably make me feel at home. I did not ask whether the work itself interested me.

At the first film meeting, everyone seemed to understand the editing program except me. I had no camera and little idea what the different settings meant. I felt completely lost as the others compared their plans. The posters on the wall seemed to be watching a visitor who had come to the wrong room. It was not the welcome I had imagined.

I asked for a short break and walked past the model room. Through the doorway I heard pupils discussing the shape of a station roof. Their conversation sounded like a favourite tune returning to me. I wanted to ask about the design instead of guessing what I ought to say. I took a breath and stepped inside. The group leader looked up.

‘Could I still join the model group?’ I asked. Ms Hart explained that the regular places were full. Then she offered a reserve role: I could prepare to take over if a member was absent. I would need to learn both planning and explaining, not just the part I already liked best. I agreed. The thought of doing that work made me much more hopeful than the film form had.

The film leader allowed me to switch (调换) groups. No model member missed a display that season, so I never presented as a replacement. Still, I did not regret moving. I spent the meetings doing work that mattered to me, and I learned that following my own interests could be more important than appearing popular.

### C组（第8—11题）

In our fictional school network, Eva uses a chat tool to plan a birthday quiz, choose materials for a poster and talk after a disappointing club meeting. She still meets friends at school, but sometimes opens the tool before asking them a question. Her example introduces a use beyond homework help: a digital companion (伙伴) that seems ready to respond whenever a pupil wants to talk.

A made-up survey asked 1200 pupils about these tools. Of the same 1200, 900 had tried a chat companion at least once, while 400 reported using one for social interactions (互动), such as discussing feelings or friendships. Also, 600 doubted the tools’ advice, and 400 had taken a serious matter to a tool instead of a person. These are different measures, and the groups can overlap. Distrusting advice does not necessarily stop someone from using a tool.

The network coordinator called the results a reason to pay attention. She said chat tools were rapidly (迅速地) becoming **woven into** pupils’ ordinary routines: they were no longer used only for a single homework task. This survey records reported behaviour, not proof that a tool caused a change in friendship skills. The figures also do not show that every user prefers a tool to a person.

Why might a pupil return to such a tool? It can respond at a convenient time, repeat an explanation patiently and allow the user to choose a topic. Such features are increasingly (越来越) common in the tools described here. Some pupils say a reply helps them organise a thought before a real conversation. These are possible attractions, not evidence that every answer is accurate or that the tool understands feelings as a person does.

The coordinator was concerned about relying (依赖) on instant agreement for every difficulty. If a pupil always avoids real disagreements by turning to a chat tool, the pupil may have fewer chances to practise listening, explaining a different view and repairing misunderstandings with classmates. A tool can also give a misleading reply. These are risks to consider; the fictional survey did not establish that any particular pupil had already lost those abilities.

The report therefore discusses both useful features and limits. It suggests keeping opportunities for real conversations while examining what a tool can and cannot provide. It does not demand that every tool be welcomed or rejected. A patient reply may be helpful in one situation, but choosing how to use it still requires judgement. Digital companionship is a question about benefits and risks, rather than a promise to replace friendship.
