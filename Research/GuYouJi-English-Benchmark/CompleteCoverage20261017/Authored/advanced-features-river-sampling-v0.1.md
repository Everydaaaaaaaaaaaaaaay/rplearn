### Keeping track of river samples

A fictional account of a local river survey distinguishes the origin of a sampling device from its later popularity. Several organisations shared equipment, and an item could appear in one organisation’s photographs long after another had developed it. For this account, the relevant origin is the earliest design explicitly credited in the project records. The history concerns how samples were labelled, divided and stored; it does not establish that the survey’s measurements represented the condition of every part of the river.

The Bracken Field Group was responsible for the split bottle tag. This was a label divided into two matching parts: one stayed on a bottle, while the second accompanied the corresponding notebook entry. Bracken’s members introduced it during the first season, before any of the other organisations adopted the system. They also developed the bank marker, a temporary numbered post used to show the precise point from which a water sample had been collected. The records attribute the first designs of both devices to Bracken, even though their appearance changed when other groups began making replacements.

Harbour Laboratory joined the survey in the following season. It used the split bottle tags and trained new volunteers to attach the two parts correctly, but its training materials identified Bracken as the originator. Millbank Team borrowed the same labels for a different purpose, connecting samples taken before and after a period of storage. Its original contribution was the silt divider, a tray that separated a collected deposit into equal working portions. The account credits Millbank with devising this tray. The divider did not replace the tags: one controlled how material was apportioned, while the other maintained the relationship between a bottle and a written record.

Storage created a further difficulty. Bottles placed at the back of a deep shelf were hard to inspect without moving those in front. Stone Bridge Workshop developed the turning rack, which allowed each row of bottles to be brought forward without rearranging their order. Its first design appears in a workshop drawing dated before the rack was used elsewhere. Willow Research Unit later assessed how frequently the rack should be turned during routine inspections. It published a useful schedule and suggested clearer numbering, but the account does not credit it with inventing the rack.

At the end of the survey, a shared manual described all four devices alongside the procedures developed by their later users. The editors kept the original design attributions separate from the names of those who wrote the clearest instructions. This prevented a successful training programme or a widely used inspection schedule from being mistaken for evidence of first invention. Equipment circulated between groups; the documentary record of its origin did not change each time that happened.
