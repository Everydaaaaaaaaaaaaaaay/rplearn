### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Volunteer internationally with Horizon Partners

Would you like to contribute your time and professional skills to projects in another community? Horizon Partners connects volunteers with local organisations working on community learning and access to shared resources. A placement offers a way to make a useful contribution while learning from the people who live there. The following conditions apply to this fictional programme.

#### Why volunteer with Horizon Partners

- Volunteers support locally chosen projects for peace and development, working with community partners rather than replacing their decisions.
- Teams include people from many countries and cultural backgrounds, bringing a range of viewpoints and approaches to shared tasks.
- A placement may have effects beyond its immediate activities when local partners continue using and sharing what they have developed together.
- Volunteers can learn about other cultures, build professional networks, study languages and gain professional and personal experience.

#### Requirements

- Applicants must be 25 or older, with no upper age limit, and hold a university degree or a higher technical diploma.
- Applicants need at least two years of relevant professional work and a good working knowledge of English, Spanish or French.
- Applicants must respect the principles of volunteering, work effectively in a multicultural team and be willing to cooperate with local people and organisations.
- Applicants need to adjust to difficult living conditions and sometimes remote locations, with strong interpersonal and organisational skills.

#### Conditions of Service

- Normal placements last six to twelve months; an extension of one to two years may be possible by agreement.
- Partners occasionally request shorter placements. Volunteers are entitled to specified allowances during their service.
- An allowance is not a salary, reward or payment in exchange for the work a volunteer completes.
- The allowances are intended to help volunteers maintain a secure standard of living at their duty station.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Leah traces her love of reading to evenings in her grandfather’s kitchen. He repaired bicycles during the day and read stories aloud after supper. At first she enjoyed the funny voices; later she began borrowing books so she could tell him what happened next. The habit continued long after she no longer needed anyone to read the words for her.

![Illustration of Leah beside donated books, not a photograph.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-shelves-B-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*Leah with books for local children*

Years later, while helping children at a neighbourhood homework room, Leah noticed how often a borrowed book had to be returned before a child could finish it. Some children had no books they could keep at home. She began collecting usable children’s books because she wanted reading to become something the children could choose freely, rather than an activity available only during a short supervised session.

Her first collection supplied 640 books to three homework rooms. Friends congratulated her, but she felt that a one-week collection could not meet a continuing need. She set herself a much larger goal: to place 120,000 books where children could take them home. She knew the number would require years of work and a system that did not depend entirely on her own cupboard.

Friends initially contributed books and money for purchases. As the project became known outside her circle, people she had never met began leaving boxes at a collection shelf in the community hall. Leah and two helpers sorted the books by reading level and checked for missing pages. Nothing was charged to the homework rooms receiving them. Small street libraries also received suitable books; they were recipients, rather than suppliers, in this account.

After three years the project had passed 26,400 books. Leah still had a long way to go, but the target helped her plan regular collections instead of waiting for a special event. She kept a notebook of rooms needing more books and asked helpers to check which ones children actually chose. Her ambition remained large, while the next task was often as small as finding a missing page or packing one more box.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

On the edge of a fictional wetland, a field team noticed that small reed birds often walked close together, separated into familiar groups and then rejoined larger gatherings. Sometimes two birds competed sharply for food; a few minutes later they fed peacefully near each other. These repeated changes in company made the researchers wonder whether the population had more than one level of social organisation.

![Illustration of one fictional reed bird, not a photograph or a social-structure diagram.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-shelves-C-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*reed birds*

A simple flock can form when many animals happen to use the same food patch. A multilevel society involves something more stable: recognisable small groups are linked within larger sets. Earlier explanations had often associated such complexity with large brains. The team wanted to examine the organisation of this small-brained bird without treating brain size alone as a reliable answer to the question.

For one year, the researchers made daily observations of 402 birds, identifying individuals by combinations of coloured leg bands. Of those 402, a subset of 52 also carried GPS devices that recorded their movements. The bands supported observations of who was near whom; the devices added information about where the tracked birds travelled. The team did not claim to have a continuous GPS record for every observed bird.

The records showed stable small groups within broader associations, with several pairs repeatedly choosing each other inside a small group. These patterns were not explained simply by all the birds reaching one feeding place at the same time. The report described a multilevel structure in this population. Its finding challenged the claim that a large brain is always necessary for such a structure; it did not show that every small-brained species has one.

The lead researcher offered a possible explanation. A bird might identify one or two familiar members and thereby recognise their group, without remembering every individual in the population. Combining groups could also help birds respond when food or threats changed. These ideas were interpretations to be investigated, rather than mechanisms the tracking records had proved. The report presented an observed structure and a reason to ask further questions about how it works.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

Breakfast is often described as a useful start to a busy day, but that familiar advice does not by itself explain how meal patterns relate to later feelings of energy. A fictional community survey examined that relationship. Its report discussed meal timing and reported tiredness, rather than testing disease, life expectancy or a treatment for any medical condition.

The researchers wanted to know whether eating once a day, missing particular meals or leaving very short intervals between meals was associated with reports of low energy later in the afternoon. They collected information about existing habits; nobody was assigned to skip a meal or follow a special diet.

At entry, 1,860 adults aged forty or older each described what they had eaten in the preceding twenty-four hours. This was one recall interview per person, not a daily series of reports. Follow-up questionnaires over the next two years asked about episodes of afternoon tiredness. Researchers compared patterns in the two sets of self-reported information.

In the survey, reporting only one meal on the recalled day was associated with more later reports of tiredness. Missing breakfast and having very short intervals between meals also showed associations. The report did not establish that these meal patterns caused the tiredness, and it did not show that every person with the same pattern felt the same way.

A nutrition educator commenting on the report focused on the food a person actually receives. “Meeting nutritional needs matters more than attaching a magic power to a particular clock time,” she said. “If people are **eliminating** meals and do not obtain the missing nutrients elsewhere, an important part of their intake may disappear.” This was her explanation of a concern, not a result from a randomized feeding experiment.

Several limitations affected the survey. A single day might not represent a person’s usual routine, and participants might forget foods or describe their habits inaccurately. Sleep, shift work and other unmeasured differences could influence both meal schedules and tiredness. A twenty-four-hour recall window should therefore not be mistaken for a record of every day during the follow-up.

The writer does not recommend making skipped breakfasts a routine shortcut without considering how food needs will be met. At the same time, the writer keeps that practical concern separate from a claim that this survey proves a harmful effect. The account invites careful reading of an association and its limits; it supplies no medical diagnosis or personal dietary plan.
