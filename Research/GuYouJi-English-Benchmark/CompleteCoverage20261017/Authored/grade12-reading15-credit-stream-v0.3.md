### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

### Baystone School certificate requirements

The following rules belong to an invented school. Students must meet all the requirements to receive its upper-school certificate.

### Compulsory credits

- 4 credits in English (one credit in each of Years 9–12)
- 3 credits in mathematics (including at least one credit in Year 11 or 12)
- 2 credits in project courses from the three approved groups
- 2 credits in science
- 1 credit in community history
- 1 credit in local geography
- 1 credit in the arts
- 1 credit in health and physical education
- 1 credit in an additional language
- 0.5 credit in career studies
- 0.5 credit in civic participation

### Optional credits

Students must earn 11 optional credits from approved courses. Up to 4 of these optional credits may come from approved partner-college courses; they are included, not added to the optional total.

### Online learning

Students who entered Year 9 in September 2026 or later must complete at least 2 credits online. These online credits count within the compulsory or optional requirements above; they are not extra credits.

### Literacy requirement

The literacy rule takes effect on 1 November 2026. Students graduating in 2026–2027 or later must satisfy the school’s literacy check as well as its credit requirements.

### Community involvement

At least 40 hours of approved community activity are required. These hours are not course credits. Before beginning, students must:

- check that the proposed activity meets the school’s approval rules;
- consult the school’s list of approved and prohibited activities;
- discuss the plan with a parent or guardian if they are under 18.

Students aged 18 or over must still verify that an activity qualifies, but the parent-or-guardian discussion rule does not apply to them.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

During an inspection in the fictional Merrow Bird Reserve, volunteer coordinator Sal noticed a nesting box tilted on its mounting post. A loose fastening could allow the box to move in strong wind. She recorded its number and referred it for inspection before it was put back into use. The team did not treat a box’s presence on the reserve’s list as proof that it remained in suitable condition.

Sal began by helping to organise the team’s observation notebooks. She learned that a brief entry such as “empty box” could leave important questions unanswered: had a volunteer looked inside at an authorised inspection, or merely seen no activity from the usual observation point? She now trains new members to record what kind of observation they actually made rather than turn an absence of sightings into a confident conclusion.

Maintenance workers **reinforced** the mounting assembly during a scheduled repair. They added supports to make it stronger, instead of replacing the whole box or only improving its appearance. Volunteers recorded the repair date and kept that information with later observations. A change after maintenance could then be considered in its proper context, rather than being treated as an unexplained event.

Two neighbourhood groups observe separate sections of the reserve. They now use the same box numbers and distinguish maintenance records from wildlife observations. When both groups described one box, the shared number revealed that their entries concerned the same object, not two different boxes. Their discussions also helped explain why a temporarily unavailable box should not be counted as an inspected empty one.

Sal welcomes the clearer records, but she does not claim that new supports alone determine whether birds use a box. Conditions and the timing of visits differ. Continued observation is still needed, and an uncertain entry is kept uncertain until there is a reason to revise it. Members need the persistence to return and the skill to separate what they saw, what was repaired and what they merely expected. Those distinctions make cooperation more useful than a larger total of unexplained notes.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Picture a student preparing to lead a short piece of music. Before the group arrives, he mentally moves through the opening: waiting for quiet, giving a signal and listening for the first entry. In the fictional account that follows, this imagined sequence serves as preparation for a shared activity. It is not a substitute for hearing what the group actually does.

Student leader Owen first imagined an opening in which everybody responded immediately. When his tutor asked him to picture a delayed entry, he realised that he had no plan for restarting calmly. He considered a second signal and then tried it during a real rehearsal. Thinking through an alternative did not make the interruption happen; it made a response available to test if the interruption occurred.

Owen later said that he felt calmer about giving the first signal. That report does not establish a change in every player’s timing, a permanent improvement in his leadership or a measured effect on his nervous system. The account gives one person’s experience and one practical adjustment. It would be a mistake to treat all those different outcomes as though they had been assessed together.

The process can be applied to other sequences. A guide might privately picture greeting a visitor who asks an unexpected question. A laboratory presenter could imagine putting down one object before picking up another. These examples involve imagining one’s own actions and possible responses. Simply listing facts about a task is different, although factual preparation may be necessary before a useful sequence can be imagined.

A mental version of an event can also leave out the very difficulty that needs attention. Owen’s first imagined group was conveniently ready and cooperative. The tutor’s question helped him inspect that assumption. The value of this kind of rehearsal therefore lies partly in revising an imagined scene and then checking it against experience. It can support preparation without guaranteeing confidence, physical skill or the response of other people.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A fictional engineering team at Lenton Studio has tested a tabletop network that changes how it supports a light load when a connection weakens. The project is an investigation of sensing and coordination. It is not evidence that living tissue can be repaired, nor a demonstration that a design is safe for a public bridge.

The network contains prebuilt links, small sensors and a controller holding a layout of their positions. A sensor first identifies a weak connection. It passes a position code to the controller, which compares the code with the layout and activates a supporting link nearby. A second reading checks whether the load has been redistributed as intended. The system does not grow a new link; it selects from links already built into the model.

This sequence distinguishes detecting a weakness from responding appropriately to it. In a trial with an incomplete layout, a warning was produced but the controller could not reliably choose the nearby support. The researchers also tested signals that did not indicate an actual weak connection. Those trials mattered because a system that activates supports unnecessarily may create a different problem rather than simply provide extra protection.

The team’s long-term aim is to design monitoring systems for repairable structures. That goal would require evidence far beyond the tabletop demonstration: different loads, outdoor conditions, repeated changes and human inspection procedures would all have to be considered. The current model says nothing sufficient about a particular real building’s safety, and the report does not recommend installing it in one.

The authors are encouraged by the clear division of roles among sensing, information checking and response. They present the mechanism as a useful idea to investigate, with failures supplying information for later designs. Its promise is not that a warning automatically makes a structure safe. It is that a coordinated response can be studied step by step, including the circumstances in which the information needed for that response is missing.
