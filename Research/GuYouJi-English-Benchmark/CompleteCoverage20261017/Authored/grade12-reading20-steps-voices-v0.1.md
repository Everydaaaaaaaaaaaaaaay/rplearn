### A组（第1—5题）

#### A1

An interview for a school history project can look deceptively simple: ask a question and record the reply. Yet a long recording may contain little that the project can use, while a brief account of one event can reveal a great deal. In the fictional project described here, pupils are collecting memories of how local spaces were used. The following four steps help them obtain a clear account without quietly turning another person's memories into their own argument.

1. Agree on a question before switching on the recorder. Explain the project and ask what the speaker is willing to discuss. “How was this square used when you were young?” offers a starting point; permission to record that conversation is not permission to publish every remark elsewhere. Agree on the intended use and any parts that should remain private. Technical preparation matters, but a perfectly working microphone does not replace an understood purpose.
2. Ask for an event rather than a general judgement. If a speaker says the square was lively, invite a description of one occasion: who was there, what happened and what the speaker did. A remembered evening concert may provide details that the word “lively” leaves unexplained. It is still one person's memory. Recording a vivid example does not establish that the square was equally busy on every evening or that everybody experienced it in the same way.
3. Check your understanding without supplying a preferred answer. Say, for example, “Have I understood correctly that the stalls moved after the rain?” rather than “They obviously moved because the organisers were careless, didn't they?” The second question inserts both an explanation and blame. Allow the speaker to correct the sequence or say they cannot remember. A pause or an uncertain answer may be more useful than quick agreement with an explanation supplied by the interviewer.
4. Return the account for a clearly defined check. Invite the speaker to flag names you misheard, passages they wish to keep private and places where your summary changes their meaning. State a reasonable date and offer a readable transcript rather than an unexplained sound file. This check can correct the representation of the interview. It cannot, by itself, verify every historical detail. A responsible project can preserve a personal account while using other records to investigate the wider history.

### B组（第6—10题）

#### B1

Dear Arun,

Your letter says you are worried about welcoming the new students at your college. I cannot give you a perfect sentence for every meeting, but I can tell you why our family has always kept a small desk near the entrance of a shared room. The furniture has moved several times. The reason for it began long before you were born, on my first evening in this town.

I had arrived with a suitcase and an address I could barely pronounce. Mrs Fen translated the caretaker's message and waited while I found the correct building. I remember feeling relieved rather than ashamed of needing help. She did not speak for me throughout the evening. Instead, she explained enough for me to ask the next question myself, then listened while I tried.

My mother remembered that distinction. When she later knew the town well, she helped newcomers read letters and find services, but made sure they could speak for themselves. I often saw her ask, “Would you like me to explain, or would you like to try first?” She was offering the choice she had once wished for, not collecting an audience for her own fluency.

Your grandfather came to one of her meetings carrying a notice from his new workplace. After receiving help, he returned the following week to practise with someone who spoke his language. His kindness, as much as his quick smile, drew me towards him. Eventually your grandfather and I arranged a welcome desk where neighbours could ask questions without having to join a formal class. It was not a place where one language was treated as the only respectable one.

Your father set up a language exchange at his library years later. Some visitors used picture cards; others wanted to rehearse a short phone call. He had adapted our way of making room for questions to a different setting. When he apologised that the library had no spare room for our old desk, I laughed. You need not inherit our room to keep its door open.

At your college, ask what help a person wants before deciding what to give. A useful welcome can end with the newcomer making the next move. You will find your own setting and your own words. What I hope you carry is the patience that once allowed me to find mine.

Yours with affection,
Grandma

### C组（第11—15题）

#### C1

In a fictional study of birds crossing the invented Selva Basin, researchers focused on places where migrants pause between long flights. These birds breed north of the basin in summer and move south for winter. A brief stop may allow an individual to feed before the next stage. The team wanted to distinguish the resting sites used in recorded journeys from sites that might remain available under a future rainfall scenario.

For five years, fieldworkers returned during the breeding season to several sites. Reaching them involved long drives followed by walks, and storms sometimes postponed work. Across the project they caught 72 different birds and took feather samples from 60 of them. Tracking equipment was attached to the caught birds, but useful complete-journey data came from 48 individuals. The report did not count an equipment failure as a bird choosing not to migrate, and the sampling total was not presented as the number caught each year.

The 48 individuals contributed 144 complete journeys. Some were followed in several years, so the journey count should not be read as a count of separate birds. Many tracked individuals used the same stopping area on successive journeys, although the length of a stop varied. Birds breeding in the western area generally paused beside inland pools; the eastern group more often followed coastal wetlands. Autumn journeys in the records lasted between 18 and 42 days, including the stops.

Researchers then used a rainfall model and maps of seasonal pools to ask where resting areas could persist. Provided that rainfall follows the selected scenario and stopover preferences remain similar, some inland resting areas might become unsuitable earlier in autumn. Coastal sites in the model changed less, but that was not a promise of unlimited capacity for arriving birds. Other rainfall pathways or a change in feeding preferences would alter the projection. No bird was observed taking a journey in the modelled future.

The account combines repeated journeys, distinctive stopping patterns and a conditional forecast. A frequently used pool matters because the tracking records show a connection to it, while its possible future loss comes from modelling. The report's warning depends on both lines of work without pretending that either supplies a certain timetable for every migrant.

### D组（第16—20题）

#### D1

Imagine feeling welcome at a training session but uncertain about your life outside it. A positive experience in one group is not necessarily the same as satisfaction with life overall. This example introduces a fictional report from the Orin region. Its writers compare two surveys while resisting the temptation to call every positive score a single measure of happiness.

The Regional Life Survey asked adult residents about overall satisfaction with life on a scale from 0 to 10. A separate set of questions concerned emotions felt the previous day. Those emotions were reported separately, rather than added together to produce the life score. The published comparison used samples aged 20–29 and samples aged 55–69. The survey did not ask respondents to rate their training group or count the number of friends they had made there.

At the earlier date, the younger age band averaged 7.0 and the older band 6.7 for life satisfaction. Four years later, fresh samples in those age bands averaged 6.6 and 7.1. Thus the older band's mean was now higher, reversing the earlier order. Because these were repeated population samples, the figures do not trace the change experienced by one fixed group of people. Nor do two age bands reveal which age is happiest across the whole population.

Another project, the Training Belonging Survey, used a scale from 1 to 3 to ask trainees about their sense of belonging in their training group. First-year trainees averaged 2.60 and later 2.35; final-year trainees averaged 2.50 and later 2.42. Both group means declined, but the first-year decline was larger on this measure. These results describe belonging in a particular setting. Their purpose here is to show a second trend with its own respondents and scale, not to turn 2.35 into a lower-precision version of 6.6. A trainee could feel part of a group while still choosing a modest life-satisfaction score.

In the later Life Survey, longer periods of solitary evening media use accompanied lower satisfaction scores. This association did not establish which came first. People feeling less satisfied might spend more time alone with media; working hours or access to company might influence both. The researchers recommend a study following people over time before proposing a direction of influence. Their current report identifies a relationship worth investigating, rather than a single demonstrated cause or a universal remedy.
