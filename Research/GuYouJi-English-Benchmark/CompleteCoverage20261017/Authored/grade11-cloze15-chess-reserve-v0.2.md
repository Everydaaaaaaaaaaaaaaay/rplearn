### 全组回填

I had captained our chess team for two years, so being named a reserve for the next match hurt. The coach said that recent results, not past titles, had guided her decision. I nodded, though I privately considered the choice unfair.
During practice, I recorded the moves of Arun, a younger player who had only started learning chess the previous year. At first I wrote them down mechanically, giving each position little thought. Then I saw him leave a piece apparently unprotected. I reached across to warn him, but the coach stopped me. Players had to make their own decisions. Arun's opponent took the piece, and two moves later Arun won. What I had taken for carelessness was a deliberate sacrifice.
Afterwards, Arun asked whether my notes showed the point at which his plan had become visible. He was not asking for praise; he wanted a record that could help him improve. We studied the moves together. I found a weakness in a different line of play, and he spotted one in mine. The discussion became an exchange rather than a lesson delivered by the older captain.
On match morning, one player fell ill. The coach asked me to take his place. My first response was relief, followed by a familiar urge to prove that I deserved my old position. Before we started, Arun handed me the notebook. Beside my earlier game, he had marked a safer response to an attack. I used that idea when a similar position arose and managed a draw.
Our team won narrowly. I could have treated the result as a personal triumph, but the notebook made that impossible. The useful suggestion had come from the player I had initially underestimated. Later, I asked to keep recording games even when I was selected to play. Being a reserve had forced me to recognise a strength that winning alone had hidden: I could learn from someone with less experience. I still wanted to earn my place. What changed was my understanding of how to earn it. Protecting a title mattered less than remaining receptive to advice.
