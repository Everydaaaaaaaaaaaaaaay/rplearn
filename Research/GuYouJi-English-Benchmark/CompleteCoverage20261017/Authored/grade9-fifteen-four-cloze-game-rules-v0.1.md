### 全组回填

Thirteen-year-old Leo volunteers at Oak Room's games club. Three board games stood unused. Their pieces were complete, but their rule sheets were missing.

The leader found the makers' official rules online. Leo used these as his source, rather than guessing from box pictures. He wrote short instruction cards, removing background stories but keeping steps players must follow. Each card named its game to prevent cards being mixed up.

One game used wooden stars as money. Leo wrote "take two". A tester asked, "Two cards or two stars?" He made the instruction more specific: "Take two wooden stars."

Leo watched new players. When they disagreed, he wanted to explain immediately. The leader asked him to stay silent and see whether his card answered their question. It did not. He marked the missing step and added it afterwards.

The leader compared his cards with the original rules. Leo had written that the lowest score won, although the original said the highest score won. He corrected this mistake.

Original instructions remain beside the cards. For rare situations not covered, players can consult them. A short card is a guide, not a replacement for complete instructions.

"Shorter isn't always clearer," Leo told the club newsletter. He records the questions asked most frequently and checks those sections first.

The three games are available again. Leo takes pride in that, without claiming he created them. The makers' names remain visible on his cards. Volunteers can suggest changes, but must check them against the original rules. Leo wants to help people play together, not give them reasons to argue.
