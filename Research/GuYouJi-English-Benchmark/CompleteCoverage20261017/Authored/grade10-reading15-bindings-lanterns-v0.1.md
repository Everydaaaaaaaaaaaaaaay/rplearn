# 高一完整阅读四篇十五题：书单、灯影展、相机支架与地图研究

## 学生题面

阅读A—D四篇的全部材料，回答第1至第15题；每题从A、B、C、D四个选项中选出最合适的一项。每题2.5分，共37.5分。书籍、人物、活动、产品及研究均为虚构。

### A组（第1—3题）

Our monthly readers’ column brings together four invented books chosen by the Harbour Reading Circle. Each recommendation comes from a different reader. These are introductions, not extracts, and no knowledge of other books is needed. Which would you put on your next reading list?

#### A1

The Last Platform — a novel by Mira Venn
When a railway line is about to close, sixteen-year-old Jo finds a box of unsigned postcards in its waiting room. Each chapter follows a different card to a former passenger, whose memory changes Jo’s first impression of the station. The mystery is not about a crime: it concerns who has kept the old room open, and why. Reader Evan likes the way ordinary journeys become a shared history.

#### A2

Between Two Covers — a memoir by Ada Pell
Pell recalls her first year repairing damaged books in a small workshop. She describes failed attempts as carefully as successful repairs and explains why an almost invisible stain can matter to an owner. There are no step-by-step repair instructions. Reader Suri recommends it to people interested in learning a craft through patient observation rather than buying tools immediately.

#### A3

Borrowed Light — a collection by Niko Vale
These short poems follow the changing view from one apartment window over a year. A travelling reflection, a neighbour’s curtain and a streetlamp become starting points for thoughts about distance. Reader Tom enjoys reading just one poem before breakfast. He warns anyone looking for a continuous plot that the collection deliberately leaves spaces between its separate scenes.

#### A4

The Quiet Orbit — a science-fiction novel by Lin Daro
A repair crew aboard an imaginary space station receives messages that seem to arrive before they are sent. The characters test several explanations instead of accepting the most exciting one. Reader Mei particularly enjoys the tense discussions: solving the puzzle depends on colleagues comparing incomplete records, not on a lone hero winning a fight. The story’s machines and physical rules are inventions.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

At the opening of the fictional Riverfold Design Exchange, visitors expecting a display of old festival lamps instead walked between tall screens of moving light. The screens carried patterns drawn from paper lanterns once made in the district. Twelve young designers had worked with six retired makers, but the final objects were intended for contemporary indoor spaces, not for use in a traditional ceremony.

The project began with a disagreement. Some designers wanted to photograph the lamps and copy their outlines onto fabric. Maker Wen objected that this would preserve the surface while losing the way a lantern was built. She opened an unfinished frame and showed how each narrow strip supported the next. The teams then used that principle of linked supports to develop folding screens. Visitors could see both the old frame and the new joints beside it.

The exchange was organised to let local makers and visiting design students learn from one another. Sales were not its measure of success: the objects were not offered for purchase. Instead, each team kept a record of a decision it had changed after talking to its partners. One student replaced a rigid plastic edge after Wen demonstrated how a flexible strip survived repeated folding.

Early public reactions were **tentative**. Several visitors said they needed to walk around the screens before deciding what they thought. By the end of the evening, the discussion board contained questions about construction as well as comments on appearance. Curator Sela called that a useful change. “A pattern may catch your eye,” she said, “but asking how it works can start a longer conversation.” The organisers plan another exchange, while admitting that one evening cannot show whether those conversations will continue.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Trying to keep a camera level on a narrow bird-watching platform can turn an enjoyable morning into a struggle. The fictional FieldRest stand is designed for that awkward space between a pocket tripod and a large professional support. This product introduction describes the maker’s proposed model; its features are part of an invented advertising scenario, not independently tested performance claims.

FieldRest folds into a flat rectangle that slides into the side pocket of its supplied case. Three legs open from the centre, but they do not have to be extended to equal lengths. On an uneven surface, you can adjust each one separately while watching a small level window. The camera plate stays attached to your camera, so attaching the camera to the stand requires one locking movement rather than repeatedly turning a screw. A red mark remains visible until the lock is fully closed.

If complicated controls have put you off similar equipment, notice the two large adjustment wheels. Each can be turned with gloves on. No phone account, wireless connection or software update is required. The maker includes a printed practice card so that you can rehearse opening and locking the stand at home before taking it outdoors. This example is meant to show ease of handling, not to promise that every user will obtain sharp photographs.

The standard pack costs 84 fictional credits and includes the stand, camera plate, case and practice card. A replacement plate costs extra. The stated maximum supported camera weight is two kilograms, and the instructions exclude use in moving vehicles or strong wind. A strap holds the folded legs together during transport; it is not a safety line for suspending a camera.

For a photographer who values a compact support without electronic controls, FieldRest offers an appealing option. Check your camera’s weight and the stated conditions before ordering. The maker’s return offer covers an unused stand returned within fourteen days, so practise the movements without mounting a camera if you may wish to return it.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

Would a map be easier to remember if its symbols resembled the places they represented? An invented study at Bellwater College explored that question with a simple indoor route. Its results suggest a possible design choice, but the researchers stress that remembering a short route in a quiet building is different from finding a safe path through a crowded town.

The team recruited forty adult volunteers who had never visited the test corridor. Participants were assigned by a random draw to two equal groups. Both received a map showing the same six turns and room positions. One version used small drawings of objects, such as a cup for a refreshment room; the other used plain numbered circles. Everyone studied the map for two minutes, then returned it and walked the route. A researcher recorded wrong turns without offering directions.

The drawing group made fewer wrong turns on average. It also took longer to study the map within the permitted two minutes. Because the team had changed the symbol style while keeping the route, room positions and study-time limit the same, the comparison provides some evidence about that design change in these conditions. It does not show that drawings make people remember every kind of information better. Nor does it establish what would happen if map users could consult the map while walking.

A second observation helped explain what the volunteers did. After the walk, several drawing-group participants described a sequence of pictured objects, while several number-group participants recalled a string of digits. The team suggests that meaningful pictures may give a route a memorable sequence. These comments are clues to a possible explanation, not a direct measurement of the mental process itself.

Independent reviewer Dr Sol welcomed the careful comparison but urged restraint. The sample was small, all participants were adults, and they were tested immediately. Children, longer delays and busy outdoor routes were not studied. Before changing public wayfinding systems, Sol recommended larger trials with different users and checks on readability at a distance. The team agreed and described its next task as testing the limits of the effect, rather than selling a universal map design.
