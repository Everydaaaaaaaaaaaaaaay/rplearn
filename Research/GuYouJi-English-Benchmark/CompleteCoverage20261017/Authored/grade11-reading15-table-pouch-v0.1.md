### A组（第1—5题）

#### A1

Westhaven Archive Access Service

The college archive can obtain a physical recording disc from a partner collection when its own copy is unavailable. It can also request an approved digital transcript of a recorded talk. These are separate services under the following fictional rules; ordering one does not include the other.

Eligible users
Currently registered college students, current employees and retired college tutors with verified archive membership may apply. Graduates without a present college role and holders of visitor tickets cannot use this service. Retired tutors should confirm their membership before their first request.

Applying and waiting
Use the online form and give the recording title, speaker and catalogue reference. Precise information helps the archive distinguish between talks with similar titles and limits delays caused by follow-up questions. Transcripts normally take two working days. A disc normally takes five to seven, although fragile or frequently requested recordings can take longer.

Access and return
Your notification email explains when the item is available. Pick up a disc at the college archive counter you selected on the form. A transcript is opened from the email link after account sign-in. The slip inside a disc case gives its return date. The supplying archive may require an earlier return; the date in that recall notice then applies. Disc loans cannot be renewed.

Fees
Choose the supplying archive’s row and the requested material’s column. These fictional credit amounts are charged once per request, with no annual membership fee added by this service.

| Supplying archive | Disc loan (credits) | Transcript copy (credits) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civic Archives | 16 | 7 |
| Commercial Archives | 24 | 11 |
| External Partner Archives | 36 | 15 |

Your college collection counter does not determine the supplying category. For a transcript, there is no physical disc to collect or return.

### B组（第6—10题）

#### B1

I still check the small repair pouch in my son’s instrument case before his first rehearsal of the month. It contains a pencil, a clean cloth and the simple spare part his teacher told him to carry. A neighbour once laughed that a student old enough to travel to rehearsals alone could surely check his own bag. She was right about what he could do, but not quite right about what the habit means to us.

At first, it was purely practical. When he was younger, a missing pencil could consume ten minutes of a lesson while everyone looked for one. We learned to replace what was used rather than discover an empty pocket at the next rehearsal. I liked the tidy certainty of it: three familiar objects, three small problems less likely to interrupt his work. That certainty is much easier to manage than the uneven business of helping someone grow up.

I have had to learn not to **hold on to** the role of organiser simply because it makes me feel useful. As his playing improved, I began slipping suggestions into the pouch: practise this passage, ask about that difficult entry. What I thought of as thoughtful reminders started to look like instructions travelling into a room where I was not present. He eventually asked if the pouch could contain tools again, rather than my opinions.

I was embarrassed, especially because he was not rejecting all help. He was asking to decide what help he needed. We agreed that he would check the contents and tell me if something needed replacing; I could still help find a spare part. The monthly check became a conversation at the kitchen table instead of a private inspection after he had gone to bed. If the pencil had already been replaced, there was nothing for me to correct.

One month he left for rehearsal with a drawing pencil that seemed too short to me. I nearly put in a longer one, then stopped and asked. He had chosen the short pencil because it fitted a small holder on his music stand. I had mistaken a deliberate choice for a problem. The explanation did not make me a perfect listener, but it gave me a useful example to remember the next time I was tempted to act first.

The pouch cannot protect him from a disappointing rehearsal, and I cannot pack away every difficulty he may encounter. What I can offer is a readiness to help with a specific request. I still care about the contents, but I care more about the agreement around them: useful help should support the work he wants to do, rather than quietly return responsibility to me.

### C组（第11—15题）

#### C1

A word can travel with an object and later be used where that object has never been seen. This possibility is explored in a fictional language project set in two invented harbour towns, Eska and Renn. Its records concern the made-up word tava. They are a reading case study, not a real account of any community’s language or a claim that all borrowed words change in the same way.

In the project’s earliest fictional records, Eska potters used tava for a shallow clay dish with a carrying handle. Traders took examples of the dish to Renn, where market workers began using the same sound as a convenient name for it. Researcher Mina Orr suggests that a shared label saved buyers and sellers from describing the unusual handle every time. At this stage, the use of the word and the movement of the object were closely connected.

A later set of workshop notes showed a change. Some Renn workers used tava for a handled wooden tray, even though Eska potters did not make that object. Interviews suggested that these speakers had noticed a useful similarity in function: both items could carry several small things at once. The records supported an extension in use, but they could not show exactly which person first made the extension or whether all residents accepted it.

An invented classroom experiment illustrated a related possibility without reproducing the towns’ history. Thirty adult volunteers learned an unfamiliar label for a handled container. Later, some also applied it to a different container with a similar carrying function. Others restricted the label to the original object. Thus the study showed variation among learners. It did not prove that a laboratory choice explains every change recorded in an actual community over many years.

Orr describes the broader idea by saying **a label can outgrow its first job**. A word initially useful for identifying one imported object may acquire another use when speakers find it convenient. The expression does not mean that older meanings must disappear. In the fictional Renn notes, tava continued to name the clay dish as well as the wooden tray. Context helped listeners decide which one a speaker wanted.

The project therefore follows usage rather than treating a word’s earliest recorded meaning as a rule that later speakers must obey. It also keeps evidence of change separate from guesses about an exact origin. A market worker summed up the fictional example: “The word travelled farther than the pot.” The remark points to how a name may reach new situations and objects, even when the original item does not accompany it.
