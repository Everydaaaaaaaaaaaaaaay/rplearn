### Testing a redesigned station sign

A fictional transport laboratory tested a redesigned station sign by asking visitors to follow a marked route through an empty training hall. Participants were unfamiliar with the hall, and each used only one version of the sign system. The route contained the same number of junctions for both groups. An observer noted wrong turns, requests for help and the point at which a participant reached the final doorway. Completion time was measured from the first junction, rather than from entry into the building, so the recorded times did not include the introductory briefing.

The study team reported that fewer visitors using the new signs asked for assistance. However, the authors separated this finding from the question of whether the design worked equally well for all readers. The pilot had recruited adults who could read the language used on the signs. No children were included, and the sample did not allow the team to compare readers with different levels of fluency. The report described the reduction in requests as evidence worth following up, while explicitly limiting the population to which that observation could reasonably be applied.

Before a further trial, the team recommended moving one arrow away from a doorway because its position could suggest that the doorway itself was the destination. They did not recommend changing the sign’s background colour. A record of the pilot and the proposed revision was sent to the station operator. The report contained no account of how individual visitors felt about the appearance of either design. The authors’ immediate concern was to make the next trial distinguish a positioning problem from a problem with the wording.
