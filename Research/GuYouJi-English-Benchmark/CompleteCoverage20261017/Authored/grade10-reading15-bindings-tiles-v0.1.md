# 高一完整阅读四篇十五题：书单、瓷片展、阅读架与修订研究

## 学生题面

### A组（第1—3题）

Welcome to the invented Pebble Street readers’ page. This month’s four recommendations concern places that change as people look at them differently. The books and their authors are fictional. Read these short descriptions to choose a story or account that matches your interests.

#### A1

The Room Above the Shop — a novel by Sel Arven
A teenager clearing an unused room finds labels for objects that are no longer there. She interviews former shop workers to reconstruct the room’s changing purpose. Some memories disagree, and she must preserve the disagreement instead of forcing a neat answer. Reader Lani admires a mystery in which learning to ask better questions matters more than finding a hidden treasure.

#### A2

Lines Along the Shore — illustrated essays by Bo Renn
Renn sketches temporary marks left by ropes, waves and wheels beside an imaginary harbour. Each drawing is accompanied by a short reflection on what can and cannot be inferred from a trace. Reader Deniz recommends it for short reading sessions. The essays can be read in any order, and the book does not offer a continuous adventure plot.

#### A3

A Seat for Everyone — a memoir by Ina Kest
Kest looks back on helping to run a tiny community cinema. Problems with seating, borrowed curtains and stubborn disagreements occupy more space than glamorous film premieres. Reader Hal enjoys the honest accounts of mistakes and the gradual discovery that welcoming an audience requires listening. It is a personal recollection, not a technical handbook for building a cinema.

#### A4

After the Signal — a science-fiction novel by Teo Marr
A distant settlement receives a message consisting only of a pause repeated at different intervals. An engineer and a musician propose rival explanations, then design tests they can both accept. Reader Wen likes their changing partnership and the way a confident guess becomes a question again. The settlement, signal and technologies exist only in the story; they are not a report of a real discovery.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Fragments that once lay in boxes beneath a workshop have become the starting point for a fictional exhibition at Moorwell Gallery. Ceramic workers and young furniture designers jointly created a set of small stools inspired by broken decorative tiles. The display does not present damaged tiles as rubbish to be hidden. It asks how a fragment can suggest a new way to arrange colour and space.

At first, the designers planned to copy complete patterns from photographs of tiled walls. Ceramic worker Dara proposed a different beginning. She gave each team three irregular fragments and asked them to trace the edges, including the broken ones. One team found that the gaps between its traced shapes formed a pattern as interesting as the coloured pieces themselves. It developed a stool seat with an open centre rather than a surface covered by copied flowers.

The collaboration was organised to share methods across crafts. Each team had to explain one choice to a partner who used different materials. A furniture student learned that a thin-looking tile edge was stronger when supported in a particular direction. Dara in turn changed the display label after a student asked her to explain a specialist word. Neither partner was treated simply as a supplier of attractive shapes.

The first seating plan for the gallery was **provisional**. After visitors in a trial session struggled to move between the stools, the organisers left more space and added a route around the outside. The objects could still be viewed closely, but visitors were no longer forced into a narrow central path.

Gallery director Ula says that the changes in the plan reflect the exchange itself. “We did not begin with a finished solution and then invite people to admire it,” she explains. “We let other people’s experience change what we made.” The gallery has recorded the process beside each object. Whether it will lead to lasting partnerships remains an open question for the next year.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Books do not always stay open where you want them, especially when you are following a diagram and need both hands. The fictional PageAngle reading stand is designed to hold a book at a chosen slope on a desk. This is an invented promotional description: the performance and price belong to the scenario and should not be taken as independent findings about a real product.

PageAngle uses a broad base with a supporting panel that clicks into four positions. Two sliding clips rest gently on the lower corners of the open pages. Their clear tips leave the page numbers visible, and each clip can be moved separately when you turn a page. The maker recommends testing the clips on a few pages first; valuable or fragile books are excluded from the intended uses. The clips cannot make a damaged binding safe.

A low front lip keeps a thick book from sliding onto the desk. When you are finished, the panel folds against the base and the clips fit into shallow slots. The folded stand is small enough for the maker’s supplied cloth sleeve. A short printed card illustrates the four positions and the release catch. In the campaign’s demonstration, a new user changes the angle without a tool. The example emphasises ease of adjustment, rather than claiming that a particular angle will cure discomfort.

The standard pack is priced at  thirty-six fictional credits, including the sleeve and instruction card. The stated book-weight limit is three kilograms. Replacement clips are available at an additional charge, but there is no electric light, automatic page turner or charging socket hidden in the base. Buyers looking for those functions should choose a different product.

If you regularly move between reading and practical work at the same desk, a fold-flat support may suit you better than a fixed shelf. Check the open book’s width against the dimensions before buying. The seller offers a display model for testing at its shop and advises customers to bring a similarly sized ordinary book. That small check can help you decide whether PageAngle fits your work, rather than relying only on the photographs.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

When people revise a notice, does a short checklist help them find problems that a general instruction misses? In a fictional experiment at Haven Learning Lab, adult volunteers revised a notice for a community room. The team measured corrections to that particular text. It did not attempt to measure a permanent improvement in writing ability.

Sixty volunteers were assigned at random to two groups. Everyone received the same notice containing a wrong opening time, an unclear booking address and a missing statement about bringing identification. They also received a separate information sheet giving the correct details. One group was simply asked to make the notice useful to a new visitor. The other received that instruction plus a three-item checklist asking them to verify time, booking location and required documents. Both groups had five minutes and could consult the information sheet throughout.

The checklist group corrected more of the three target problems on average. However, the team had provided both the checklist structure and specific prompts about what to check. The experiment cannot show whether a blank checklist, with its headings removed, would be equally useful. It supports a claim about this guided revision activity, not a claim that every list improves every piece of writing.

The researchers also counted unnecessary changes to correct information. The checklist group did not clearly differ from the other group on that measure. Keeping this result separate mattered: fixing a target error and avoiding a new error are different achievements. A single total labelled “better writing” would have concealed that distinction.

Reviewer Dr Elin welcomed the practical comparison but cautioned against using the findings to advertise a general writing course. The volunteers were adults, the task was short, and they had the correct information beside them. The study did not test whether they could identify problems in a new text without prompts a week later. Elin proposed a follow-up with unfamiliar notices and delayed tests. The team agreed that such evidence was needed before making claims about independent revision skills.
