# 六级：地名翻译与人物声音

### Passage 1

The translator of a fictional novel set in the invented region of Selna encounters a difficulty before completing the opening chapter. A hill outside the main town has a name that can mean either “shelter” or “concealment”, depending on who uses it. The town’s inhabitants disagree about an event associated with that hill. Choosing a single English equivalent would therefore appear to settle a disagreement that the novel itself deliberately sustains.

An editor initially recommends “Shelter Hill”. It is easy to pronounce, looks convincing on the map printed inside the book, and gives readers a phrase they can remember. The translator objects that this fluency comes at a cost. A character who describes the hill as a place where evidence was hidden would seem to be arguing against a reassuring official name. In the original, the character is also making use of a second meaning already present in the same word. Translation would change the resources available to that voice.

Retaining the Selna name offers another possibility, but not a perfect escape from interpretation. English readers would no longer hear either everyday meaning immediately. A glossary could explain both, yet consulting it would interrupt scenes in which the name occurs casually. Printing the name in italics might draw attention to its unfamiliarity even when, for the characters, it is an entirely ordinary part of the landscape. Each solution directs attention somewhere and away from something else.

The translator eventually keeps the original name, explains the two meanings in a brief introductory note, and lets later dialogue bring out their different associations. The editor accepts this arrangement after seeing a revised chapter. They do not add an explanation every time the hill appears. The aim is to give readers enough orientation to follow the disagreement without converting each mention into a lesson about language.

This decision does not establish a rule that place names should always remain unchanged. A name serving only to locate a journey might present a different problem. Here, the important task is to preserve the possibility of competing interpretations, rather than to preserve every visible feature of the original word. The translator describes her work as arranging a relationship among name, narrative and reader. An apparently convenient equivalent can alter that relationship; an unfamiliar expression can also distort it if its unfamiliarity is made more prominent than the story requires. Fidelity, in this case, involves deciding which difference the translation must allow readers to notice.

1. Why is the hill’s name difficult to translate?

   D. It would require the original author to remove the hill from the plot.
