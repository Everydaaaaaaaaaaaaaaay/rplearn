## 作者核对

### 1：archive

archive拥有影片并组织项目，cinema仅广告所在地点。

### 2：time code / timecode

time code标定位点；timecode连写为同一技术词的已列正字法变体，未作模糊词根匹配。

### 3：accent

音质好，难点明确是口音accent。

### 4：laughter

笑声改变话语友善/严肃含义，故保留文字提示。

### 5：line breaks

专段反思line breaks，按意群而非固定字符数分行。

### 6：rehearsal

试放叫rehearsal，非正式公映。

### 7：silence

空白停顿可传达内容，不必每处加解释。

### 8：punctuation

结尾明确标点变化比只盯单词更影响阅读。

### 完整原创独白脚本

I joined the subtitle project through a local film archive. I had assumed that I would be working for a cinema, because that was where I saw the advertisement. The archive, however, owned the films and arranged the project. Most of the recordings were interviews with people describing jobs that had disappeared from the town.

Each video came with a time code in one corner. I had watched films with numbers on the screen before without thinking about their purpose. Here the time code allowed me to tell the editor exactly where a sentence began and ended. Describing a point as halfway through the interview would have been useless when we were exchanging notes by email.

Understanding the words was not always straightforward. The microphones had been quite good, so background noise was less of a problem than I expected. It was the accent that caused difficulty. Some speakers used local pronunciations I had never heard before. A volunteer who had grown up in the area helped us with those passages, but never simply guessed an unclear word.

We also had to decide what to do with sounds that were not speech. Laughter was particularly important. In one interview, a remark looked unfriendly when it appeared alone on the screen. The laughter afterwards made the intention clear. We kept a short written indication of it, rather than letting the sentence seem more serious than it had sounded.

I spent far more time on line breaks than I had anticipated. Dividing a subtitle after the same number of characters every time made it difficult to read. We tried to keep small units of meaning together, so that a name was not left on one line while the rest of the phrase appeared below it. The words were right, but their arrangement still mattered.

Before the public screening, we held what the coordinator called a rehearsal. Several people watched the films without being told what to look for. They pointed out places where a subtitle vanished before they could finish reading it. That was more helpful than asking the people who had typed the subtitles to watch them yet again.

The most unexpected lesson concerned silence. I had wanted to fill every pause with an explanatory note. Watching the films with an audience taught me that silence could be part of what a speaker was communicating. We did not need to put our own interpretation into every empty moment.

The skill that improved most was my use of punctuation. I had thought mainly about the accuracy of individual words. Now I could see how a comma, or the end of a sentence, changed the way a viewer followed a thought. That was a useful lesson even when I went back to writing ordinary emails.
