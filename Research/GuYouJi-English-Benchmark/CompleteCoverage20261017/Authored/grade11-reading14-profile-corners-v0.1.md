### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Volunteer internationally with Common Ground Network

Would you like to contribute your time and professional skills to projects in another community? Common Ground Network connects volunteers with local organisations working on community workshops and shared reading spaces. A placement offers a way to make a useful contribution while learning from the people who live there. The following conditions apply to this fictional programme.

#### Why volunteer with Common Ground Network

- Volunteers support locally chosen projects for peace and development, working with community partners rather than replacing their decisions.
- Teams include people from many countries and cultural backgrounds, bringing a range of viewpoints and approaches to shared tasks.
- A placement may have effects beyond its immediate activities when local partners continue using and sharing what they have developed together.
- Volunteers can learn about other cultures, build professional networks, study languages and gain professional and personal experience.

#### Requirements

- Applicants must be 25 or older, with no upper age limit, and hold a university degree or a higher technical diploma.
- Applicants need at least two years of relevant professional work and a good working knowledge of English, Spanish or French.
- Applicants must respect the principles of volunteering, work effectively in a multicultural team and be willing to cooperate with local people and organisations.
- Applicants need to adjust to difficult living conditions and sometimes remote locations, with strong interpersonal and organisational skills.

#### Conditions of Service

- Normal placements last six to twelve months; an extension of one to two years may be possible by agreement.
- Partners occasionally request shorter placements. Volunteers are entitled to specified allowances during their service.
- An allowance is not a salary, reward or payment in exchange for the work a volunteer completes.
- The allowances are intended to help volunteers maintain a secure standard of living at their duty station.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Nora’s first memories of books involve an aunt who worked late shifts at the railway station. On evenings off, the aunt read plays aloud at the kitchen table, sometimes letting Nora choose a character. As Nora became able to read the lines herself, she wanted to find other stories they could perform. Her aunt’s patient attention made books a source of companionship before they became a school subject.

![Illustration of Nora beside donated books, not a photograph.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-corners-B-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*Nora with books for local children*

Later, as a volunteer at a family centre, Nora met children who enjoyed the weekly story session but had nothing to read between visits. She did not think interest alone would solve the problem if books remained out of reach. She began gathering children’s books so that the centre could give them away and children could continue reading at home whenever they wished.

A holiday appeal collected 920 books. The shelves looked full, but Nora knew several nearby centres had similar needs. She chose an ambitious target of 180,000 books and divided the work into monthly collections. Rather than pretending that she could finish quickly, she put a small table in a shared storeroom and recruited neighbours who could check books and pack parcels after work.

Friends supplied the earliest books and contributed money for purchases. When a town radio programme described the project, listeners who had never met Nora began delivering books to the storeroom. The volunteers sorted the donations and sent them free to centres and street reading corners. A small library at an evening learning club received some parcels too. The club did not send its shelves back to Nora or sell books on her behalf.

By the end of the fifth year, 43,800 books had been delivered. The original target was still distant, but Nora kept arranging collections and checking the reading corners that needed particular titles. She valued the long-term aim because it reminded the team that access could not depend on one generous holiday. For a child choosing a first book to keep, a parcel delivered that week already made a difference.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

A fictional observation team became interested in ridge birds when it repeatedly saw the same small groups separate from a larger gathering and later join it again. Within a small group, some birds seemed especially likely to stay together. They sometimes competed for food, yet their associations were not erased by every disagreement. This pattern, rather than an attempt to train the birds, prompted a study of their society.

![Illustration of one fictional ridge bird, not a photograph or a social-structure diagram.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-corners-C-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*ridge birds*

The team distinguished a multilevel society from a temporary crowd. If a population has recognisable groups within larger associations, it has more organisation than birds merely arriving at a rich food source. Some previous explanations linked this complexity with having a large brain. Ridge birds were small-brained in the fictional account, making them an interesting case for examining whether a large brain was truly necessary.

Researchers spent a year making daily observations of 358 birds marked with individual combinations of leg bands. They fitted 48 of those same birds with GPS devices. The observations provided information about repeated companions, while tracking described the movements of the equipped subset. There were 310 observed birds without GPS devices, so the researchers did not have continuous location records for the whole population.

The team found stable small groups within larger sets and repeated close partnerships within some groups. Larger gatherings could divide and reunite without losing all their smaller associations. The report therefore described a multilevel society in this population. A small brain did not prevent this organisation, although the work did not show that brain size had no relevance to any kind of animal behaviour.

One researcher suggested that recognising a few members could help an individual identify a group without memorising every bird. Flexible combinations of groups might also be useful when a food supply or a threat changed. These were possible explanations, not demonstrated causes. The team could report the layered pattern with confidence while leaving open how much recognition and learning an individual bird required to maintain it.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

A fictional workplace newsletter asks whether regularly missing breakfast should be regarded as an uncomplicated way to save time. It refers to a survey of meal schedules and reports of feeling low in energy. The account is written to illustrate the interpretation of observational information. It does not report mortality, establish a medical risk or test a treatment.

The researchers asked whether existing patterns such as eating one meal a day, omitting a meal or eating meals very close together were associated with later reports of afternoon fatigue. Participants were not told to adopt any particular schedule. Because the routines already existed, the study could describe relationships without showing what would happen if a routine were deliberately changed.

At enrolment, 1,640 adults aged forty-two or older each recalled their food and meals during the previous twenty-four hours. Each person completed this dietary recall once. Three later questionnaires, six months apart, asked about periods of low energy during the afternoon. The analysis compared the entry-day meal information with these later self-reports; it was not based on a new dietary interview every day.

The survey found that some meal patterns were linked to more reports of low energy. Among them were reporting only one meal on the recalled day, omitting breakfast and leaving very short intervals between meals. A link did not establish that the meal pattern caused the reported fatigue. Nor did it mean every participant with a particular meal pattern reported fatigue at all three follow-ups.

A nutrition educator discussing the findings turned to the question of overall intake. She said that **forgoing** a meal without obtaining its nutrients elsewhere could leave an unmet need. “The important issue is whether the whole day provides what a person needs, rather than whether a meal has a popular label,” she explained. This was a view offered to interpret a concern, not an experimentally measured effect of breakfast in the survey.

A person’s remembered day might differ from their usual habits. Foods could be forgotten, and later reports of energy were not direct laboratory measurements. Sleep, job schedules and other differences were not fully measured and might influence both sides of the association. The twenty-four-hour phrase defined a recall window; it did not mean that meals were reported daily throughout the following eighteen months.

The writer is unconvinced that routine breakfast omission is a harmless shortcut when there has been no thought about meeting food needs. However, the writer also rejects turning these survey associations into certainty about what causes fatigue. Concern about a habit and caution about evidence can coexist. The fictional account offers neither a diagnosis nor an individual plan for what to eat.
