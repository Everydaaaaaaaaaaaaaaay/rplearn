# 初一独立选做阅读三题：借书队列调查

## 学生题面

独立选做阅读：阅读全部六段，回答3题；每题从A、B、C中选出最合适的一项。每题2分，共6分。文中学校调查和数据均为虚构，请只依据本文定义、材料及其明确限制作答。

Have you ever borrowed more library books than you could read? A large pile can look exciting on the first afternoon. A week later, however, some books may still be closed while other readers are waiting for them. In a report about several school libraries, students used the name ‘book piling’ for a particular borrowing habit. The name describes a habit in the report, not every large pile of books.

The report uses two features together. First, a student borrows more books than the student plans to read before the return date. Second, that student returns at least one of them without opening it. Simply borrowing a long book is not enough. A keen reader who opens and reads every borrowed book does not meet this definition, even when the reader carries a heavy bag.

Why investigate this habit? A book lying unopened in one bag cannot reach a classmate who needs it. The waiting list may grow although enough reading time is available elsewhere. Understanding the habit could help libraries design fairer borrowing arrangements. The aim is to make books easier to share, rather than to decide which students are good or bad readers.

For this fictional study, the team asked 2184 students about the previous three months. Students reported how many books they had borrowed, how many they had opened and why they had chosen them. The team also compared these reports with the dates on library records. The records showed borrowing and returning, but could not show whether a page had actually been read.

Students who reported borrowing books mainly because friends chose them also reported more book piling. Those who made a short reading plan reported less. These are connections in the reports, not proof that following a friend causes the habit. Some students may also remember their reading less clearly than others, so the figures need careful interpretation.

The team suggested trying a small reading-plan card before changing any borrowing limit. A library could then check whether books reached waiting readers sooner. The study did not test that card, so it cannot tell us whether the idea works. Its findings describe the surveyed libraries and should not be treated as a rule for all young readers.
