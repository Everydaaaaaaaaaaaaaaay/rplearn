# 高二完整阅读四篇十五题：博物馆活动、清单服务、分类与分拣研究

## 学生题面

### A组（第1—3题）

Northgate Museum welcomes families to explore objects and the stories around them. All four programmes are free, but every family must reserve a place online before taking part. A reservation applies to the named session; it is not a general museum membership. Adults take part with their children, and no previous knowledge of the collections is needed. Read the four descriptions before choosing.

#### A1

Object Weekends
Designed for visitors of all ages, these sessions run on selected weekends throughout the year. A museum guide brings out an everyday object and asks how it might have been used. Visitors can compare materials, try a simple construction activity and talk with staff. Each weekend has a different topic; it is not a weekly course with homework.

#### A2

Small Discoverers
This programme is specifically designed for families with children aged 0–5. At the reserved session, adults and children move between low activity stations, exploring a theme through objects, picture books and play. Families may choose their order of activities after arrival. They still need the advance reservation required above; flexible movement does not mean unbooked admission.

#### A3

One Object, Many Questions
Held once each month, this programme is designed for families with children aged 3–8. A museum educator uses several items from the collection to introduce that month’s question. Families make or compare something together, and an invited maker sometimes joins the discussion. The activities take place in the museum’s learning room.

#### A4

Collections at Home
This online session is for families joining from home. It is held once each month and led by museum educators, with no visiting maker scheduled. Families use ordinary household items to explore a collection-related idea. A short materials list is sent after booking; specialist equipment is not needed. The aim is shared exploration rather than producing an identical object in every home.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

When visitor Lena started using the self-service printing desk at fictional Hillcross Library, choosing the right charge was more difficult than collecting the pages. Several prices were displayed on a screen that changed before she had finished reading. Lena, who was learning the local language, usually asked staff to repeat the information and worried that she was delaying the queue.

Library assistant Dev noticed that she returned on the same afternoon each week. He could have selected the usual settings for her without asking, but he realised that a familiar order was not necessarily the order she wanted that day. Instead, he asked whether a small written list of the choices would help. Lena said it would, provided she could keep the list beside her while using the machine.

Dev prepared a plain card showing the current page sizes and charges. He checked it with his supervisor and asked Lena to point out anything unclear. She suggested putting the size beside the price instead of listing all sizes first and all prices later. The revised card also remained available to other visitors; it was not marked with her name or treated as a test of her reading.

The following week, Lena selected a different paper size and completed the request herself. What impressed her was not that somebody had guessed her usual choice. “I could see the alternatives and decide,” she said. Dev had spent only a short time preparing the card, but he had also taken her suggestion seriously.

The library has since placed copies beside the desk. Staff still offer help, and a card cannot answer every question or remove every language difficulty. Yet Dev sees the change as part of ordinary service rather than a special favour. “Knowing a visitor’s routine should help us ask a better question,” he says, “not stop us asking one.” For Lena, the improvement was small enough to fit beside a printer and important enough to change how welcome she felt.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Two catalogues in the fictional town of Fenwick list many of the same objects but place them under different headings. The repair workshop groups a wooden handle with other tool parts. The local history room places a similar handle beside objects from an old kitchen. Neither arrangement is simply a careless version of the other: each was developed to answer a different kind of question.

The workshop’s system grew from practical requests. A worker looking for something to fit a damaged tool needed to compare size, shape and function quickly. Grouping parts by the work they could do was useful. The history room, by contrast, collected accounts of how objects had been used by particular households. Its visitors often wanted to reconstruct a setting or a period, so keeping related stories together made sense.

The difference became troublesome when the two organisations began sharing records. A volunteer searching one familiar category assumed that an object absent from it was absent from the collection. In fact, it appeared under another heading. The difficulty came from treating a category as a natural property of an object rather than as a choice made for a purpose.

The teams described this **divergence** as a movement towards different arrangements. They did not erase one catalogue and force every user into the other. Instead, they added short explanations and links between related entries. A handle could be found through its role as a part or through its place in a household account. The new links changed access without claiming that the two perspectives had become identical.

This fictional example illustrates why exchanging information requires more than copying labels. Users need to know what question a category helps answer and what it may leave out. A common access guide can make different systems easier to use while preserving useful distinctions. Difference, in this case, is neither a reason to stop cooperating nor evidence that one group thinks better than the other.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

Could a combination of people and software sort returned books more effectively than either working alone? An invented study at Orley Training Centre compared three decision conditions in a library simulation. It examined correct routing decisions under a time limit, not the safety of real transport systems or the value of every form of artificial intelligence.

Thirty adults were randomly assigned to three equal groups. All saw the same list of returned books, shelf positions and temporary closures. In the manual-only condition, participants planned routes using the printed information. In the automatic-only condition, they accepted a scheduling tool’s routes without changing them. In the combined condition, they could review the tool’s suggestions and correct them using the closure notices. Everyone received the same practice task and worked for the same period.

The combined group completed the most correct routes on average. The tool rapidly arranged ordinary items, while participants sometimes noticed a closure that its stored plan had missed. The manual group spent more time on routine ordering. The automatic group produced routes quickly but retained some routes through closed areas. Speed alone would therefore have given an incomplete picture of success.

These findings came from one comparison of three conditions, not from three separately described experiments. They suggest that, for this task and tool, human review and automated organisation could complement one another. They do not show that a combined system always wins. If every closure were already represented accurately in the tool, the balance might differ.

Researcher Bea Lin argues that future trials should vary the notices, time available and participants’ experience. Training would also matter: being allowed to override a suggestion is different from knowing when an override is justified. The centre plans to examine those questions before recommending any workplace use. Its immediate lesson is to evaluate the particular division of work, rather than to assume that adding software automatically removes the need for judgement.
