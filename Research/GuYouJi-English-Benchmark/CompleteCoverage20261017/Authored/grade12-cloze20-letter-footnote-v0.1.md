### 全组回填

I offered to translate a former resident's letter for a history exhibition. His daughter wanted it displayed years after his death. Knowing both languages, I felt confident before finishing the first paragraph.

The letter described a journey. Then came a phrase about a riverside house. The words had two possible meanings, and the surrounding sentences did not clearly support either one. I chose the meaning that made the story flow more smoothly. A hesitation on the page would only confuse the visitor, I thought.

The writer's daughter asked why my draft described the house as empty. She remembered an aunt living there around that time, though she was uncertain of the year. Her memory did not prove my version wrong, but it gave me a reason to question it.

Neither rechecking the handwriting nor consulting an older dictionary settled the issue. I was tempted to choose the other meaning simply because it now seemed more plausible. The project organiser asked whether changing my guess would make it any less of a guess.

I wrote a note stating both possibilities and the limits of the evidence. The daughter read it. She had wanted to hear her father's voice, not to have every uncertainty resolved on his behalf. She offered a photograph, but its date was unknown. We displayed it separately, making that clear rather than treating the photograph as proof.

For the exhibition, we placed the original letter beside the translation. Visitors could see which phrases were straightforward and which needed a note. I worried that the note would make my work seem incomplete. One visitor, however, said she appreciated knowing which parts she could rely on and which remained open to interpretation.

Someone suggested removing the note to tidy the display. The organiser left the decision to me, as the translator. I thought of the fluent sentence I had written at the start. It had sounded certain because I had hidden the difficult choice from view, not because I had resolved it.

The note stayed. It did not make the exhibition an incomprehensible puzzle. Most of the letter was still accessible, and the uncertain phrase was only one small part. Yet acknowledging that part changed the relationship between the reader and my work. I was no longer asking visitors to accept my fluency as a guarantee of accuracy.

The daughter thanked me after the exhibition. She did not say I had discovered her father's exact meaning, but that I had been careful with his words. That mattered more than praise for elegant English.

I still try to write translations that read naturally. But when the evidence runs out, I no longer regard a note as an admission of failure. It can be a way to be explicit about what the work can and cannot establish. The question in the letter remains unanswered. Perhaps another dated document will one day help. Until then, leaving the question visible is more responsible than giving it a confident answer I cannot defend.
