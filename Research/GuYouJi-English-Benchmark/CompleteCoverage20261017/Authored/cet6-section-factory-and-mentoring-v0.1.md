# 六级：旧厂房与职场互助两篇阅读

## 学生题面

Read both passages and choose the best answer to each question. 每篇五题，每题只选一项；全部材料与问题均需作答。

### Passage 1

The fictional city of Keldon inherits a disused printing factory when its owner transfers the site to the municipality. A consultant proposes converting the building into studios for small design businesses. Another proposal would demolish it and construct a standard office block. The council’s first financial comparison appears to favour demolition: adapting the old structure involves uncertain repair costs, whereas the new block can be priced using a familiar specification.

An architect argues that this comparison treats uncertainty unevenly. The new block’s construction cost may be relatively predictable, but its future tenants are not. A large building designed around a small number of long leases could leave the council exposed if a major tenant departed. The factory’s irregular rooms, initially described as a disadvantage, might permit smaller units to be combined or divided as demand changed. Predictability at the moment of construction was not the only form of security worth considering.

This argument does not make adaptation automatically preferable. Surveyors discover that parts of the factory roof need substantial work. Some prospective tenants require equipment that the existing floors cannot support without reinforcement. The architect accepts these findings and removes several proposed uses from her plan. She distinguishes defending the option of reuse from defending every imaginative activity that could be pictured inside an attractive old building.

The council eventually commissions a limited first stage, converting one wing while securing the rest against weather damage. Rental agreements allow tenants to move between units within the building as their businesses develop. The initial rents will not recover the entire cost of the site, so the council must state what other benefits justify its investment. Supporting small firms and retaining a familiar neighbourhood landmark are public objectives, not additional rental income disguised under another name.

Critics remain concerned that gradual development could become a way of postponing a difficult decision indefinitely. In response, the agreement specifies a date for reviewing occupancy, maintenance costs and demand for the remaining space. The council will then decide whether to proceed, revise the scheme or close it. A staged commitment is meant to preserve options, not to remove the obligation to choose among them.

For the city’s finance officer, the case changes the question posed at the start. Instead of asking which design eliminates uncertainty, she asks which uncertainties each arrangement creates, who would bear them, and what opportunities remain for adjustment. Neither a conventional new building nor an adaptable old one can supply an answer in advance. Their value depends partly on the relationship between present expenditure, future use and the institution’s capacity to respond when expectations fail.

### Passage 2

An imaginary engineering firm, Veyra, introduces an online question board to reduce interruptions to its most experienced staff. Junior engineers are asked to post technical questions, and a senior employee is assigned to answer them during a daily period. Managers expect this arrangement to protect concentrated work while giving newcomers a permanent record of useful explanations. What first appears to be a scheduling reform soon exposes differences in what employees understand by asking for help.

During the first weeks, short questions about established procedures receive quick, clear responses. More complicated problems attract fewer posts than managers expected. Interviews reveal that some newcomers cannot identify which part of a problem is appropriate to describe publicly. They worry that including too much detail will waste colleagues’ time, but omitting context may make a sensible question appear elementary. The difficulty precedes the act of typing: deciding how to frame an uncertainty is itself part of learning the job.

Experienced employees recognise this problem from informal conversations. A junior colleague standing beside a drawing may begin with an inaccurate account of what is troubling her. Through a sequence of questions, both people discover that the apparent calculation problem is actually a disagreement about what the client requested. An answer posted to the original calculation question might be mathematically correct while leaving the practical difficulty untouched. The value of the conversation lies partly in changing the question.

The firm consequently adds small weekly discussion sessions. Participants bring unfinished work, and the facilitator helps them articulate what they do not yet understand. Conclusions that are useful to others can later be written for the board. The sessions are not treated as evidence that written explanations have failed. They create a different setting for work that may be necessary before a concise explanation can be produced.

This arrangement also introduces costs. Senior staff must allocate time to discussion, and a public session may still discourage somebody from revealing a difficulty. The firm therefore keeps private appointments available and asks facilitators to avoid turning uncertainty into a display of individual incompetence. Managers cannot simply count posted answers as a measure of all the support being provided.

The episode illustrates a limit to separating knowledge transmission from the conditions in which a need for knowledge becomes recognisable. A searchable answer is valuable when a person can locate the problem it addresses. Where that ability is still developing, interaction may help constitute the task rather than merely deliver information about it. Protecting expert attention remains important, but allocating that attention requires understanding the forms of work through which others learn to ask an answerable question.
