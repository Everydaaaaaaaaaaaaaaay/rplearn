# 四级：安静展厅与周转箱两篇阅读

## 学生题面

Read both passages and choose the best answer to each question. 每篇五题，每题只选一项；全部材料与问题均需作答。

### Passage 1

A small museum in the fictional town of Denhurst reopened with a feature that surprised some visitors: a room without automatic sound effects. Elsewhere, recorded voices and music accompanied displays. In the quiet room, visitors could read about ordinary objects and choose whether to hear a recording through headphones. Silence, rather than continuous sound, was the starting point.

The change came after a family described leaving an earlier exhibition halfway through. One child had enjoyed the objects but found the sudden noises distressing. Museum staff initially considered offering a separate visit before normal opening hours. The family explained that travelling at that time would be difficult. They wanted more control over sound during an ordinary visit, not merely an invitation to arrive earlier.

The museum therefore tested individual listening devices beside a display of kitchen tools. A visitor could pick up a recording or continue reading without it. Staff supplied written versions of the recordings and made sure that essential information appeared on the display itself. Choosing silence would not mean missing the explanation that made an object understandable.

Some regular visitors feared that the exhibition would become dull. The curator replied that the museum was adding a choice, rather than forbidding lively experiences. Other rooms still included group demonstrations. The quiet room offered a different pace, and visitors could move between the two. A person seeking calm did not have to leave the museum altogether.

The arrangement also changed staff duties. Instead of simply switching on a sound system each morning, they checked the headphones, replaced damaged written sheets and helped visitors find the version they preferred. The curator regarded this work as part of the exhibition, not as an optional kindness. A choice advertised at the entrance was useful only if people could actually exercise it inside. People could make that decision without asking staff to silence an entire room.

### Passage 2

A fictional vegetable supplier, Green Basket, replaced disposable delivery boxes with reusable crates. Each crate was stronger than a cardboard box and could be folded when empty. The company expected its drivers to collect yesterday’s crates while delivering today’s orders. Managers calculated that repeated use would reduce the number of new boxes they needed to buy.

The first difficulty appeared at small restaurants. Deliveries arrived before breakfast, but cooks did not always empty the crates immediately. Drivers could not wait while vegetables were moved into storage. A crate that was theoretically reusable might therefore remain in a kitchen for several days. Meanwhile, the supplier needed another one for the next delivery.

Customers suggested leaving their empty crates outside overnight. That helped collections, but some restaurants had no secure outdoor space. Others shared an entrance with flats, whose residents objected to a growing stack beside the door. There was no single collection arrangement that suited every customer, even though the containers themselves were identical.

Green Basket responded by discussing return times when restaurants placed orders. Larger kitchens agreed to weekly collections, while some smaller ones chose a brief afternoon visit. The company also offered an ordinary cardboard box where a return system was impractical. It did not describe customers choosing that option as careless: their buildings and working hours could make a difference.

A later review counted crates still at customers’ premises as well as those ready for another journey. The change made the apparent saving less impressive than the original forecast. Yet it also showed managers where more containers or better collection routes were needed. They kept the scheme, but stopped treating the durability of a crate as sufficient evidence that it would circulate efficiently. The object could be reused; the work needed to return it still had to be organised.
