### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

A marsh committee is arguing about how many reeds to cut. Both sides reject a plan that would destroy the bed in a single season. Their disagreement remains even after they agree to protect the marsh from pollution. For the habitat group, the aim is to leave the living community as undisturbed as possible. It proposes almost no cutting. For the craft group, reeds are also a source of material for local basket makers. Its aim is the largest continuing harvest that the bed can replace. It does not seek the largest possible harvest this year regardless of later losses.

The graph presents a fictional model, not measurements from an actual marsh. Moving right means stronger cutting pressure; moving up means a larger steady harvest. The single curve represents harvest alone. The four letters mark points on that curve, and no curve for the remaining reed stock is included. A point's height therefore cannot be read as the amount of reeds still standing. Use the diagram together with the explanation when considering the craft group's stated objective.

![A model reed bed. Horizontal axis: Cutting pressure, low to high. Vertical axis: Steady reed harvest. A single smooth curve starts at zero at B, rises through A, reaches its only peak at C, and falls to zero at D. No stock curve or numerical measurements are shown.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade10-reading15-graph-reeds-curve-v0.1.svg)

In this model, new growth depends on the size of the remaining bed. A very small bed has too few growing stems to replace much material. A very crowded bed also adds material slowly because light and space are limited. A medium-sized bed adds the most new material. If cutting removes only that addition, the bed can continue at a stable size. This account explains the curve within the model; it does not supply a universal rule for every wetland.

Choosing a peak on a diagram would not settle the committee's decision. Nesting birds, water conditions and the needs of people who use the marsh would require separate evidence. Nor does a high harvest show that the habitat group has achieved its different aim. The graph compares one kind of outcome, not the worth of every possible purpose.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

In her earlier novel, Rope Across the Water, Iona Vale wrote about a ferry crew whose private disagreements were hidden beneath a reliable timetable. Her new book, Messages at the Landing, turns from imagined workers to her own years at a small crossing. The difference matters: this is a memoir about learning to ask for access, not another adventure set on a boat. The reviewer is judging how Iona tells that story, rather than claiming to have lived it.

Iona describes gradually finding spoken announcements difficult to follow. At first she laughed whenever the others laughed, even when she had missed the joke. She calls this **keeping up appearances**: she wanted to seem to understand, so she concealed the difficulty instead of requesting a written message. Later, she began asking colleagues to write route changes and found people willing to help. The change in the book is her willingness to state what she needs, not a claim that her hearing returned.

The early chapters connect her family memories with old landing notices and accounts of local workers. Readers expecting an uninterrupted personal story may find these pages slow. Yet the documents do a particular job. They show why a spoken announcement seemed normal to the crew and why Iona initially felt awkward asking for something different. The research supports the personal narrative instead of replacing it with a general history of transport.

A later chapter follows her attempt to prepare an accessible exhibition about the crossing. She first tries to write every panel alone, then invites former passengers to comment. Their corrections expose details she had overlooked. The scene prevents the memoir from becoming a simple story of one unusually capable individual solving everything.

This thoughtful book is strongest when a small exchange reveals a large change in confidence. Some opening pages could be shorter, but the closing exhibition gives the earlier records a purpose. Readers leave with a particular person's account of making needs visible, not instructions for treating hearing conditions.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

The imaginary Harbor Lab is testing a sunlight-powered plate for separating water from a prepared salty solution. Its report begins with a practical difficulty: making water evaporate is not enough. A useful device must also collect the vapour and keep solids from blocking the active surface. The team uses a defined laboratory mixture. It makes no claim that an unfamiliar liquid can become safe drinking water simply by passing through the plate.

Only the dark central strip receives a surface treatment. Fine channels in that strip draw liquid from a shallow tray and spread it into a thin layer. Sunlight warms this layer, and some water becomes vapour. A transparent cover guides the vapour toward a cooler collection edge, where it returns to liquid. The outer margin remains untreated and serves as a receiving area for the concentrated solution. It is not an additional heated working strip.

Without an escape route, concentrated material can dry into a crust that interrupts the supply of liquid. Narrow drainage cuts lead the remaining solution out of the active strip before a thick deposit forms. In the reported trial, the team compares an open-cut plate with an otherwise similar sealed-cut plate. Water movement lasts longer in the first. This supports the proposed drainage role under the trial conditions; it does not show that every possible mixture would behave in the same way.

The team also puts a replaceable collecting pad at the margin. A coating on this pad captures a marker mineral from the leftover liquid. Recovering that mineral for reuse is a second purpose, distinct from collecting water. When the report describes material on the pad, it is demonstrating this extra application, not reporting that the fresh-water container has filled with mineral.

Before any outdoor use, the researchers would need to examine cleaning, changing sunlight and the quality of the collected water. The report offers a mechanism worth investigating, not a completed public water service. The pad's successful performance with the prepared mixture cannot remove those remaining questions.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A fictional team wants to study how children follow spoken instructions during family activities. To simplify an early experiment, it first plays recorded sentences in a quiet room. That makes it easier to compare what listeners can hear, but it removes much of the shared activity surrounding ordinary conversation. The team then asks what this narrow setting may have left out.

For a follow-up, it visits two voluntarily participating family clubs. At the Riverside club, members value a shared cooking hour and several people often speak around one table. At the Orchard club, families arrange short conversations in separate corners before gathering for a meal. These are descriptions of those clubs' chosen routines. The families are not assigned at random, and the report does not claim that their routines represent an ethnic group or all families in a region.

The observers find more overlapping speech at Riverside. They also record more invitations for children to ask about ingredients. At Orchard, individual instructions are easier to separate from other voices, but there are fewer of these invitations during the observed period. The two measures answer different questions. A count of invitations is a measure of opportunities to join in; it is not a test of how many words a child actually heard or understood.

This distinction matters for a child who finds speech in noise difficult to access. Having many people nearby may offer chances to communicate, while overlapping voices can still obscure a particular sentence. Neither fact cancels the other. The team therefore avoids ranking one club as simply better for language development. It has not measured long-term learning, and the observations alone cannot establish which routine caused any individual result.

For a later pilot, the team suggests keeping the shared cooking activity while offering written instructions and a quieter place for checking a message. Families would help choose the arrangement. Whether it improves access must be tested; the proposal is not a medical recommendation. It aims to work with an existing social routine while addressing a specific barrier, rather than requiring every family to adopt the same pattern.
