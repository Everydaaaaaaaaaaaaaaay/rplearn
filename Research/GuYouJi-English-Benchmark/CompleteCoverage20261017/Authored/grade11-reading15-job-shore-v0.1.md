## 学生题面

### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Bramble Shore Partnership — Shore Crew Leader

The fictional Bramble Shore Partnership invites applications for a paid youth fieldwork placement. The crew combines practical conservation with learning to record changes along a shore. Leaders work alongside site staff rather than directing the organisation’s national activities.

#### Key duties

Each leader guides 10–15 high-school students in teamwork, simple path maintenance and careful observation. The leader also keeps attendance records, checks equipment and reports concerns to site staff. A placement includes administrative work as well as practical sessions.

#### Schedule

The placement has two phases: spring (March–May 2027), Tuesday to Saturday; summer (June–August 2027), Monday to Friday. Leaders work 40 hours per week in either phase. The change of season changes the regular working days, not the weekly hours.

#### Location

Bramble Shore Reserve, beside the fictional town of Easthaven.

#### Qualifications

Applicants must be at least 21 on the first working day and hold a current driving licence held for at least 3 years; exactly three years satisfies that minimum. Applicants must be able to lift 40 pounds and work outdoors. Current CPR and outdoor first-aid certificates are required before field duties; training to obtain them is available before the placement. Previous conservation work and project leadership are preferred, not compulsory.

#### Compensation

$750 per week, paid every 2 weeks. The payment for two ordinary weeks is $1500 before deductions. The placement includes staff training; this is a fictional notice, not an actual offer of employment.

#### Work challenges

Sessions take place outdoors, where wind, wet ground and insects can change the working conditions. Leaders sometimes carry a field pack for several miles and must adjust a planned activity when site staff identify a problem. The post requires positive cooperation with people of different ages; it is not a desk-only appointment.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

A clock on a community-hall wall used to stop at the same minute every afternoon. At the fictional Bramble Repair Circle, an older volunteer brought it to a table where a teenager was learning to record faults. They did not immediately replace the clock. First they compared what happened when it was standing upright and when it was tilted. The small task gave both people a reason to ask questions.

The circle began after a neighbourhood newsletter invited residents to offer repair skills for one afternoon a month. It has since added sewing, simple tool maintenance and a place to exchange notes about objects that need specialist attention. Repairs are free; voluntary donations pay for materials. The organisers would rather decline a repair they cannot complete responsibly than treat every object as proof of their success.

Volunteer Eli joined after moving into a nearby flat. “I knew the streets, but I had hardly spoken to anyone,” he says. Working beside other residents gave him a regular reason to return and helped him feel part of the neighbourhood. He describes a change in his own experience. The circle has not compared his wellbeing with a control group, and his comment is not presented as a measured health effect.

The organisers are **attuned** to the different ways people take part. When they notice that a noisy demonstration makes questions hard to hear, they move the discussion to a quieter table. A visitor who cannot stay for a whole session can leave a dated note with the object instead. These adjustments do not create a faster repair for every visitor, but they help people enter a conversation that might otherwise pass them by.

The clock now works, yet the organisers keep a photograph of the shared inspection rather than a count of minutes saved. The circle's value also lies in the relationships formed around an uncertain task. It gives people of different generations an opportunity to learn each other's methods, while accepting that some objects must still go elsewhere for repair.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Dividing household work into equal shares sounds straightforward until partners ask what a share contains. Does taking full responsibility for the washing balance taking full responsibility for the cooking? Or does fairness feel different when both partners take part in each task? A fictional survey report examines that distinction without assuming that equal total hours describe every feature of a division of work.

In one comparison, 100 couples reported sharing responsibility for each regular chore, while another 100 reported assigning different chores to different partners. The partners in both groups estimated roughly equal individual shares of their household's total work. In the shared-responsibility group, 60 couples described their arrangement as fair; in the separate-task group, 30 did so. Those proportions show an association in the surveyed groups, not the result of assigning couples to different arrangements experimentally.

One possible explanation is that shared responsibility encourages partners to discuss details that a simple list of assigned jobs leaves invisible. Another runs in the opposite direction: couples who already communicate well may be more likely to choose shared responsibility. The report cannot decide between those directions from this comparison. It also does not claim that one arrangement suits every household or that equal hours are irrelevant.

A “grass is greener” effect offers another possibility. If you never prepare the food before cooking, washing a few plates may seem like the only demanding part of the evening. An unfamiliar job can look easier because you see its result but miss its preparation. As an outside researcher in this fictional report puts it, “A task can look lighter when you never have to prepare for it.” Actually doing the work may reveal effort that an observer had underestimated.

The report therefore combines a difference in reported fairness with several possible interpretations. It invites closer investigation of how partners understand one another's work. It does not convert the observed association into a proven recipe for a happier relationship.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A fictional engineering team has built a six-fingered robotic hand that can work both while attached to an arm and while moving independently across a table. The project explores whether changing the familiar shape of a hand can expand what a small machine does. Its demonstrations use lightweight objects in a controlled setting, rather than a finished product working in people's homes.

“We wanted to question the shape of a hand, not just copy its outline,” says the project leader. The fingers can bend backwards as well as forwards, allowing contact on either side of the palm. This feature makes the design different from a straightforward imitation of a human hand. It does not mean that the machine understands every object it touches or can carry an unlimited load.

To compare designs, the team used a search algorithm that combined features, simulated a task and retained better-performing combinations for another round. The process generated several candidates for physical testing. A good simulated result did not remove the need to test joints, balance and grip on the actual prototype. The algorithm helped explore design possibilities; it was not evidence that the machine learned every new job without guidance.

When attached to the arm, the hand could hold a light container against one side of its palm while securing a second object against the other. After detaching, it moved most steadily on four supporting fingers while using the remaining two to carry an object. In another arrangement, five fingers supported it and one carried a lighter item. The two arrangements used the same six fingers, with different roles, rather than extra fingers appearing during a task.

A later version might inspect narrow equipment spaces that a larger robot cannot enter. The team is also interested in whether such a design could eventually become a human-operated assistive hand. That possibility would require research into how a person could control and interpret movements unlike those of an ordinary hand. The report treats it as a question for further work, while presenting the current design as a useful challenge to familiar assumptions.
