### A组（第1—3题）

Four fictional caves feature in a museum’s coast-and-lake exhibition. Their displays draw on different local features. The information describes what the programmes show rather than providing routes for entering the caves.

#### A1

Fernmouth Cave
The district’s only recorded nesting site for the imagined pale-collared martin lies on ledges inside this sea chamber. Visitors follow the birds through a camera feed in a shore building. The chamber is shorter than several neighbouring caves, and its entrance has no unusual coloured bands. What distinguishes it in the exhibition is the nesting community. The display compares images taken during the breeding season with those from quieter months.

#### A2

Ice Lantern
A winter display shows light travelling through ridges of clear shore ice outside this lakeside opening. At certain times of day, the ridges scatter small patches of colour across the rock. Images are relayed to an indoor gallery, so the programme does not require visitors to go onto the ice. The summer display explores the shape of the opening, but it lacks the coloured winter light effect for which this cave is best known.

#### A3

Round Pool
This flooded chamber is visible as a circular patch of darker water near the coast. A camera lowered from the exhibition’s viewing boat lets visitors see ledges beneath the surface. The boat remains the viewing platform throughout the programme. An old suggestion that the circle was perfectly regular has been revised after further surveys; its outline is now described as uneven. The exhibition uses that revision to show how measurements can change a description.

#### A4

Striped Vault
Several bands of contrasting rock rise above the water inside a broad coastal arch. Visitors watch from a tour boat outside the opening while a guide uses photographs to trace the stripes along the walls. The bands are directly visible above the surface, unlike the submerged ledges at Round Pool. The programme uses no underwater camera and makes no claim to show nesting birds. Its subject is the patterned rock and the surveys that recorded it.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

In 1988, twelve students working backstage at a college theatre learned one another’s habits before they knew much about one another’s lives. A late rehearsal meant finding missing props together, carrying scenery through a narrow doorway and staying until the last light was switched off. The work brought them into conversation. By the end of the first year, the group called itself the Side Door Company, after the entrance through which they always arrived.

Today the twelve have different jobs and live in different regions, yet a yearly gathering remains in their calendars. The programme is rarely dramatic: a shared meal, a walk, several hours of unfinished stories. “I never have to carry a heavy week alone,” says Priya. She means that people who have known her for decades will make time to listen or help, not that her old theatre colleagues now perform a paid lifting service.

The friends have had to develop ways of staying in contact that fit changing circumstances. They plan travel early, rotate hosts and check before adding a demanding activity. When a misunderstanding appears in a message, they try to resolve it in conversation instead of assuming the worst. They acknowledge that distance and conflicting responsibilities can make these efforts awkward. Still, they value the security of being known by people who remember earlier versions of their lives without demanding a return to them.

That flexibility mattered when Tomas began training for a new career and could not afford the usual reunion journey. Two friends suggested a shorter gathering in his town, where others could stay cheaply. Nobody described the change as a favour that he would have to repay. Tomas later said that the altered plan allowed him to belong without pretending that his circumstances matched everyone else’s. Friendship, for him, was visible in the arrangement rather than in a grand declaration.

Their children now organise a small display of theatre photographs whenever the group meets. Sometimes a child recognises a parent only after hearing the story behind a costume. The archive has become a way of sharing relationships as well as images. The Side Door Company no longer needs a college play to bring it together. Its members continue to make a place for one another, and a younger generation has begun to understand why they do.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

For residents of the fictional city of Selwyn, the Lume River is both a record of disruption and a source of connection. It flows from east to west through the historic centre. Stones beside the old market carry flood dates, reminders of occasions when the town had to rebuild. Yet the same banks supported trade, neighbourhood gatherings and the movement of goods. The river has helped organise the city’s common life even when that relationship was difficult.

The address system offers a small but persistent example. In streets parallel to the Lume, building numbers increase downstream, towards the west. In streets perpendicular to it, numbering starts at the bank and rises as the street leads away from the river. The first rule follows the current; the second uses distance from the water. Someone who confuses the two may walk in the wrong direction even while correctly remembering a building number.

Older accounts describe a crowded riverside rather than a quiet scenic edge. Boat crews unloaded goods, traders argued over space and neighbours gathered after work. The river was not simply something to look at from a distance. Activities linked to work and relaxation occupied the same stretch of bank, creating repeated opportunities for people from different parts of the city to meet.

In 1965, a road was built along the northern bank and old delivery yards were turned into car parks. The scheme improved access for vehicles but narrowed the places where a pedestrian could reach the water. It was an investment in a riverside road and parking, not in a bus terminal or a railway station. The physical layout gradually made the river seem farther from everyday life, though its course had not moved.

Beginning in 2018, the city converted sections of that road into a pedestrian route and then a riverside park. Some paved surfaces remained for public use, while planted margins were allowed to develop beside them. A planner described the ideal as a bank where residents could spend time without squeezing out the river’s other living systems. The aim was not an empty nature reserve or an uninterrupted line of leisure equipment, but renewed contact between urban life and a natural passage through the city.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

In a fictional design studio, engineers are considering a car display that would warn of possible sharp changes in weather farther along a route. One imagined use is to highlight a stretch where heavy rain may soon affect travel. The system would interpret information about conditions; it would not control rainfall, remove floodwater or guarantee that continuing a journey was safe.

The attraction, according to the studio’s account, is the possibility of adding local detail to a regional forecast. Occupants might see where an estimate was uncertain instead of receiving one symbol for an entire district. A warning would be useful only if the display could communicate both the possible event and the limits of the evidence behind it. More confident language would not, by itself, make the prediction better.

The account describes ideas that still need evaluation. It contains no completed road experiment establishing accuracy, and no evidence that a particular number of accidents has already been avoided. Weather differs between seasons and landscapes, so a few successful demonstrations would not justify a universal claim. The designers envisage repeated testing rather than a single result that settles the system’s value.

Pressure, temperature and humidity sensors would supply measurements. Software would then combine those readings with earlier observations and use a model to estimate how conditions might develop at points ahead. Incoming measurements would allow the estimate to change. This separates the task of gathering evidence from the task of drawing a forecast from it: a stream of numbers does not interpret itself, however quickly it arrives.

The proposed network would connect vehicles to other vehicles and to fixed stations. Each reading would need its place and time attached, so the model could distinguish current nearby evidence from older information elsewhere. The account describes checking those labels as **imperative**, because unrecognised stale data could distort an estimate. A larger network could therefore help only if the added observations were assessed rather than merely collected.

Should dependable testing methods and data checks develop, the writer foresees route-specific forecasts becoming an ordinary part of vehicle displays in a future decade. This remains a conditional picture, not a certified date of arrival or a promise of perfect prediction. The prospect is a change in how travellers receive weather information; whether that prospect can be realised depends on work the proposal has not yet completed.
