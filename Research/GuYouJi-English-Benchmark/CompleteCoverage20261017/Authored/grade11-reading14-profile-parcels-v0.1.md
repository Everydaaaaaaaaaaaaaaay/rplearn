### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Volunteer internationally with Bridge Exchange

Would you like to contribute your time and professional skills to projects in another community? Bridge Exchange connects volunteers with local organisations working on local libraries and practical skills projects. A placement offers a way to make a useful contribution while learning from the people who live there. The following conditions apply to this fictional programme.

#### Why volunteer with Bridge Exchange

- Volunteers support locally chosen projects for peace and development, working with community partners rather than replacing their decisions.
- Teams include people from many countries and cultural backgrounds, bringing a range of viewpoints and approaches to shared tasks.
- A placement may have effects beyond its immediate activities when local partners continue using and sharing what they have developed together.
- Volunteers can learn about other cultures, build professional networks, study languages and gain professional and personal experience.

#### Requirements

- Applicants must be 25 or older, with no upper age limit, and hold a university degree or a higher technical diploma.
- Applicants need at least two years of relevant professional work and a good working knowledge of English, Spanish or French.
- Applicants must respect the principles of volunteering, work effectively in a multicultural team and be willing to cooperate with local people and organisations.
- Applicants need to adjust to difficult living conditions and sometimes remote locations, with strong interpersonal and organisational skills.

#### Conditions of Service

- Normal placements last six to twelve months; an extension of one to two years may be possible by agreement.
- Partners occasionally request shorter placements. Volunteers are entitled to specified allowances during their service.
- An allowance is not a salary, reward or payment in exchange for the work a volunteer completes.
- The allowances are intended to help volunteers maintain a secure standard of living at their duty station.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

When Amir talks about becoming a reader, he does not begin with a school prize. He remembers his older sister sitting on the stairs with a science book, reading a page aloud and asking what he thought the next picture would show. She had little money for new books, so they visited a second-hand shop together. He came to enjoy the search almost as much as the story.

![Illustration of Amir beside donated books, not a photograph.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-parcels-B-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*Amir with books for local children*

As an adult, Amir helped organise afternoon activities at schools beyond the town centre. Some children wanted to borrow a story but had to leave before the shared cupboard opened. Others lived too far from a public library to visit often. He started a book-box project to make books easier to reach and to give children a chance to discover that reading could be chosen for pleasure, not only assigned as homework.

A weekend collection brought in 780 usable books. That result encouraged him, but the schools on his list needed a steady supply. Amir announced a goal of 150,000 books over several years. It was deliberately larger than his own shelves could hold. He arranged a sorting space at a village hall and invited volunteers to help, accepting that a useful long-term project would need routines as well as enthusiasm.

At first, friends gave books or money to help buy suitable titles. After a local newsletter described the scheme, unfamiliar residents began bringing sealed boxes to the hall’s collection desk. They did not have to know Amir or join his team to donate. The boxes were checked and then sent free to participating schools. A reading club at a residential centre also received books; no books were taken from its existing collection.

Four years later, the project had distributed 31,200 books. Amir was still far from his goal and said so plainly at each meeting. Nevertheless, he continued arranging deliveries, asking which reading levels were short of books and helping volunteers repair loose covers. He regarded the large target as a direction to work towards, rather than a reason to dismiss the smaller number of children who could now take a book home.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

The meadow birds in this fictional field report did not keep the same distance from one another throughout the day. A few familiar birds stayed close as they moved, several such groups sometimes joined, and then the larger gathering separated again. The birds could compete for a place near food yet later share a resting area. Observing these recurring social patterns led a field team to ask whether it was seeing groups within groups.

![Illustration of one fictional meadow bird, not a photograph or a social-structure diagram.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade11-reading14-profile-parcels-C-illustration-v0.1.svg)

*meadow birds*

A large crowd at a good feeding site need not have a lasting social structure. To call a society multilevel, the researchers needed evidence that smaller groups remained recognisable while forming wider associations. This possibility was interesting because the birds had small brains compared with many species already studied. The project could test a claim about necessary brain size without assuming that size directly explained every social decision.

Over a year the team watched 476 individually banded birds each day. A subgroup of 64 of those birds carried GPS devices, allowing movements of that subgroup to be followed between observation points. The devices did not turn the remaining 412 birds into continuously tracked individuals. Leg bands and location records supplied different kinds of information, which the team combined when describing associations.

The results indicated stable small groups, linked sets of those groups and especially frequent partnerships inside some small groups. The pattern persisted across changes of location, so it was more than a single crowd around food. A multilevel society was possible in this small-brained population. The researchers did not conclude that the birds had the same social lives as humans or that every kind of bird would show the pattern.

One interpretation concerned the amount an individual needed to recognise. Knowing a few familiar members might identify a whole group, reducing the need to recognise everyone separately. Joining a larger set could also be useful under a particular threat. Neither explanation was established just by plotting movements on a map. The report distinguished the observed organisation from questions about the mental processes that might support it.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

A notice at a fictional adult learning centre asks whether missing breakfast is really an efficient way to save time. A survey mentioned in the centre’s newsletter offers some relevant information, but not a simple answer for every reader. It explores reported meal patterns and difficulties concentrating later in the day. It is not a clinical study of disease or a test of a dietary treatment.

The survey’s aim was to examine associations between the number and timing of meals and later reports of difficulty concentrating. Its participants already followed different routines. The researchers did not instruct anyone to change meals, and the newsletter reminds readers that comparing existing groups is different from randomly assigning a meal schedule.

At the start, 2,140 adults aged forty-five or older recalled the food and meals of the previous twenty-four hours in a single interview. Over eighteen months they answered separate questions about occasions when afternoon concentration was difficult. Researchers then compared those later answers with the meal information collected at entry. There was no daily dietary interview across those eighteen months.

The pattern reported in the newsletter was that people recalling only one meal, a missed breakfast or very short gaps between meals also more often reported difficulty concentrating later. These were associations in self-reported data. The survey could not show whether changing meal timing would change concentration for any particular participant, or whether another difference accounted for part of the pattern.

Asked to comment, a nutrition educator said that the key question was whether the person’s overall eating pattern supplied the nutrients needed. “Regularly **eliminating** a meal can leave a gap if the food it would have provided is never replaced,” he said. “Giving breakfast a special name does not make the rest of the day’s intake irrelevant.” His comments explained a practical consideration; the survey had not experimentally tested his account.

The data had important limits. The one recalled day might have been unusual, and a participant could forget a snack or describe a meal differently from another person. Working hours and sleep could affect both meal routines and concentration. A twenty-four-hour window describes what a person was asked to remember, not how often the researchers repeated the interview.

The newsletter’s writer is therefore reluctant to present habitual breakfast omission as a sensible shortcut when people have not considered how to meet their food needs. That stance should not be confused with certainty that breakfast controls concentration. The writer discusses both a concern about routine omission and the survey’s limited ability to explain the association. No reader is given a personal diagnosis or meal prescription.
