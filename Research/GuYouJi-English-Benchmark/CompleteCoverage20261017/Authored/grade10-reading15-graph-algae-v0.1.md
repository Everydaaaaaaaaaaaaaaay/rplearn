### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

A group of designers imagines a managed pool that grows algae for making model-building sheets. It uses the pool to discuss a familiar problem: two plans can be called sustainable while serving different purposes. One group wants the pool to develop with almost no human removal. It values the living community being left alone. The other wants the greatest repeated supply of algae that the pool can replace. Both oppose emptying the pool once and losing its future growth. Their shared opposition to destruction does not make their positive goals identical.

The diagram is an invented model of collection, not an observation of an actual species. Removal pressure increases along the horizontal axis. Sustained collection increases along the vertical axis. Four letters identify points on a single curve. There is no second line for algae remaining in the water. The highest point thus represents a collection outcome; it cannot be treated as the largest remaining living stock. The supply group asks which labelled point best serves its stated objective within these assumptions.

![A fictional managed algae pool. Horizontal axis: Removal pressure, low to high. Vertical axis: Sustained algae collection. A single smooth curve starts at zero at C, rises through B, reaches its only peak at D, and falls to zero at A. No stock curve or numerical measurements are shown.](build/english-benchmark-authority-20261017/grade10-reading15-graph-algae-curve-v0.1.svg)

In the model, a nearly empty pool has little living material available to grow. A very crowded pool also produces new material slowly because growth is limited by shared resources. An intermediate amount produces the greatest addition. Removing only that addition allows the same amount of living material to remain. The supply group bases its preferred steady collection on this replacement process, not on the possibility of scooping out everything that is present at one moment.

The designers emphasise that the diagram is a tool for comparing ideas, not permission to manage a real lake this way. Real conditions would require evidence about organisms, water movement and other users. The group favouring minimal disturbance might still disagree with choosing the curve's highest point. That is not necessarily a failure to understand the picture: the group could understand it while putting a different outcome first. A model can make that choice clearer without making the choice for everyone.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

The Listening Labels is Selma Perrin's account of working in a local museum after she began relying more on written communication. Although the cover shows a large exhibition room, the book is not a catalogue of objects. It is a memoir about how an experienced guide learned to recognise barriers she had once failed to notice. This review considers both the achievement of that personal narrative and the places where its documentary detail slows the pace.

Early in the book, Selma describes returning to staff meetings after a long break. She could read the agenda but struggled when several voices overlapped. With written decisions and a clear chance to ask questions, she gradually **found her footing**. She began making proposals again and explaining what helped her work. The phrase marks gaining confidence in a changed situation; it does not describe learning to walk through the gallery or a medical improvement in hearing.

Selma's earlier fiction had followed a collector searching for a lost instrument. Here she examines her own letters and the museum's meeting notes instead. Some early pages give more background than a reader seeking an immediate story may want. Yet the notes show that important decisions had often been made in quick spoken exchanges outside formal meetings. They help explain why simply printing an agenda did not give her access to every decision.

The most engaging later chapter concerns a display that pairs objects with accounts from visitors. Selma assumes at first that writing longer labels will solve the communication problem. A visitor points out that length and clarity are different things. Selma listens, shortens some passages and makes space for questions. The episode gives others a role in shaping the project instead of presenting the narrator as a solitary expert.

For readers interested in how a workplace can become more open to participation, the memoir offers a vivid individual perspective. Its records are valuable when they reveal an assumption, less so when they merely list dates. The book earns its hopeful ending through small revisions to shared work, while leaving broad claims about medicine and universal access outside its scope.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

A fictional student research team describes a two-part plate for recovering water from a prepared laboratory salt solution. The report separates a useful laboratory result from a finished real-world service. Its objective is to keep liquid moving through a heated working area while leading the increasingly concentrated remainder somewhere else. Merely finding a dark material that becomes hot would not answer the whole design problem.

The team treats a strip of metal so that fine surface paths draw solution from a lower tray. The paths spread a shallow film across the dark strip, which absorbs sunlight. Some of the water becomes vapour. A clear hood sends it toward a cooler wall, where drops form and run into a water collector. Beside the strip is an untreated receiving shelf. Concentrated liquid moves onto that shelf; it does not flow back into the separate collected-water container.

The designers leave short open exits along the edge of the working strip. These allow thickening solution to leave before solid deposits block the supply paths. To investigate the exits, they compare two otherwise similar plates under the same light with the same prepared liquid. One has open exits; the other has them sealed. In that comparison, the open-exit plate continues moving liquid longer. The team presents this as evidence about drainage in the tested arrangement, not a claim that deposits can never appear.

On the receiving shelf sits a replaceable mineral-catching strip. Its coating retains a chosen mineral from the concentrated remainder. The team can later remove the strip and collect that material for reuse. This provides a second application of the arrangement beyond recovering water. It is important that readers keep the two destinations separate: mineral on the shelf and water in the collector are different outputs of different stages.

The experiment leaves practical questions unanswered. Would the exits remain open after repeated cleaning? How would changing daylight affect the flow? Would the recovered water meet a particular safety requirement? The fictional team has not yet tested these issues. Its report explains how the prototype is intended to work and where the next tests belong, without turning a mechanism demonstration into a guarantee for homes or communities.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

When a fictional research team studies children's understanding of spoken museum directions, it initially uses a controlled recording in an empty room. The choice makes the sound material easy to repeat. It also leaves out something important about an ordinary visit: people share interests, interrupt each other and invite children to contribute. The team adds a small observation study to consider these social features alongside access to speech.

Two voluntary weekend groups use different routines. The Makers group gathers around one exhibit and values a continuous shared discussion. The Explorers group usually lets pairs inspect objects quietly before coming together. These are local practices chosen by participants, not characteristics assigned to cultural or ethnic populations. Since people selected their group, the study cannot assume that the routine alone explains any difference the observers record.

At the shared exhibit, the team records more simultaneous voices and more invitations for children to suggest explanations. In the paired visits, single directions are easier to distinguish, while there are fewer public invitations in the same period. Counting invitations describes the social openings offered to a child. Checking whether a direction can be heard describes access to its words. The researchers resist adding the two counts together as if they measured exactly the same thing.

For children who have difficulty following speech amid other voices, a lively exchange can therefore contain both an opportunity and a barrier. Many invitations do not ensure that the words arrive clearly. Equally, a perfectly audible direction does not necessarily make space for the child to ask a question. The team has measured neither later vocabulary growth nor a lasting change in participation, so it does not announce that one routine is the best way to develop language.

Its next proposal is a pilot with the Makers group. The shared visit would remain, but the guide would display key directions and leave agreed pauses for questions. The participants would help decide when those pauses fit their activity. The team would then test whether access actually improved. The proposal respects the group's reason for gathering while addressing a particular obstacle. It offers no diagnosis or established treatment and makes no claim to describe all museum visitors.
