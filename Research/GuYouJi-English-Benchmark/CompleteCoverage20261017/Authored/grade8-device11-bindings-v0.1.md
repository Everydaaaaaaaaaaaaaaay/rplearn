### B组（第4—7题）

#### The Room Beside the Robot Club

At my new school, a long line of pupils gathered beside the robot-club form. I could also see notices for drama, gardening and book binding (装订). I had enjoyed repairing book covers at my old school, so the last group should have been my natural choice. Instead, I joined the line beside the most popular notice.

I remembered earning a badge (徽章) for helping with a book-care display. I liked finding a practical way to make a damaged cover useful again. But the pupils around me were talking excitedly about robots, and I wanted to fit in (融入). I signed up for their club without asking myself whether I wanted to do the same work. The neat row of names seemed more important than the activity.

During the robot meeting, the others compared programs and talked about moving parts. I had little experience with either. I felt lost and could not think of a question I wanted to discuss. The club itself was not at fault; I had chosen it for the people’s approval rather than for the work I would be doing. That difference became clear before the meeting ended.

I asked to take a short break. As I walked along the corridor, I heard the book-care group discussing how to plan a cover display. The familiar topic felt as welcoming as hearing my name called by an old friend. I stopped outside, listened for a moment and decided to ask for another chance. Then I stepped into the room and faced the leader.

‘May I still join the book-care group?’ I asked Ms Reed. The regular places were taken, but she offered a reserve position. I could prepare both the cover-label and visitor-explanation roles, ready to replace a member who could not attend. I accepted. Preparing two jobs would take effort, but it was effort I was willing to make. I could imagine enjoying the work even without being the centre of attention.

The robot leader let me switch (调换) groups. Nobody in book care was absent from a display that season, so I never demonstrated as a replacement. Even so, I was pleased with the choice. I had stopped trying to borrow someone else’s interest and begun doing work I valued. Being in the fashionable room had seemed important; finding the right work mattered more.

### C组（第8—11题）

In a fictional school network, Tess opens a chat tool to plan a reading game, organise a club message and discuss a disappointing day. She calls it a digital companion (伙伴), while still talking and spending time with classmates. Her experience introduces a wider topic: pupils may use a tool not only to obtain a homework answer, but also to find a response when they want company.

A made-up survey questioned 1320 pupils. Using that same total, 990 reported having tried a chat companion, and 440 said they used one for social interactions (互动). In other questions, 660 doubted the advice and 440 had taken a serious matter to a tool instead of a person. The categories measure different things and may overlap. A doubtful user could still be included among those who talked to a tool about something serious.

The coordinator said chat tools were rapidly (迅速地) becoming **woven into** everyday routines. Their place in pupils’ lives was no longer limited to occasional homework questions. These self-reports do not demonstrate that a tool caused any change in a pupil’s social abilities. Nor do they show that every pupil who tried one had decided to choose it over people.

The report describes several possible attractions. Replies can arrive when a friend is busy, a topic can be revisited and a user can organise a thought without worrying about interrupting someone. Such exchanges are increasingly (越来越) familiar in the fictional network. Some pupils find that convenient. It still leaves a separate question: whether a particular reply is accurate and useful. Availability is not the same as a person’s understanding of a situation.

The coordinator was cautious about relying (依赖) on a digital reply for every disagreement. If a pupil repeatedly avoids talking through difficulties with people, there may be fewer opportunities to practise explaining, listening and reaching an understanding. Some replies may also sound certain while being mistaken. Those possibilities deserve attention, but the survey did not prove that each companion user had already become less able to maintain friendships.

Useful features and possible risks both belong in the discussion. The report suggests examining a tool’s limits while protecting time for real conversation. It does not claim that every use is helpful or that every use must be rejected. A convenient reply can have a role, but it cannot be treated as a guaranteed solution to another person’s feelings or to every difficulty between friends.
