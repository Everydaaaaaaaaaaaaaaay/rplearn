### 全组回填

For weeks, our relay team had been trying to improve its changeovers. I recorded lap times, trusting the numbers more than anyone's impressions. If a swimmer felt faster but the clock disagreed, I believed the clock.

One Tuesday, every recorded time was slower. I assumed that the team had lost concentration. I urged everyone to work harder. Our youngest swimmer, Jo, asked whether the timing method had stayed the same. I thought she was trying to escape responsibility. The pool and distance were unchanged.

Jo pointed to a detail I had overlooked. Last week, the coach had started my stopwatch from the far end. This week, I had started it after hearing her whistle while standing near the entrance. I dismissed the difference as too minor to matter. Jo did not insist that this explained all the slower times. She simply asked us to test it before blaming the swimmers.

With the coach supervising, we ran a short comparison. Two volunteers timed the same swim while standing beside each other. Then one moved to my earlier position, and they repeated the procedure. The records diverged: the two watches agreed closely when their users stood together but differed more when the users stood apart. We had not isolated the exact reason for the difference, yet the old and new records could no longer be treated as directly comparable.

I wanted to remove Tuesday's page, as if tearing it out would make the problem disappear. The coach stopped me. Instead, I added a note explaining how the measurements had been taken and why any comparison required caution.

At the next meeting, I withdrew my accusation. Jo looked relieved. She had not been asking to avoid practice; she had wanted the team to draw a justified conclusion. We still needed to train, but we also needed a consistent way to monitor our progress.

I had treated a number as if it came without a history. Now each page records the timing position, procedure and any interruption. These details make the notebook less concise, but more useful. When a result surprises me, my first question is no longer, “Who has failed?” I ask what might have changed and what else we need to check. The figures still matter. I have given up the belief that they can interpret themselves. Jo's question did not weaken our training. It improved the way we evaluated it. A reliable record needs more than neat columns; it needs enough context for another person to understand both what was measured and what remains uncertain.
