### A组（第1—5题）

#### A1

Joining a shared project can be harder than doing a familiar job alone. You may know how to make something yet hesitate to suggest an idea, or discover that everyone is waiting for someone else to begin. The following advice concerns cooperation in a fictional school workshop. Its aim is to make contributions easier to combine, rather than to decide who is the most talented member. A quiet participant should have a useful place in the work without being expected to become a different person.

1. Choose a task that leaves room for another person. Instead of arriving with a finished design and asking for agreement, bring a question that someone else can help answer. For a model theatre, for example, you could prepare two possible entrances and ask a partner to test how a figure moves through them. The work is substantial enough to discuss but still open to change. Simply giving somebody a decorative job after all the decisions have been made offers much less opportunity to contribute.
2. Make uncertainty visible before offering an answer. Say which part you have checked and which part is still a guess. A label such as “measured on the small model only” tells your partner where further evidence is needed. This does not require an apology for every suggestion. It helps another person build on your work without unknowingly treating a prediction as a result. Someone who asks a careful question may be supporting the project rather than attacking its author.
3. Use a small trial to resolve a disagreement. If two members disagree about the width of a doorway, try the same figure through each version before choosing. Agree beforehand on what would count as success. Changing the figure halfway through would make the results difficult to compare. A trial need not settle every design question, but it can replace an argument about personal preference with a more specific discussion of what happened.
4. Leave a clear route for the next contribution. Before ending a session, record the version tested, the result and one unresolved question. Invite the next member to take up that question instead of guessing your intentions from an unfinished object. A photograph may help, but it does not explain why a doorway was changed. The note should allow someone who missed the session to continue intelligently. Cooperation lasts beyond the moment when all the participants are standing at the same bench.

### B组（第6—10题）

#### B1

Dear Nella,

You asked why the battered wooden box still stands beside our workbench when we have newer tools. Before you leave for your own first workshop, I want to tell you its story. Nothing about it belongs in a museum of famous inventions. What matters is the way its use changed as it passed between people who were learning to trust one another.

When I was fourteen, a broken wheel prevented my father from taking his cart to the market. We could not afford a replacement immediately. Mr Vale lent us a box of tools and spent an evening showing us how to fit a temporary wooden support. My father was relieved, but it was the patience of the explanation that stayed with him. “When we can help somebody learn,” he said, “we must leave time for the questions.”

Later, after our circumstances improved, my father kept a place at his bench for neighbours trying unfamiliar repairs. He refused payment for teaching a method he had been shown freely. He did ask people to explain the method back to him before working alone. I watched him turn a practical favour into a habit of making room for learners, even when finishing the repair himself would have been faster.

At one of those sessions he met Lena, who had moved into the village and was trying to mend a music stand. Her persistence impressed me, as did her willingness to ask why a joint had failed. Years afterwards, your grandmother and I opened our workbench to a small group every month. She often reminded me that being grateful for a teacher meant becoming patient with a beginner ourselves.

Your mother began a weekly repair session at her community centre last year. She used photographs and written steps, not our old tools, because her visitors brought different objects. I was delighted. The box was no longer the important part. A borrowed opportunity had become a practice she could adapt, rather than a possession she had to preserve unchanged.

Keep the box if you like it, Nella. More importantly, when someone brings an uncertain first attempt to you, remember the evening Mr Vale stayed to answer questions. You can carry this family story forward without copying our workshop or repeating every repair.

With love,
Grandad

### C组（第11—15题）

#### C1

Every spring in an invented coastal region called Leren, a population of migratory marsh birds travels north to breed. The birds return south after summer, spending winter in milder wetlands. Their routes differ across breeding areas. A fictional field report asks how these journeys relate to present habitat and what might change if future warming alters the wetlands. It separates movements actually recorded from possibilities produced by a computer model.

Over four summers, the team visited several nesting sites, reaching some by boat and returning when weather permitted. Researchers caught 64 different birds in total. They collected feather samples from 52 of these birds to compare inherited characteristics and fitted small tracking devices to the captured group. Devices on 43 birds eventually returned usable records of at least one complete migration. A failed device was not treated as evidence that its bird had remained at the nesting site.

Those 43 tracked birds supplied 129 completed migration flights over the study period. The number of flights exceeded the number of birds because some individuals contributed journeys in several seasons. Individual birds often returned to a similar route, while birds from different breeding areas tended to use different corridors. Recorded autumn journeys lasted between 16 and 39 days. These observations describe the monitored journeys, not every member of the population.

To explore future habitat, the researchers combined maps with a climate model using a specified rising-temperature pathway. If warming continues along that pathway and the birds retain the same habitat preferences, the southern winter range could extend northwards. At the same time, some northern breeding wetlands could become less suitable. The model does not observe future birds, nor does it establish that every bird will respond alike. Different temperature pathways or changed habitat preferences could produce different projections.

The report therefore links repeated travel records with a conditional question about habitat. Tracking helps reveal where the monitored birds went; modelling explores where suitable places might later remain. Neither technique alone guarantees the future of this population, but keeping their roles separate makes the report more informative.

### D组（第16—20题）

#### D1

A student can enjoy a lively afternoon and still feel dissatisfied with life as a whole. Conversely, a difficult day need not erase a person's broader sense that life is going well. This distinction opens a fictional report from Harbour District. Before presenting changes in survey scores, the writers explain why a question about yesterday and a question about life overall are measuring different things.

The district's Living Survey asked separate representative samples of residents aged 18–29 and residents aged 60 or over about overall satisfaction with life. Respondents used a scale from 0 to 10. They also recorded positive and negative feelings from the previous day, but those answers formed a separate part of the report. The life-satisfaction score was not a sum of yesterday's emotions, and an enjoyable event could not by itself establish a person's answer to the broader question.

In the earlier survey, the younger sample averaged 7.2 and the older sample 6.8 on the life-satisfaction scale. Five years later, new samples from the same age bands averaged 6.5 and 7.0 respectively. The younger band's average had fallen below the older band's. This comparison concerns averages for age bands at two dates; it does not show that every young respondent became less satisfied or that the same people were interviewed twice. It also does not rank every possible age group in the district.

A separate School Involvement Survey asked pupils how often they had felt involved in school activities during the previous month, using a scale from 1 to 3. Year 9 samples averaged 2.20 and then 2.08; Year 12 samples averaged 2.14 and then 2.10. Both averages fell, with a larger numerical decline in Year 9 on this particular scale. These figures provide a second, specifically school-related trend. They cannot be added to the residents' life-satisfaction scores or interpreted as the same outcome measured less precisely.

Within the later Living Survey, more evening screen time was associated with lower reported satisfaction. The researchers did not assign screen time experimentally. Work schedules, health or dissatisfaction itself might affect the amount of time people spent online. The report proposes following these possibilities in later research. Its findings support investigating a relationship, while leaving the direction and causes of that relationship open.
