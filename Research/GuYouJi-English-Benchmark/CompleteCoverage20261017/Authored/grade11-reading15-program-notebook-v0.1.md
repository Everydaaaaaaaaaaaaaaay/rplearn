### A组（第1—3题）

Explore the fictional Harbour Cabinet Museum with a programme that suits your family. All four programmes are free, but every family must reserve a place online before taking part. A confirmation message gives the session details and anything simple to bring. The programme descriptions below state their intended audiences and locations; a flexible activity format does not cancel the advance-booking rule.

#### A1

Harbour Clues
For visitors of all ages, Harbour Clues runs on selected weekends during the year. Families examine collection objects and consider what each might reveal about daily life beside the harbour. Guides offer short demonstrations and invite questions. Different weekends use different objects, so families can join a single session without following a fixed sequence of lessons.

#### A2

First Finds
First Finds is specifically designed for families with children aged 0–5. An educator introduces one theme through picture books, objects and playful activities, with caregivers taking part alongside their children. Families can choose which activity to try first during their reserved session. Places are still reserved online beforehand; free choice within the session is not permission to arrive without booking.

#### A3

Discover Together
This museum-room programme for families with children aged 3–8 is offered once each month. Families compare a small selection of objects connected with that month’s theme and create their own response through making or drawing. A guest craft worker sometimes contributes a demonstration. The purpose is exploration together, not judging families against one model answer.

#### A4

At-Home Cabinet
For families joining from home, this educator-led online session is held once each month. Ordinary household objects help participants explore an idea linked to the museum collection. There is no scheduled guest craft worker. A preparation message is sent after booking, and alternatives are suggested if a family lacks one of the proposed materials.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

At the fictional Ashford Garden Centre, employee Rafi knew that customer Elin usually bought the same small tray of seedlings. On a busy morning, he began reaching for it as soon as he saw her. Then he noticed that she was carrying a notebook and stopped to ask whether she wanted her usual order. That pause turned a familiar transaction into a different conversation.

Elin was planning a shared planting area with several neighbours. She needed to compare the centre’s labelled tray sizes and collection dates before taking any proposal back to the group. A single tray chosen for her would not answer those questions. She did not ask Rafi to make the plan; she asked if she could keep a note of the available choices while they spoke.

Rafi offered a spare order sheet and wrote the sizes beside the corresponding collection dates. He then asked Elin to check that the notes were clear. She pointed out that two tray names looked almost identical when written quickly. Rafi added the full names and placed the dates on separate lines. Elin could now show her neighbours the alternatives instead of trying to reproduce a hurried conversation from memory.

The next week she returned with a decision that differed from her usual purchase. “The useful part was being able to take the choices away,” she said. Rafi had not promised that every tray would suit the neighbours’ plot, and the notes did not replace their discussion. They had simply made the information easier to share and consider.

The centre has since kept spare comparison sheets at the counter. Staff confirm current details each time rather than treating an old sheet as a permanent offer. Rafi says he learned that remembering a customer can be helpful without being a substitute for listening. His small pause allowed Elin to explain a changed purpose, and her feedback improved a sheet that other customers might also use. The report’s lesson lies in that exchange, not in claiming that a familiar order should always be repeated.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

A short instruction that works well for an experienced group may puzzle a newcomer. In a fictional community workshop, the phrase “check the edge” had been used for years. Long-serving members knew which edge, what to compare it with and whom to ask if it differed. A new member saw several edges and had no reason to know which comparison was intended.

The phrase developed during repeated work on the same kind of object. Members could point, demonstrate and ask questions while using it. Over time, some of that shared experience disappeared from the written instruction because the regular group no longer needed it stated. The words became shorter, but the knowledge required to use them did not disappear.

The difficulty became clear when the workshop offered sessions to visitors. A volunteer initially assumed that the newcomers were failing to pay attention. Watching them work changed that view. They were checking carefully, but not all were checking the same edge. Their actions followed reasonable interpretations of an instruction that depended on information they had not been given.

Much of the instruction’s meaning was **implicit**: experienced members understood it without finding it fully expressed in the words. To help visitors, the team added a named reference point, a simple comparison and a person to consult if unsure. It also kept a shorter reminder version for regular members. The two versions served different audiences without implying that one group was less capable of learning.

This example illustrates a problem of shared knowledge, not a reason to make every instruction as long as possible. Extra words can also obscure a useful direction if they add nothing relevant. The aim is to identify what a reader must know and decide which parts can reasonably be assumed. A change of audience may require an explanation that was unnecessary among people working together every week.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

An invented project at the Roven Skills Institute examined repair-job scheduling under three decision arrangements. A planning tool could organise ordinary jobs quickly, but its stored estimates did not always reflect a newly reported shortage of a particular part. Researchers wanted to compare the arrangements in a defined exercise rather than assume that automation alone would improve a real repair business.

Thirty adult volunteers were assigned by a random draw to three equal groups. They all received the same job list, estimated repair times and part-shortage notices. The planner-only group arranged the work using those documents. The automatic-only group accepted the tool’s timetable without editing it. The joint-decision group examined the proposed timetable and could change it in response to the notices. Practice time and the total time allowed were identical.

The joint-decision group produced the most workable schedules on average. Its members used the tool to organise routine jobs and changed some entries after noticing that the required part was unavailable. The planner-only group generally noticed the shortages but spent longer constructing an initial timetable. The automatic-only group finished quickly yet retained some jobs that could not begin as scheduled. The result depended on counting workable plans, not just speed.

This was a single comparison involving three groups. It provided support for combining this tool with informed review under the exercise’s conditions. A different tool that incorporated shortages accurately might produce a different pattern. The study also did not show how trainees would perform after months of work or when a notice itself was incorrect.

Institute researcher Jon Vale emphasises that an opportunity to change a timetable is useful only if reviewers understand what a change affects. Training and clear responsibility would remain important in any later trial. The team therefore plans to vary the task and the reliability of the information before drawing workplace conclusions. For now, the finding concerns a particular partnership between routine organisation and human judgement, not the disappearance of either role.
