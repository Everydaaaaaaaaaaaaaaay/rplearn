### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Aster Day Club

Our day club is opening a small workshop for adults with learning disabilities. We need volunteers who enjoy helping people choose activities at their own pace. There are several ways to contribute, and applicants from different backgrounds are welcome. The coordinator will introduce each new companion to the group and explain the routine.

#### Position

Workshop Companion (No pay; free meals during each duty period).

#### Place

Northmere

#### Hours

Part time. One weekday morning and one afternoon, arranged with the coordinator.

#### Role

You will support members as they prepare simple projects, find materials and try unfamiliar tasks. You will help them understand workshop rules and protect their rights and personal belongings. Staff will remain available when a member needs additional support. Our first concern is that members feel valued and know that their choices deserve to be heard.

#### Skills and Experience Required

Applicants must be honest and patient, listen carefully, hold a current driving licence, and communicate fluently in both written and spoken English. Careful listening matters when a member explains an idea; driving and English communication are needed for agreed trips and written arrangements. Experience helping participants in a comparable support workshop is preferred, although applicants without it may still apply.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Owen arrived early to guide families at his school’s open afternoon. While collecting his badge, he kept thinking about the astronomy talk his friend was going to give. He hoped to find a front-row seat later. A teacher mentioned that the model display had changed rooms, but Owen, still imagining the talk, hurried away before checking the new room number.

Soon three families asked him where to find the models. He was unusually anxious about giving them an answer: at the previous open afternoon, he had confidently sent one family to the wrong building. He had promised himself to check directions properly this time. Now he could remember the old room but not the teacher’s new instruction.

He tried calling the front desk, only to discover that his phone had almost no charge and shut down before the call connected. For a moment he considered sending the families towards the old room anyway. Then he remembered a geography teacher’s advice to start from a reliable reference point instead of filling gaps with guesses.

Owen asked the families to wait beside the entrance desk. He went to the noticeboard, where the current programme showed the model display in the library rather than the science room. To make sure the programme was current, he checked its date and asked the teacher standing by the board to confirm the change. He then returned and led the families to the library, explaining why the route differed from the older signs still on the wall.

When the last family had entered, Owen helped replace an old sign near the stairs. The task took longer than guessing would have done, but nobody needed to retrace a journey. Later, he reached the astronomy room just as his friend began speaking. There was no seat at the front, yet Owen found he could enjoy the talk perfectly well from the back.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

At the town’s making room, a shelf of unused paper bags is labelled “Take what you need—**no strings attached**.” Visitors do not have to pay, register or promise a future contribution. People with spare bags can add clean, usable ones. The shelf began after a class finished making clay models and several learners had nothing suitable in which to carry their work home.

The first report describes a retired gardener bringing a stack of bags and a young apprentice taking three for a painting lesson. They attended different sessions and had never met, but each found a use for the same shelf. The room kept a short note of both visits in its first report. Neither visitor was asked to explain an income or prove that a purchase would have been impossible.

The organisers soon learned that a shared shelf still needs care. Bags with broken bottoms were put aside because they could not carry work safely. A note asked donors to check each bag before adding it. This rule concerned the condition of donated materials; it did not create a rule requiring users to donate before they could take a bag.

The making room does not count success by how many people become permanent donors. One week someone may have a cupboard full of spare bags; another week they may need one unexpectedly. The organisers want a small exchange of useful things to become a familiar part of visiting the room. They are not selling membership or collecting money for new equipment.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

Students can now put a typed label on a diagram by choosing a template and sending it to a printer. Such tools are convenient, and they reduce the need to make every label by hand. But a polished label does not necessarily show how well its writer understands the words. A fictional teaching report considers what should still be taught when the final page is produced digitally.

The report separates producing a clean copy from composing a message. A student who merely pastes a suggested label may never decide whether its words describe the diagram accurately. A student who plans the wording first, using either handwriting or a keyboard, has to make that decision. The authors therefore look at the processes behind the finished page.

Their first activity compares handwritten and typed explanations of the same simple diagram. Twenty-eight students complete both versions in different orders. The handwritten explanations contain more links between parts of the diagram in this activity; the typed copies are revised more quickly. The report treats these as different advantages, not a reason to remove either tool from the classroom.

A second activity focuses on spelling. Students must choose between similar forms offered by a checking program and explain the meaning they intend. Those with stronger knowledge of the forms make more choices consistent with their explanations. The program can detect an unusual spelling, but it cannot always select the intended word. Spelling knowledge supports the link between what a writer wants to say and the symbols used to say it.

The authors describe a word taking shape as an **inner picture** before a student writes it. An idea comes first; a likely word form is brought into view in thought, then placed on paper or entered through keys. If a letter pattern seems unfamiliar, the student may pause to check it. This process happens before the page itself offers anything for the eyes to inspect.

The report argues for teaching spelling and giving students real chances to compose by hand while also teaching thoughtful use of digital tools. Choosing from a checker’s list still calls for knowledge; the existence of a list does not supply that knowledge automatically. The activities are fictional and limited, so they do not establish that one method is best for all students or all writing tasks.
