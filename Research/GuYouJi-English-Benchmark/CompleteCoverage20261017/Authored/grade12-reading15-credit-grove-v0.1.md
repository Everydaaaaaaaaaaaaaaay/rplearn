### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

### Rowan Upper School certificate requirements

The following rules belong to an invented school. Students must meet all the requirements to receive its upper-school certificate.

### Compulsory credits

- 4 credits in English (one credit in each of Years 9–12)
- 4 credits in mathematics (including at least one credit in Year 11 or 12)
- 3 credits in project courses from the three approved groups
- 3 credits in science
- 1 credit in community history
- 1 credit in local geography
- 1 credit in the arts
- 1 credit in health and physical education
- 1 credit in an additional language
- 0.5 credit in career studies
- 0.5 credit in civic participation

### Optional credits

Students must earn 10 optional credits from approved courses. Up to 3 of these optional credits may come from approved partner-college courses; they are included, not added to the optional total.

### Online learning

Students who entered Year 9 in September 2024 or later must complete at least 2 credits online. These online credits count within the compulsory or optional requirements above; they are not extra credits.

### Literacy requirement

The literacy rule takes effect on 1 October 2026. Students graduating in 2026–2027 or later must satisfy the school’s literacy check as well as its credit requirements.

### Community involvement

At least 40 hours of approved community activity are required. These hours are not course credits. Before beginning, students must:

- check that the proposed activity meets the school’s approval rules;
- consult the school’s list of approved and prohibited activities;
- discuss the plan with a parent or guardian if they are under 18.

Students aged 18 or over must still verify that an activity qualifies, but the parent-or-guardian discussion rule does not apply to them.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

On a wet morning in the fictional Aldercombe Grove, conservation volunteer Mikel found that a temporary fence had fallen across the edge of a marked path. People had begun stepping around it into a newly planted strip. He recorded the damaged section and placed a warning at a safe distance rather than asking passing visitors to make their own way through the plants.

Mikel first joined the grove’s volunteer team as a student sorting field notes. He now helps train new members, whose work includes checking path markers, clearing discarded material and noting changes after heavy rain. He says that the least visible part of the work is often its most useful: a clear record can explain why a later repair was needed when the damage itself is no longer there.

An older fence was **decommissioned** during the team’s autumn work. It was formally taken out of use, and its unsafe sections were removed before a replacement was put into service. Mikel asked new volunteers to compare the old repair notes with what they could see on the ground. That prevented them from treating a recently replaced panel as a piece of abandoned material.

The grove crosses the boundary between two local districts. Their teams now share dates, photographs and agreed location names, so that observations from opposite ends can be combined. They also meet to discuss cases in which the same sign has been described differently. The aim is to improve the reliability of joint work, not to decide which district produces the most impressive number of reports.

The teams have recorded fewer obstacles beside the repaired path, but Mikel is careful about what that demonstrates. Conditions change with weather, foot traffic and the timing of visits. Continued observation is needed to tell whether an improvement lasts. He values volunteers who can work steadily in difficult conditions and also reconsider an interpretation when another person’s record supplies new information. Protecting the grove depends on both kinds of effort.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Suppose a new museum volunteer is about to explain an unfamiliar object to visitors. She may know several facts yet still be unsure how to begin. In a fictional training session, volunteer Isha tried picturing the first minute: where she would stand, what she would point to and which question a visitor might ask. The imagined scene helped make a vague worry more specific.

While rehearsing in thought, she noticed that her intended opening depended on a small mark visitors could not see from where she planned to stand. She moved the object during the real practice and tested the view with a partner. The imagined difficulty was useful because it led to a check. It was not evidence that a view imagined from one position must match the view another person actually has.

Afterwards, Isha reported feeling less uncertain about her first sentence. The account does not include a measurement of physical skill or a controlled comparison of visitor learning. Her change in confidence and the practical adjustment are different kinds of information. Neither establishes that imagining an explanation has the same effect on the body as delivering it, or that every visitor will now understand it.

Mental rehearsal in this account means running through a sequence from a particular point of view, then asking what might have been left out. A presenter can picture a transition between two ideas. A guide can imagine being interrupted and consider where to resume. Watching somebody else speak may provide useful examples, but it is different from privately imagining one’s own next action. Both can be followed by practice with real listeners.

The limits matter because imagination tends to supply conditions that are convenient. A cooperative listener in thought may ask a clear question; an actual visitor may ask something unexpected. Rehearsal should therefore leave a plan open to revision rather than freeze it into a script that must work. Isha’s experience illustrates a preparatory use of imagination: making assumptions noticeable enough to test. It does not make confidence a substitute for evidence about performance.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

Researchers in a fictional project at Greyford Lab have constructed a digital model that restores a simple surface pattern after a unit is removed. The project is concerned with coordinating a sequence of decisions. It does not show that an organism regrows a limb or that a computer can recover the true history of an object when the evidence is missing.

The model combines a damage map with a set of permitted local patterns. First, a process marks the damaged position, distinguishing an empty place from a unit that is present but differently coloured. The controller then checks the neighbouring pattern against its stored rules and directs a matching unit into the gap. A final comparison verifies whether the joins are consistent. The rules guide placement; they are not a physical frame supporting a real surface.

In some trials, the surrounding units supported more than one possible completion. The model then flagged the gap as unresolved instead of announcing that one guess had recovered the original. This distinction was central to the design: producing a neat pattern and identifying a uniquely supported reconstruction are not the same thing. The reported success concerned gaps for which the permitted rules selected one completion.

The researchers hope to improve the repair of modular surfaces by learning how local information can guide coordinated replacement. Before any physical application, they would need to investigate materials, forces, wear and cases with incomplete information. A digital join that satisfies a rule says nothing by itself about whether a physical joint can carry a load.

The report is positive about the model’s ability to expose where a decision is supported and where it remains uncertain. Its promise lies partly in the refusal to hide unresolved cases. Further work is presented as testing those boundaries, rather than simply increasing the number of attractive completed patterns. The model offers a design principle to explore, not a finished method for repairing every damaged object.
