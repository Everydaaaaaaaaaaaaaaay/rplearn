### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

### Northbay Upper School certificate requirements

The following rules belong to an invented school. Students must meet all the requirements to receive its upper-school certificate.

### Compulsory credits

- 4 credits in English (one credit in each of Years 9–12)
- 3 credits in mathematics (including at least one credit in Year 11 or 12)
- 3 credits in project courses from the three approved groups
- 2 credits in science
- 1 credit in community history
- 1 credit in local geography
- 1 credit in the arts
- 1 credit in health and physical education
- 1 credit in an additional language
- 0.5 credit in career studies
- 0.5 credit in civic participation

### Optional credits

Students must earn 12 optional credits from approved courses. Up to 4 of these optional credits may come from approved partner-college courses; they are included, not added to the optional total.

### Online learning

Students who entered Year 9 in September 2025 or later must complete at least 2 credits online. These online credits count within the compulsory or optional requirements above; they are not extra credits.

### Literacy requirement

The literacy rule takes effect on 1 September 2026. Students graduating in 2026–2027 or later must satisfy the school’s literacy check as well as its credit requirements.

### Community involvement

At least 40 hours of approved community activity are required. These hours are not course credits. Before beginning, students must:

- check that the proposed activity meets the school’s approval rules;
- consult the school’s list of approved and prohibited activities;
- discuss the plan with a parent or guardian if they are under 18.

Students aged 18 or over must still verify that an activity qualifies, but the parent-or-guardian discussion rule does not apply to them.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

During a winter inspection of the fictional Rellin Dune Reserve, volunteer leader Anya noticed fresh footprints beside a damaged path barrier. The marks mattered because visitors leaving the marked route could disturb the young plants the team was trying to protect. She recorded the location before touching anything. A photograph of an empty path would not have shown where the problem had begun.

Anya joined the reserve’s volunteer group eight years earlier, initially helping to label observations collected by more experienced members. She now coordinates visits, checks equipment and teaches new volunteers how to keep records that another person can understand. Strong wind and shifting sand often make the work uncomfortable, but the team avoids entering unsafe areas merely to complete a planned route.

Last autumn, volunteers **dismantled** an old barrier that had become unstable. They took its loose sections apart and removed them before installing a better-marked replacement. The task required more than enthusiasm: members had to identify which pieces were unsafe and carry them away without trampling the area they meant to protect. Anya asked the newer volunteers to observe the first section before working in pairs.

The reserve lies between two villages, whose teams once kept separate records of damaged signs. They now exchange dated notes and use the same location labels. A repair recorded by one team is therefore less likely to be mistaken for a new problem by the other. Anya regards this cooperation as part of conservation work itself, because repeated visits are of limited value if the information cannot be combined.

Recent checks have found fewer people leaving the path at that spot, although the team does not claim that a new barrier alone caused every change. Weather and visitor numbers vary too. The volunteers plan to continue observing and adjusting the route. Their work combines practical effort with careful judgement: noticing a problem, acting without creating another one, and leaving a record that others can use.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

Imagine preparing to explain a simple demonstration in front of a class. You can read your notes again, but you can also picture yourself lifting the first object, pausing for a question and moving to the next step. That imagined sequence is a kind of mental rehearsal. In this fictional example, it helps a presenter inspect a plan before carrying it out; it does not count as having performed the demonstration.

Presenter Mara tried the approach before a practice session. As she pictured the sequence, she realised that a question from the audience might arrive before she had put one object down. Her plan had left no hand free for the next one. She changed the order in her notes, then tested it with the actual objects. The useful point was the problem she had noticed, not a promise that imagining an action makes every detail physically possible.

After the session, Mara said that she had felt more prepared to begin. That was a report about her own experience. Nobody measured a change in her strength, recorded her brain activity or compared her performance with a large control group. It would therefore be unjustified to turn her comment into a general claim that mental rehearsal produces the same bodily effects as physical practice.

The approach can be understood as a way of asking more specific questions. “What happens after I lift this?” may reveal something that “Will I do well?” leaves vague. A musician could similarly picture a difficult transition before testing it on an instrument. A volunteer might mentally walk through a greeting and the first question a visitor is likely to ask. In each case, thinking identifies a possible issue; real conditions may still expose another one.

Mental rehearsal is most useful in this account when it leads back to observation and adjustment. An imagined audience may be quieter than the real class, and a step that seems smooth in thought may need physical practice. Mara’s experience suggests a modest role for imagination: it can make a plan available for inspection. It cannot certify success, replace every rehearsal or settle what another person will do.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A fictional materials project at Vey Institute has explored how a protective panel might coordinate small repairs. The model is inspired by the idea of parts responding to a local problem, but it is an engineered demonstration, not a study showing that an organism can regenerate or that a human injury can be treated.

The team built panels from small connected units and combined local sensing with a shared location code. In a trial, a sensor first detects a break in one unit’s outer layer. It then sends a location signal to neighbouring units. A controller checks that signal against the panel’s layout and guides a replacement patch towards the indicated gap. Only after the patch is aligned does a fastening step hold it in place. The sequence matters: attaching a patch before its position is checked could leave the original gap uncovered.

The researchers describe the controller as coordinating information, not as supplying the patch material or generating unlimited energy. Sensors, a stored layout, replacement material and the fastening process each play a different role. When a location code was deliberately left out of one trial, the system could signal that something was wrong without placing the patch correctly. Detecting damage was therefore not the same achievement as completing a repair.

The team hopes eventually to develop coatings that repair small defects more reliably. That is an objective for later work, not an established industrial benefit. The trials used small flat panels under controlled conditions; uneven surfaces, changing temperatures and repeated damage remain untested. A functioning model does not yet establish a durable product.

The report presents the coordinated sequence as a promising design idea while keeping the remaining questions visible. Its interest lies in making several limited components work together, rather than describing one component as a universal repair device. Further tests will have to show where the approach fails as well as where it works.
