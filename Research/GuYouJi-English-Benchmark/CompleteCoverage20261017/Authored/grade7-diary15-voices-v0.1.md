### A组（第1—3题）

Homes do not all look alike. Three children describe where they live and the things they do around their homes.

#### A1

Nora
Our wooden house stands on short posts above the ground. After heavy rain, water runs below the floor instead of staying against it. That is why my family chose this design. Steps lead to a small balcony where Dad keeps pots of flowers. I like watching the rain from there, but I take my shoes inside before a storm.

#### A2

Ben
Our house is beside a large vegetable garden. We grow beans in summer and carrots in the cooler months. I help to carry vegetables to the kitchen when they are ready; we cannot pick every kind in every season. We do not keep cows or sheep. A narrow path through the garden leads to my grandparents’ little house.

#### A3

Suki
My home faces a network of **waterways**: long channels made for boats to move through our town. A small boat carries us to the market on Saturdays. When I visit my friend across the channel, I usually use the footbridge. I can hear people talking from passing boats, but there is no road for cars directly outside our front door.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

It is my human’s birthday today. I, Pip the cat, have planned to be very helpful. Here is my report.

5 a.m. — I heard a bag beside the bed and thought breakfast had arrived early. Wonderful! I jumped down to investigate. The bag held paper for wrapping gifts, so I sat on it to keep it flat. My human said my name in a rather surprised voice.

7 a.m. — My human moved the paper onto a high shelf. I watched from the floor. Surely she could see how well I had pressed it! She gave me my usual dry food. I ate it, but I was beginning to think she did not understand my birthday service.

8 a.m. — A visitor brought a box tied with a green ribbon. I was ready to catch the ribbon, but my human put the whole box in a cupboard. Then everyone went out. She had left my red ball nearby, as if an old toy could replace that wonderful ribbon.

3 p.m. — I have already pushed my red ball under the sofa and pulled it out again. That was my afternoon game. I plan to sleep on the chair next, but first I must finish this report. I have not slept there yet.

9 p.m. — My human came home and opened her gifts. She finally gave me the green ribbon, but only held it up for a moment before putting it away. I wanted a long game. She laughed and called me a good helper. What a strange idea of help she has!

12 a.m. (start of the next day) — The birthday has ended; this is midnight at the start of tomorrow. My human has gone to bed. I expected a day full of new games, but I mostly watched things being put beyond my reach. Tomorrow I shall arrange my own entertainment.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

At our fictional school club, we play a target game called Gate Roll. Players roll a soft ball across a marked floor towards small gates. A narrow gate earns more points than a wide one, but reaching it is harder. Everyone begins close to the gates, and the club leader changes the starting line as players gain practice. The game is a club invention, not an official sport with national rules.

One rule is important in our version: wait behind the starting line until the previous player’s ball has stopped. Then take your turn from the same line as the other players in your round. The leader measures a result only after the ball has come to rest. These rules make the scores easier to compare; this article is not a complete set of safety instructions.

Power is not the whole answer. A strong roll may pass the best gate. Before rolling, a player chooses a route and considers what might happen. That is a little like chess: both ask you to think ahead rather than act without a plan. The movements and equipment, of course, are quite different.

I enjoy the game because each turn offers a new small problem. If you like choosing a plan and then testing it with your hands, our club game may interest you. You need not roll hard to begin. I would recommend trying a simple round before deciding whether it is for you.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

Mara wanted to cross a glass bridge to the bell garden. At the gate, a notice said, “Speak your own name and the bridge will hear you.” She tried, but the voice that came out sounded like her brother’s. Earlier that morning it had sounded like a bird. “I must get my own voice back,” she said. She was not hungry and was not looking for food.

Mara searched beside the path for something that might help. Behind a red leaf she found a small silver whistle. A blue snail was sitting on it, reading a book upside down. Mara stepped closer, but the snail kept turning the pages. At last it looked up and asked, “Whose voice have you brought?”

“▲,” Mara replied. “It has changed three times today. I remember my usual voice, but I cannot make it come out.”

“That is a poor answer,” said the snail. “Explain it properly.”

Mara explained how her voice had changed on the walk. “Could the whistle help?” she asked politely.

“Questions, questions!” said the snail. “You should bring me an answer.” It shut the book and looked away.

Mara felt annoyed. The snail had heard her explanation but had offered no help. She turned to leave. Behind her it called, “Wait! There is something about the whistle you ought to know.” She stopped and came back. The snail was just opening its book again when a bell rang on the far side of the glass bridge. Mara was still on the gate side, with her strange voice and no way across yet.
