### 全组回填

I helped pack the stock in my uncle's record shop during its final week. Rising rent made the business impossible to maintain; no final sale could reverse that. Still, I wanted the closing to become a success story.

I suggested a farewell evening with music and refreshments. My uncle agreed, provided we did not pretend the shop was reopening. He wanted to thank customers, not give them false hope. My poster announced the closing date and invited people to share a memory of the shop.

I expected stories about rare records and famous performers. Instead, a bus driver recalled buying one after his first paid shift. A retired teacher described afternoons spent listening without buying anything. Another customer said the shop had been a warm place to wait when her bus was delayed. Their memories were less about the objects than the experiences the shop had provided.

At first, I worried that these ordinary accounts would make the evening disappointing. My uncle, though, listened without looking at the till. One customer recalled how my uncle had replaced a damaged sleeve for free. He had not known the record was her late brother's gift. He had done a small kindness without knowing its significance.

I asked whether we could write down some of the stories. A few people preferred not to have theirs shared, so we left them unrecorded. Others wrote short cards, which we pinned beside the remaining records.

The evening did not save the business. The rent remained unchanged; my uncle had arranged another job. I had hoped the event would somehow alter the ending. What it changed was our understanding of what that ending meant.

On closing day, a customer returned a record borrowed years earlier. Finding it while moving house, he had felt guilty about keeping it. My uncle accepted his apology and asked what he had thought of the music. Their conversation lasted longer than the shop's last transaction.

We locked the door at six. I wanted to say something memorable, but my uncle simply asked me to carry the box of cards. The cards seemed too slight to stand for twenty years of work. Yet their stories supplied a context for purchases that had looked ordinary on paper.

Later, I made an album with space for more stories. I did not label it “How we saved the shop”. That would have been untrue. Nor did I call the whole experience a failure. The loss of the business was real, but it did not erase the value of what had happened there.

My uncle still misses the shop. He does not need me to replace that feeling with a cheerful slogan. Sometimes we discuss a card in the album. Remembering does not unlock the locked door. It gives us a way to carry part of the place forward without denying that it has gone. I had wanted a neat result that would remove the disappointment. Instead, I learned to let gratitude and sadness coexist. Neither had to cancel the other.
