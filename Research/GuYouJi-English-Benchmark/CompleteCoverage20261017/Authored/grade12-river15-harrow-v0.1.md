### A组（第1—3题）

The following fictional coastal and lakeside caves offer four different subjects for a visitor-centre exhibition. The descriptions concern their features and viewing programmes, not instructions for entering caves.

#### A1

Swift Chamber
A sheltered sea chamber on the north coast has become known for the island’s only recorded nesting colony of silver-wing swifts, an invented bird in this account. Cameras relay images from the nesting ledges to a viewing room above the bay. Although the entrance looks enormous from the water, the exhibition makes no claim that this is the region’s longest cave. Its reputation rests chiefly on the nesting colony rather than its size.

#### A2

Mirror Cove
This lakeside cave is presented through a different exhibition each season. The winter display is particularly popular: low sunlight reflects from a sheet of shore ice and sends moving patterns onto the entrance wall. Visitors watch the effect from the centre’s enclosed gallery. The programme does not involve walking across the frozen lake. Summer displays concentrate on the lake’s changing water level and are not advertised as the best time to see the reflected winter patterns.

#### A3

Blue Shaft
The roof of an old chamber has collapsed, leaving a deep, circular opening connected to the bay. In the fictional visitor programme, a boat with a viewing screen carries people above the opening while an underwater camera shows its lower walls. The attraction lies in seeing a vertical structure from the water rather than following a dry walking tunnel. The exhibition notes that its depth is not a measure of its age.

#### A4

Copper Arch
Bands of reddish stone give this cave its name. A boat-based tour stays outside the arch while a guide explains the stripes visible on the walls. No underwater camera is used here: the coloured layers can be seen directly from the boat. A narrow ledge above the arch contains old survey marks, but it is not part of the visitor route. The tour’s focus is the pattern of the rock rather than nesting birds.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

When eight students first squeezed into a university print room in 1982, they were united by a practical problem: the campus magazine needed to reach readers, and the small press kept jamming. They spent evenings cleaning rollers, arguing over headlines and carrying finished pages across the wet courtyard. Somewhere between the deadlines, they became friends. Someone called them the Night Printers, and the name survived long after the magazine changed its equipment.

Now living in six different towns, the eight still arrange an annual weekend together. Their shared calendar contains birthdays, hospital visits and the dates when somebody needs help moving house. “I never have to build a rescue team from scratch,” says Leena, one of the group. “The team is already there.” She is not talking about an emergency service, but about friends who know when a short message means more than it says.

Staying close has required deliberate work. They alternate the location of reunions, check travel costs before making plans and leave space for those who cannot join a video call. None claims that a group chat can replace every meeting. Occasionally a missed message has caused hurt, and a long conversation has been needed to settle it. Yet they describe the effort as worthwhile because familiarity allows them to offer the kind of help that a newly made acquaintance might not know is needed.

When Arun closed his printing business, he was reluctant to tell anyone how unsettled he felt. A friend from the group invited him to repair old books on Saturday mornings. The invitation did not reopen his business or solve every financial problem. It did give him a regular reason to leave home and talk while his hands were busy. He later said that being useful alongside a friend mattered more than receiving another speech about staying positive.

The connection has spread beyond the original eight. Their children exchange books, and two of their grandchildren now help label photographs for the group archive. At a recent reunion, a daughter asked why the album contained so many pictures of an unreliable printing press. “That machine made us stay in the same room,” Leena replied. “We chose to keep returning after we no longer had to.”

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

In the fictional city of Harrow, the Vale River runs from west to east through the old centre. Flood marks cut into warehouse doorways record periods when the river seemed more threatening than useful. A local saying, “The town bends, but keeps its footing,” recalls those difficult seasons. Yet trade, work and shared memory also grew along the water, which became the thread holding the city’s separate neighbourhoods together.

The river’s importance can even be read in an address. On streets parallel to the Vale, building numbers increase downstream, towards the east. On streets perpendicular to it, numbering begins at the riverbank and increases away from the water. A visitor may therefore need to identify the direction of a street before using a number to work out where a building stands. The system preserves an older habit of treating the river as the point from which the town was understood.

Early photographs show crowded landing places, with traders unloading baskets while children watched the boats. Washing, buying, talking and resting happened beside the same water. The river was a workplace, but its banks were also ordinary social spaces. People did not need to make a special journey to an isolated park in order to spend time there.

That relationship changed in the 1970s. A four-lane road was laid along the southern bank, and several former loading yards became car parks. Traffic moved efficiently through the centre, but pedestrians found few places to reach the water. The change was road-and-parking construction, not the creation of a new railway or bus interchange.

In 2014 the city began converting part of the riverside road into a walking route. A second section later reopened as a park, retaining some old mooring rings beside beds of riverside plants. A planner described the aim as neither recreating a perfect past nor filling every metre with equipment: people should be able to use the banks while leaving room for the river’s living systems. Reconnection, in this account, means shared urban life alongside a functioning natural corridor.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

Imagine a car that could warn its occupants of rapidly changing weather on the next part of a journey. In a fictional design proposal, the system would flag conditions that might produce local flooding and suggest that the route needs reassessment. It would not prevent storms or make every road safe. The proposal concerns the interpretation of weather information, not control over the weather itself.

A designer in the account describes the attraction as reducing the gap between a general forecast and what is happening along a particular route. A forecast for an entire district may be useful while still failing to describe a short stretch of road. The proposed display would show uncertainty as well as the warning, so that a changing estimate would not appear to be an infallible instruction.

Several elements would have to work together before the imagined system could become dependable. The account describes a possible development path, not the results of a completed trial. It offers no measured accuracy rate and no calculation of lives saved. Road decisions would still require information beyond the car’s prediction.

Sensors would record temperature, air pressure and humidity. An onboard model would combine those readings with recent observations, search for patterns and produce a short-range estimate for locations ahead. Measurements alone would not be a forecast: the model would have to interpret them. Its output would then be revised as further readings arrived.

A network could add observations from other vehicles and fixed weather stations. Data would need a location and a time stamp; an old reading from a distant valley could otherwise be mistaken for current local evidence. The designer calls this quality checking **imperative**: without it, sharing more observations could multiply errors rather than improve an estimate. Connecting devices would supply additional evidence, not automatic certainty.

If these difficulties were resolved, the account predicts that route-specific weather displays might become a familiar part of travel in the mid-2030s. That is a conditional outlook for an imagined technology, not a promise that it will arrive by a fixed date. The ending asks readers to picture a possible change in everyday journeys while remembering what still has to be demonstrated.
