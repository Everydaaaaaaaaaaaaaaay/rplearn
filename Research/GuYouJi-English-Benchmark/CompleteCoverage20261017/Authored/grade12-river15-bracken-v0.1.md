### A组（第1—3题）

A regional exhibition introduces four fictional caves along a lake and the adjoining coast. Each description highlights a different feature and an organised viewing programme; it is not a guide to independent exploration.

#### A1

Bat Bell Cave
The chamber is best known as the only recorded summer nursery of copper-eared bats in the fictional district. A remote camera allows visitors in the exhibition room to watch the animals without entering the nursery. The cave has an impressive entrance, but measurements place it well below the longest caves in the area. The nursery, rather than a claim about record dimensions, explains why researchers and visitors take a particular interest in it.

#### A2

Frost Window
At this lake cave, a winter temperature pattern produces thin curtains of frost around the outer opening. The centre’s winter gallery display attracts special interest because cameras reveal how the curtains alter from morning to afternoon. Visitors remain in the gallery; no route over lake ice is offered. In warmer months the same screens show changing shadows, but these are a different programme, without the frost formations that distinguish the winter display.

#### A3

Deep Ring
A submerged circular chamber appears as a dark ring beneath clear water near the coast. The exhibition’s viewing boat carries a small remote camera, which provides images of the rock below the surface. Visitors need not enter the water to follow the chamber’s outline. The name refers to its shape in the view from above, not to an object that was found inside it. Researchers in the account are still discussing how its entrance widened.

#### A4

Ribbon Hall
Long, pale bands run across the dark walls of this coastal chamber. Visitors observe the bands from a tour boat positioned outside the entrance while a guide compares them with drawings from an earlier survey. Unlike Deep Ring, the programme does not use underwater images; the rock patterns are above the surface. The hall’s appeal comes from the contrast of colours and the story of its surveys, not from a claim that it contains a bat nursery.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

On the roof of a college science building in 1976, ten first-year astronomy students discovered that waiting for a clear sky left plenty of time for conversation. They shared flasks of tea, took turns adjusting an awkward telescope and disagreed over whether a faint mark on a photograph was worth another hour outside. They became known as the Roof Circle. The name began as a joke, but the friendships grew out of those long evenings spent together.

Nearly half a century later, the ten live far apart and no longer share a telescope. They still reserve one weekend each year for a reunion, choosing a town that keeps the journey manageable for the largest number. When a member cannot travel, another carries a phone around the meal so that the missing friend can take part. “If one of us drops a thread, there are nine hands to catch it,” says David. The image describes mutual support, not a piece of equipment used on the roof.

The Circle does not present its history as an effortless triumph over distance. Plans have failed, messages have gone unanswered and disagreements have occasionally needed a private call rather than another remark in the group chat. The friends set aside time to repair those small breaks because the relationship gives them something valuable. As one member puts it, an old friend can remember a hope you once had without insisting that you remain the same person forever.

When Celia began caring for her father, she stopped attending the annual weekends. Instead of repeatedly telling her to make more time, the group arranged short visits in pairs, with meals that could be reheated. She did not need to entertain everyone at once or repeat the same explanation ten times. Later, when she could travel again, nobody treated her absence as a debt to be repaid. She says their willingness to adjust was more comforting than any promise that nothing would change.

Their archive now includes photographs taken by their children. At a recent reunion, three teenagers helped identify places in the older images and asked about the strange coats their parents had worn. The conversations gave a new generation a share in the group’s stories. The Roof Circle has lasted because its members keep finding ways to meet one another’s changing lives, rather than trying to remain students forever.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

The fictional city of Bracken grew around the Nera River, which flows from north to south through its oldest neighbourhoods. A line cut into the market wall records a flood that closed the central square for weeks. The same river also carried goods to that square and drew workers to its banks. In the city archive, descriptions of danger sit beside accounts of trade and celebration: the river has shaped a common life, not merely marked an edge on a map.

That central position survives in the address system. Along streets parallel to the Nera, numbers increase downstream, towards the south. Along perpendicular streets, the first numbers are beside the river and larger numbers occur farther from its bank. Knowing which kind of street an address belongs to can therefore help a newcomer interpret it. The two rules are related to the river, but they describe different directions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

In early photographs, stalls stand beside the landing steps and neighbours sit among bundles waiting to be loaded. The water’s edge joined work to leisure. People went there to buy, wash, exchange news and watch the passing boats. It was not a special attraction separated from daily life; many daily activities happened there precisely because the river was close at hand.

In 1968, planners replaced part of that shared edge with a riverside road. Former storage yards were converted into car parks. The new layout helped vehicles cross the centre, while walls and traffic made access to the bank less convenient for people on foot. No new railway station or bus terminal was built as part of this change: the account is specifically about the road and parking spaces.

The city began reversing part of that decision in 2011, opening a former traffic lane to walkers. Later plans added a riverside park while preserving strips of plants and shallower margins for wildlife. A local planner said the object was not to exclude people in the name of nature, or to turn the entire river into a sports facility. It was to let urban life return without removing the natural processes that made the river a living corridor in the first place.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

An imagined vehicle called the Wayfinder would display a warning when observations suggested that weather might change sharply along a planned route. Its designers picture it drawing attention to an approaching band of heavy rain before the occupants reach it. They do not suggest that a car could stop the rain or guarantee a risk-free journey. The proposed function is a forecast, with all the uncertainty that word implies.

In the fictional account, a broad regional forecast is the starting point rather than the enemy of local information. The vehicle would add observations gathered near the journey and identify stretches where conditions might differ from the regional picture. A designer describes the goal as making uncertainty visible and useful, instead of hiding it behind a single reassuring symbol on a screen.

This account sets out a development proposal, not an evaluation of a product already in use. No road trial has established an accuracy percentage or a number of accidents prevented. The designers would have to test the system across seasons and locations before claiming a reliable benefit. A promising display on one demonstration route would not settle those questions.

The vehicle’s sensors would first collect readings of pressure, temperature and moisture. A computer model would combine them with earlier observations and estimate how conditions might develop ahead. As new measurements arrived, the estimate would be recalculated. Thus a sensor reading would be an input, while a forecast would result from interpreting several inputs through a model. Collecting a larger volume of readings alone would not perform that interpretation.

Linked vehicles and roadside stations could contribute further observations. To combine them responsibly, the system would need to distinguish recent local measurements from old or distant ones. The designers call this filtering **imperative**, since a rapidly growing network could otherwise circulate the same misleading reading many times. The connection between devices would widen the evidence available to the model, without making all of that evidence equally useful.

If testing and data quality improved as hoped, the writer predicts that weather displays tailored to a route might become ordinary vehicle features in a later decade. The picture is deliberately conditional. It offers a possible direction for development, not a deadline that manufacturers have already proved they can meet. Readers are invited to imagine the change while keeping the proposal separate from demonstrated performance.
