### Tools around a community sound archive

The following fictional history follows a collection of local sound recordings and the practical devices developed around it. A device’s origin is assigned to the group credited with its first documented design, rather than to every group that subsequently used it. These distinctions matter because the surviving reports often describe a tool only after it has travelled to another institution. A familiar name in a later handbook is therefore not sufficient evidence of where the original arrangement began.

North Quay Volunteers began collecting outdoor interviews before the other groups joined the project. Their first documented contribution was the paired timestamp card, a card with two linked time fields, one identifying the recording and the other a particular spoken passage. This reduced confusion between whole recordings and the short extracts selected for public listening sessions. Two years later, North Quay Volunteers devised the weather sleeve, a folded cover that protected the card without hiding either field. The archive’s early reports credit both designs to that group. Later replacements changed the card’s size but retained the paired fields.

Dock Road Library adopted the cards when it began receiving copies of the recordings. Its own innovation was a copy register, which tracked which institution held each duplicate. The library’s public guide reproduced an illustration of the timestamp card, although the accompanying note acknowledged the earlier North Quay design. Alder Studio subsequently borrowed the cards too. It added a different device: the pitch flag, a short reference tone attached to a working copy to help technicians recognise changes in playback speed. The account credits Alder Studio with the first design of that flag, rather than with the cards it happened to use alongside it.

Hill Laboratory later compared several versions of the pitch flag and proposed a standard position for it on each working copy. The laboratory’s recommendations became widely circulated, which explains why its name often appears beside the device in later manuals. Its reports nevertheless treated the flag as an existing invention. Rowan Workshop addressed another problem entirely. It produced the first documented spool cradle, a support that held a reel steady while its outer wrapping was removed. Rowan’s illustrations show the cradle supporting a stationary reel; the studio’s pitch flag, by contrast, was used during playback.

The final exhibition brought examples from all five groups together. Visitors saw redesigned covers, standardised flags and newly copied cards in one display, but the catalogue preserved the earlier attributions. It warned that the institution supplying an exhibition object was not necessarily the institution that originated its design. The purpose of keeping this distinction was to make the collection’s practical history visible, rather than to award every later user a separate claim to invention.
