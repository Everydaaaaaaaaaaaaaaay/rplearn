### 完整原创独白脚本

I first encountered a travelling planetarium when I was fourteen. It was not in a museum, as people usually assume, but in our school sports hall. From outside, it resembled a large black tent, and I was more curious about the structure than about the stars. Years later, when the organisation advertised for assistants, that memory made me apply.

Setting it up was the first skill I learnt. I had imagined that a framework held the dome upright. In fact, an electric fan keeps air moving into it, and a second member of the team checks that the entrance is properly closed. The fan is much quieter inside than you might expect, although the presenter pauses if somebody opens the door at the wrong moment.

I was concerned that children would be uncomfortable sitting on a school floor. A carpet deals with that problem. We carry it rolled up with the rest of the equipment, and the audience sits directly on it rather than on chairs. At my first session, I spent ages making sure the edges were flat. It seemed a small job until a group of excited children arrived.

The darkness also takes some getting used to. Assistants carry a red torch for moving between the entrance and the projector. Ordinary bright light would interrupt the show. I practised using the torch without pointing it towards anyone's face, and learnt to keep the entrance clear while the presenter was speaking.

The first part of the image that the presenter asks children to identify is the horizon. It gives them a reference point before the sky starts to move. I expected everyone to want names for individual stars immediately, but that comes later. Without a sense of where they are looking, a long list of names is not particularly useful.

After each visit I keep a record of the children's questions. Attendance figures are recorded by the office, so I do not duplicate those. The questions help the next presenter judge what a particular age group is interested in. They also remind me how much there is still to learn.

The least romantic part of the job is the timetable. A delayed van can affect three schools, not just the first one. We leave time for packing and driving, and I check the timetable before agreeing to any change in a visit's length.

I once thought of the assistant as a spare presenter. Now I prefer the word conductor: someone who helps all the separate parts happen at the right moment. You may never speak about a star yourself, but you still help make the whole experience possible.
