### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Kestrel Grove Association — Grove Crew Leader

The fictional Kestrel Grove Association is recruiting leaders for a youth conservation crew. Its spring and summer placement gives high-school students experience of working on a shared outdoor site. Site specialists decide which areas can be used; leaders help students carry out the agreed tasks.

#### Key duties

A leader supervises 10–15 high-school students, teaches clear recording habits and supports teamwork during sign maintenance and habitat observation. Keeping the attendance list, checking borrowed tools and communicating with site specialists are regular duties. The leader remains part of the working crew.

#### Schedule

The placement has two phases: spring (March–May 2027), Tuesday to Saturday; summer (June–August 2027), Monday to Friday. Leaders work 40 hours per week in either phase. The change of season changes the regular working days, not the weekly hours.

#### Location

Kestrel Grove Field Centre, outside the fictional town of Norwick.

#### Qualifications

Applicants must be at least 21 on the first working day and hold a current driving licence held for at least 3 years; exactly three years satisfies that minimum. Applicants must be able to lift 40 pounds and work outdoors. Current CPR and outdoor first-aid certificates are required before field duties; training to obtain them is available before the placement. Previous conservation work and project leadership are preferred, not compulsory.

#### Compensation

$750 per week, paid every 2 weeks. The payment for two ordinary weeks is $1500 before deductions. The placement includes staff training; this is a fictional notice, not an actual offer of employment.

#### Work challenges

Activities involve uneven paths, changing temperatures and occasional contact with insects or irritating plants. A field pack may have to be carried for several miles. Leaders need to change an activity when conditions require it and communicate respectfully with students and staff. Most sessions are practical outdoor work rather than classroom lectures.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

At a fictional repair afternoon in Norwick, a schoolbag lay open beside a tray of zips and buckles. Its owner expected a volunteer to take it away and return it repaired. Instead, the volunteer invited the owner to describe when the zip caught and asked a younger helper to record the result of a short test. The repair became a shared investigation rather than an invisible service.

The Norwick Patch Group started when a local noticeboard asked residents to bring both worn objects and skills they could share. The first meeting occupied one table. Now the group offers fabric repairs, a small tool desk and short demonstrations led by residents. Nobody has to buy a replacement object to attend. The service is free, with optional donations helping to cover materials and room costs.

Mina began volunteering after leaving a job with regular contact with customers. She missed those everyday conversations. At the repair table, she found she could explain a familiar method while hearing about the different ways people used their belongings. “I look forward to recognising people at the next meeting,” she says. This is her account of renewed contact, not a measured claim that volunteering changes everybody's mental or physical health.

The group is **resourceful** when a repair cannot be completed immediately. After a visitor brought a bag with a missing part, members used an unused sample to demonstrate the fitting and drew a simple diagram to take away. They did not pretend the sample was a permanent replacement. Finding a workable way to help mattered more than appearing to finish every job. They also keep a list of local specialists for tasks beyond the volunteers' experience.

Some visitors return with an object; others return to show how they used a suggestion. The organisers welcome both. A teenager who once wrote labels now leads a short session with an older member beside her. What the group passes on is more than a technique for fixing a zip: people learn whom they can ask, what they can contribute and when an uncertain repair needs another pair of eyes.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

An equal number of household tasks need not produce an equal sense of fairness. One task may involve noticing supplies, planning a sequence and finishing it; another may become visible only when someone points it out. A fictional survey asks whether couples feel differently about shared responsibility for each chore and separate ownership of different chores. It examines that distinction rather than treating a task count as a complete description of domestic work.

Researchers compared two groups of 100 couples. In one, partners shared responsibility across their regular chores; in the other, they assigned particular chores to particular partners. Both groups reported roughly equal personal shares of their household's total weekly work. 50 couples in the first group described the division as fair, compared with 25 in the second. The larger proportion did not come from a controlled change imposed by the researchers. It came from people reporting arrangements they already used.

Shared responsibility might encourage conversations about the parts of a job that otherwise remain unseen. But an existing habit of cooperation might also lead couples to share chores in the first place. The report treats these as different possible directions of explanation. The survey cannot demonstrate that changing the chore arrangement would produce the reported difference for a particular couple.

There may also be a “grass is greener” effect. A person who never organises the laundry may see only a neat pile of clothes and imagine a simpler job than clearing the kitchen. Sorting, checking labels and remembering a collection time can disappear from that view. A researcher not involved in the fictional survey comments, “Finishing a job is only the visible end of the work it involves.” Doing an unfamiliar chore could make its hidden demands easier to appreciate, although that possible mechanism was not separately tested here.

The findings are useful as a starting point for examining how fairness is understood. They compare reports under two arrangements and consider explanations that another study could test. They do not show that a numerical balance of hours is worthless or that there is one scientifically settled household routine for everyone.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A six-fingered hand in a fictional robotics laboratory can leave its mechanical arm, cross a table and bring back a small object. The team designed it to test how one device might combine grasping with independent movement. It has been demonstrated with light objects on a prepared surface. Those demonstrations are an initial design study, not a record of a device working reliably in factories or as part of a human body.

“A hand-shaped machine need not inherit all the restrictions of a human hand,” the designer explains. The fingers bend towards either side of the palm, rather than closing in only one familiar direction. This gives the hand two useful gripping surfaces. The team is exploring the consequences of that difference, not claiming that an unfamiliar shape automatically improves every task.

The design search used a genetic algorithm: simulated combinations were compared on a set of tasks, and stronger candidates supplied features for the next round. The team then built versions to test physically. A shape that looked balanced in simulation could still perform poorly when a joint behaved differently on the table. Design search and physical checking therefore had separate roles in the process.

With the hand connected to the arm, two light objects could be secured on opposite sides of the palm. When disconnected, it could use four fingers to support movement and two to hold an object. For a lighter load it could instead walk on five fingers and reserve one for carrying. Changing the roles did not increase the total beyond six. The team also checked the connection before allowing the arm to lift a returned object, rather than treating arrival beside the arm as successful attachment.

The researchers see a possible use in checking narrow spaces around equipment. A version controlled by a person might also be considered for an assistive hand, but how a user would command and interpret these movements remains unresolved. The report distinguishes what the prototype did from what later designs might do. Its interest lies in expanding a design question while keeping the need for further tests in view.
