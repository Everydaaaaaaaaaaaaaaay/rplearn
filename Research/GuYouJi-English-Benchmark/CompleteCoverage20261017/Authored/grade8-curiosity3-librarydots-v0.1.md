# 初二六段三题四选：读书卡上的圆点

## 学生题面

独立选做阅读：阅读全部六段和五处词语释义，回答3题；每题从A、B、C、D中选出最合适的一项，每题2分，共6分。故事事例为虚构，请依据本文作者的观点和具体语境作答。

It is easy to think that curiosity belongs mainly to people who study unusual subjects. Yet a question can begin with a mark on a notice or a word used in a familiar conversation. We do not have to travel somewhere new to meet an unfamiliar idea. Sometimes we only need to see something close at hand through another person’s eyes.

For this writer, curiosity begins when a clue (线索) makes us aware of a gap (差距) in our understanding. We see that our present explanation does not account for a detail, and we want to investigate. The detail may be small, and we may struggle to put the question into words. That does not make the uncertainty useless. It gives us a place from which to start looking, before we have found an answer or decided exactly where the search will lead.

Consider a fictional visit to our school reading club. I was sorting its book cards when an older member asked me to turn over (翻转卡片) one with a blue dot. I had assumed the dot marked a type of story. On the back, however, we found a note showing which club meeting would discuss that book. The member explained that blue meant the second meeting in this club’s own system. I asked about the other colours, and we compared several cards with the timetable. What I had treated as decoration turned out to connect the cards with a plan.

This was a small local arrangement, not a code used by every library. Still, it changed the way I looked at those cards. Before the conversation, I had not realised that my guess about the dots was only a guess. Once another person drew attention to the back of a card, I had a question I could examine. I learned something by checking a detail that had seemed too familiar to deserve attention.

Other people’s experiences can help us notice where our own explanations are incomplete. We can pause (暂停) and ask what someone means instead of pretending that a passing comment is already clear. We should also ask where an explanation comes from. In the club story, comparing the cards with the timetable mattered as much as listening to the older member. Being open to another view does not require accepting it without checking; it gives us another idea to investigate.

We can approach (处理) a familiar situation by noticing an unexplained detail, asking a question and testing an idea against relevant material. We may discover a useful connection, or realise that we need to ask a different question. Either can move our thinking forward. The writer values that process more than looking knowledgeable in every conversation. A familiar place can offer another learning opportunity when we are willing to examine what we have only assumed.
