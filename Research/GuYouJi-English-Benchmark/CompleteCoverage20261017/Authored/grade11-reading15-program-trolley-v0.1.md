### A组（第1—3题）

The fictional Citywheel Museum offers four ways for families to explore movement, making and everyday life. All four programmes are free, but every family must reserve a place online before taking part. Booking confirms one session rather than a place on a continuing course. The advertised age group describes the programme’s intended audience, so check the description as well as the title before reserving.

#### A1

Routes and Stories
These sessions welcome visitors of all ages on selected public-holiday weekends. A guide uses objects from the transport collection to begin conversations about ordinary journeys. Families can make a simple route model and compare their ideas with other groups. Topics change between sessions; attending an earlier event is not a requirement for joining a later one.

#### A2

Tiny Tracks
A programme specifically designed for families with children aged 0–5, Tiny Tracks combines a short picture-book introduction with playful exploration of objects and materials. Children take part with an accompanying adult. Once admitted to the reserved session, a family can move between activities at its own pace. This flexibility concerns the activities, not whether a booking is required.

#### A3

City Pieces
Once each month, an educator leads this programme for families with children aged 3–8. Participants compare several collection objects connected to that month’s theme, then build or draw together. A visiting designer sometimes demonstrates a technique. This session is held in the museum studio, and adults are expected to join in rather than leave children there.

#### A4

Window Workshop
An online gathering for families joining from home, Window Workshop takes place once each month. Museum educators suggest a way to explore a collection theme using materials commonly found at home. There is no scheduled visiting designer. Families receive a preparation note after booking and can choose how to adapt the activity to their available space.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

At the fictional Berrin Library, regular borrower Tomas usually collected reserved books from a high counter. One week he arrived with his arm in a sling and discovered that managing a heavy bag while reaching for the books was awkward. He asked assistant Nila to set them down while he found his card. What happened next began with a question rather than a guess.

Nila did not assume that Tomas wanted someone else to choose or carry everything for him. She asked where he would like the books placed. He pointed to an empty trolley beside the counter. With the books there, he could check the titles and arrange his bag without balancing the whole pile against his body. He thanked her for giving him time instead of completing the transaction over his head.

The trolley had previously been kept behind the desk for staff returns. Nila asked the supervisor whether one could remain in front during busy periods. Before changing the arrangement, she tried two positions with Tomas so that it would not block the path or prevent someone reaching the counter. His suggestion was to keep it beside the lower end, where a visitor could ask to use it.

The following weeks showed that the change was useful to other borrowers too: one was sorting books for several family members, and another needed to put down a bulky bag. Staff still asked before moving anyone’s belongings. A convenient surface did not mean that all visitors wanted the same help, or that the library knew why each person found it useful.

Tomas later wrote a short message thanking Nila. “I asked for somewhere to put the books, and she listened to the somewhere,” he said. Nila describes the trolley as ordinary equipment finally being used from a visitor’s point of view. The library has not solved every access problem, but it has changed a routine by paying attention to a specific request. That, for her, is a practical part of welcoming people rather than an exceptional act of rescue.

### C组（第8—11题）

#### C1

When two teams report different completion times for the same kind of task, it is tempting to decide immediately that one works faster. A fictional repair centre discovered why that conclusion can be premature. Its desk team and workshop team both used the heading “time to completion”, but the two clocks did not begin at the same event.

The desk team measured from the moment a customer handed in an object until it was collected. Its figure included waiting for a part and waiting for the customer to return. The workshop team measured only the time between starting the repair and finishing the work. That shorter period answered a useful question about active work, but it was not a measurement of the customer’s entire wait.

The distinction emerged at a planning meeting. A manager compared the totals and asked why the desk was so slow. Desk staff pointed out that they did not perform the repairs whose timing appeared in the workshop column. Nor could they control when a customer came back. The numbers had been gathered carefully, yet their shared heading invited a comparison that the definitions did not support.

For that purpose, the measures were **incompatible**: they could not be treated as equivalent descriptions of the same interval. The centre did not discard either record. It renamed the columns, wrote a start and end event beside each one, and added separate waiting stages. A reader could then ask about active repair time or about overall service time without confusing the two.

The example is about the meaning of a measure, not an argument against comparing performance. A fair comparison requires a shared definition or a clear account of the difference. Counting more precisely does not by itself repair a mismatch in what is being counted. Before asking which number is better, it is worth asking what work, delay or experience each number actually represents.

### D组（第12—15题）

#### D1

A fictional packing workshop has tested whether workers using a planning tool should be allowed to revise its instructions. The tool assigned small items to boxes, taking account of size but sometimes failing to recognise a newly added instruction to keep two items apart. The study compared one task under three conditions, not the general merits of all warehouse software.

Thirty-six adult volunteers were randomly allocated to equal groups. Everyone received the same order lists, item descriptions and separation notices. In the human-only condition, participants made a packing plan without the tool. In the tool-only condition, they followed its proposed plans without revision. In the reviewed-tool condition, they could examine and change a proposal before completing each order. All groups had equal practice and the same working period.

The reviewed-tool group completed the largest number of correct orders on average. The tool reduced some routine arranging, while human reviewers noticed separation notices that its stored template had missed. The human-only group often produced valid plans but completed fewer orders in the time. The tool-only group worked quickly, but some completed boxes failed the separation requirement. The researchers counted correct orders rather than treating the fastest completion as automatically best.

The comparison suggests that a particular combination of routine organisation and informed checking can be useful. It does not establish a permanent advantage for every mixed system. Better data in a later tool, different orders or inexperienced reviewers could change the outcome. The study also did not measure fatigue over a full working week.

Project lead Sora Pell says that future training should help reviewers understand why they accept or change a suggestion. A worker who clicks approval without reading the notice is not performing the role tested here. Before recommending workplace use, the team wants trials with more varied tasks and tools. It describes the result as a question about designing the division of work, rather than a reason to remove people from the process.
