### A组（第1—3题）

#### A1

Maple Neighbour Centre

Would you like to help adults with learning disabilities take part in a shared reading room? Our centre is looking for volunteers who can make room for other people’s choices. Applicants of different ages and backgrounds are welcome. We will explain the room’s arrangements before the first duty period.

#### Position

Reading-room Companion (No pay; free meals during each duty period).

#### Place

Westbridge

#### Hours

Part time. Two afternoons a week, with dates agreed before a placement begins.

#### Role

You will help visitors choose reading materials, learn simple ways of organising their books and find the support they ask for. You will also explain the room’s rules and help visitors understand their rights and keep their belongings safe. Our first concern is that each visitor knows that their opinions matter and that they are valued.

#### Skills and Experience Required

Applicants must be honest and patient, listen carefully, hold a current driving licence, and communicate fluently in both written and spoken English. These are essential requirements because companions may need to collect materials by car and discuss arrangements with visitors and staff. Previous experience supporting people in a similar community setting is preferred, but is not an essential requirement.

### B组（第4—7题）

#### B1

Nia was carrying a folder of scene cues towards the small theatre, thinking about the poem she would read after the puppet show. She pictured the last line receiving warm applause. At the entrance, a helper told her that two folders had been moved during cleaning. Still absorbed in the poem, Nia nodded without checking which one she was holding.

Five minutes before the show, she opened the folder and found last month’s timetable instead of the scene cues. She was particularly worried because she was the only person backstage who knew exactly when each recorded sound should begin. The performers knew their own movements, but they depended on her signals for the sound changes. The audience was already taking its seats.

Her first thought was to print the cues again. At the office, however, the printer had run out of paper, and the person with the cupboard key had gone to welcome guests. Nia looked at the empty tray, then remembered her drama teacher’s advice: when the full plan is unavailable, begin with the parts you know and make them visible.

She returned to the stage and took four blank cards from the art box. On each card she wrote the opening words of a scene and a simple sound instruction. Rather than trying to reproduce the whole script, she checked these four signals with the performers. They agreed to pause briefly at each change so that she could hold up the right card. All the information they needed for the sound changes was now in front of them.

The performance began a minute later than planned, but the sound followed the agreed signals. After the show, the helper found the missing folder under a pile of posters. Nia thanked him and put a clear label on its cover. When her own poem was announced, she smiled: she could return to thinking about the last line, now that the scene-cue problem had been solved.

### C组（第8—10题）

#### C1

A small board beside the community postbox offers blank postcards **without a catch**. The sign asks people to leave a usable card if they have a spare one and to take one if they need it. There is no fee, registration form or requirement to leave something before taking a card. The idea grew from a resident’s forgotten birthday message: she had a pen and a stamp, but no card to write on.

The organisers expected the first bundle to disappear quickly. It did, but several residents soon added cards of their own. Some were plain; others carried drawings made for the project. A bus driver who had left a bundle the previous week later stopped to take one for a neighbour. He laughed that he had not expected to use the board so soon after adding to it. The organiser recorded both visits in the report.

Not everything went smoothly. A few cards became wet before a cover was fitted, and one pile contained used cards with no writing space left. Helpers moved those aside and put a clearer explanation next to the board. They did not begin checking people’s income or judging need by clothing.

The organisers say the project is intended to make offering and accepting a small favour feel ordinary. It is not a shop promotion or a collection for a named charity. They hope residents will notice what someone nearby might need and feel equally comfortable receiving help themselves.

### D组（第11—14题）

#### D1

When short instructions can be typed, stored and printed in seconds, there are fewer occasions to write them all by hand. Some people therefore ask whether handwriting practice has much purpose left. A fictional school research team examined a narrower question: what can students learn from composing words themselves, even when digital tools are available?

The team did not begin by treating a pen as automatically better than a keyboard. Its report asks teachers to consider how a task is organised and what students actually do. Copying a neat sentence repeatedly is different from deciding how to explain a route to another person. The tool and the thinking required by the task should not be confused.

In one activity, thirty-two students wrote short route instructions once by hand and once on a keyboard. The order was changed between groups. The handwritten versions included more complete directions in this particular activity, while the typed versions were easier for partners to edit. The team recorded these different results instead of turning them into a claim that one tool always produces better writing.

A second activity examined spelling knowledge. Students who could explain the difference between similar-looking words were more successful at choosing the word that matched their own intended message. A checking program could offer alternatives, but it could not decide what a student meant to say. Knowing word forms was therefore connected with expressing an idea accurately, rather than merely making a page look tidy.

The report describes preparing a word in the **mind’s eye** before putting it on paper or a screen. A writer begins with an idea, brings a possible word form into view mentally and then turns it into written symbols. No separate physical display is involved: the writer can prepare the form while looking away from a blank page. This mental preparation may also help a writer notice a form that does not fit the intended word.

The team concludes that teaching word formation and giving students opportunities to compose by hand can still be useful alongside digital editing. Students need enough spelling knowledge to judge the alternatives a checker presents. These classroom activities are limited examples in a fictional account, not measured evidence that handwriting guarantees better learning in every setting.
