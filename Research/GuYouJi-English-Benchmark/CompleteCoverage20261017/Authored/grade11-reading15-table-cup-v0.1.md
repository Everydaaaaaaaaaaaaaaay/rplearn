### A组（第1—5题）

#### A1

Pellwick Learning Network: Teaching-Material Requests

A member of Pellwick College may request a teaching kit from another centre when the college’s own kit is unavailable. Digital copies of approved activity worksheets can also be requested. This fictional service supplies materials for study and teaching; it does not enrol users in the centres’ courses.

Who may apply
Current Pellwick students and employees are eligible, as are retired Pellwick instructors whose membership has been confirmed. A former student’s old library card is not enough. Guests attending a public lecture must ask their host to explain other available services.

Making a request
Give the kit’s catalogue number or the worksheet’s full title on the electronic form. Accurate details reduce delays caused by requests for clarification. A worksheet usually becomes available within one working day. A physical kit normally takes four to eight working days, and a kit already reserved elsewhere may take longer.

Receiving and returning material
A notification is sent to your registered email address. Collect a kit from the college office you chose on the form; open a worksheet through your account using the email link. A kit’s due date is attached to its case. If the supplying centre needs it sooner, a recall email gives a revised return date, which replaces the date on the case. Kits cannot be renewed, even if no other Pellwick user is waiting.

Fees per request
The supplying centre’s category determines the fee, regardless of your collection office. All figures are fictional credits.

| Supplying centre | Kit loan (credits) | Worksheet copy (credits) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Community Centres | 14 | 6 |
| School Centres | 22 | 9 |
| Visiting Partner Centres | 30 | 12 |

The fee is for the requested material only. It is not a course fee, and ordering a worksheet does not reserve the matching physical kit.

### B组（第6—10题）

#### B1

A second cup waits beside the kettle on Saturday mornings, even though my daughter now lives across town and does not visit every week. Friends sometimes ask why I set it out before I know whether she is coming. I used to say that it saved a minute later. Now I can admit that saving time has little to do with it. The cup is an invitation I can make without demanding an answer.

When she first started an apprenticeship, she often called at our flat after the early bus. She would be carrying a bag of fabric samples, tired before her working day had properly begun. Making tea was a practical thing I could do while she checked the address of a delivery or searched for a missing note. We talked because we happened to be standing in the same small kitchen, not because I had scheduled a discussion about her future.

I still want to **keep a door open**, though I no longer expect to hear every detail of her week. She is learning a trade I do not understand particularly well. If each visit becomes an interview about her progress, I suspect she will begin to feel she needs a polished report before calling round. The spare cup reminds me that being welcome should not depend on having good news, or indeed on having much news at all.

One morning she arrived after a difficult meeting with her supervisor. I asked a question too quickly and began suggesting what she should have said. She put down the samples and told me, gently but clearly, that she had already spent an hour explaining herself. For a while we drank in silence. Then she showed me a piece of cloth she liked. That small change of subject was not an avoidance of everything important. It was a way of being together that did not require another explanation.

I cannot claim that tea makes an unhappy morning disappear. Sometimes she leaves as worried as she arrived, and sometimes I still confuse helping with offering a solution. But I am learning that attention can take the form of waiting as well as asking. We have had useful conversations while washing cups, after we had stopped trying to produce one.

When she sends a message saying she cannot come, I put the second cup back on the shelf. I do not send her a photograph of it or ask why she is staying away. An invitation would become a burden if I made her responsible for my disappointment. I hope the habit can remain flexible: a familiar place to pause when she wants it, and no debt to repay when she does not.

### C组（第11—15题）

#### C1

Silence in a conversation is often treated as something to repair. In the fictional adult-learning centre at Norridge, however, tutors noticed that some drawing partners worked together quietly and reported feeling comfortable. The observation prompted a question: does every pause mean that a conversation has failed, or can a shared activity give silence a different meaning?

Tutor Ren Hale began by collecting short accounts from workshop members. New partners sometimes interpreted a pause as a sign that the other person was bored. Partners who had established a way of working together more often described the same kind of pause as time for looking closely. Hale suggested that a common task can provide an understood focus, reducing uncertainty about what the other person is doing. This was an explanation to investigate, not a rule that familiar people never feel awkward.

An invented follow-up study involved twenty-four pairs of adults completing a drawing task. Half the pairs received a shared instruction to inspect the drawing quietly for a minute before discussing it. The others received no such instruction. Afterwards, each participant rated how comfortable the pause had felt. The instructed pairs gave higher comfort ratings on average. The task was brief, and the researchers did not measure whether their drawings were better.

Observers who watched silent clips of the activity provided a useful contrast. Without knowing which instruction a pair had received, observers could not reliably identify the pairs reporting the most comfort. A quiet scene seen from outside was therefore not a dependable guide to how the people in it felt. This finding does not mean that people’s own reports are complete measures of every aspect of an interaction; it shows why the two perspectives were kept separate.

Hale calls an agreed pause **a space rather than a hole**. A hole suggests that something necessary is missing. A space can have a purpose: both people know that they are examining the same problem and will speak again when they have something to contribute. The distinction depends on shared understanding. Silence imposed by exclusion, fear or an ignored question would be a different case, and the centre’s study offers no reason to dismiss it.

The project has encouraged tutors to explain the purpose of quiet periods instead of simply insisting that groups talk more. It has not produced a treatment for social anxiety or a universal guide to conversation. One participant’s comment captures the narrower point: “We were not out of things to say; we were giving the drawing time.” The silence, for that pair, belonged to the activity rather than interrupting it.
