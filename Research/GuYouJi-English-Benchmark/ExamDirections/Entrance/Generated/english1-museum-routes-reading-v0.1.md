A municipal museum is introducing an app that recommends a route through its galleries. Visitors select their interests, and the app directs them towards relevant displays. Someone interested in painting can avoid industrial machinery; a visitor fascinated by transport can head straight for the railway collection. For people with limited time, this is a welcome convenience. Yet the museum's curators wonder whether a more efficient visit is necessarily a richer one.

The existing galleries were arranged to bring apparently unrelated objects into conversation. A celebrated portrait hangs near a collection of household tools, inviting visitors to consider both artistic achievement and ordinary work. Such encounters do not always answer a question visitors brought with them. Sometimes they produce a question that visitors had never thought to ask.

Personal recommendations operate differently. They begin with preferences that users can already name and tend to supply more of the same. This can reduce the friction of finding a particular object. But some friction has value: passing an unfamiliar display may interrupt a settled assumption. Eliminating every diversion risks turning a public collection into a mirror of individual expectations.

An initial review found that app users spent more time at the displays they had selected. That result is useful, but it cannot settle the curators' concern. The review did not record whether visitors encountered unfamiliar ideas, and the people who chose the app may have had more specific interests to begin with. Longer attention to a chosen object does not, by itself, demonstrate a broader understanding of the collection.

The curators therefore propose an alternative. The app would retain its practical search function, while each suggested route would also include an unexpected stop, briefly explained by a curator. Visitors could skip that stop, and those who preferred to explore without a phone could still follow the existing gallery arrangement. The proposal aims to add an opportunity rather than impose a uniform experience.

A museum should help people find what they seek and give them a chance to discover what they did not know they wanted. Technology can assist both purposes. Its success should be judged by the opportunities it opens as well as the minutes it saves.

   A. examine every object in the museum.

   A. An arrangement can encourage unplanned connections.
   B. Artistic achievement should be ranked above ordinary work.
   C. Visitors should learn to name their preferences more precisely.
   D. Objects are best understood through their monetary value.
3. The word “friction” in the third paragraph refers mainly to
   A. conflict between visitors and museum staff.
   B. damage caused by moving historical objects.
   C. technical errors that prevent the app from working.
   D. effort or interruption during the search for displays.
4. The author treats the initial review as insufficient because it
   A. proves that visitors dislike unfamiliar objects.
   B. leaves broader learning and differences between users unexamined.
   C. measures visits only by the number of galleries entered.
   D. establishes that personalised routes shorten all visits.
5. The proposed unexpected stop is intended to
   A. require everyone to follow an identical route.
   B. remove the app's function of searching for particular objects.

   D. restrict access for people who arrive without a phone.
