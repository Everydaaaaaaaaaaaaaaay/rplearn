# 长篇段落信息匹配：cet6-editorial-change-records-matching

## 学生题面

阅读整篇文章，为十个陈述选择信息所在段落A—O。同一段可以重复使用，也可不用；不是每项只用一次。

本文是虚构案例；不代表真实机构研究或实测结论。

### A

A link to a research article once appeared to identify a finished object: a paper with a title, a date, and a stable set of pages. The same address may now lead to a corrected table, an added statement, or a notice that the paper should no longer be relied upon. At the fictional journal Meridian Studies, editors reviewing this problem found that access to the latest text was only part of their obligation. Readers also needed to understand which text earlier readers had encountered and what had changed between those encounters.

### B

The editors began by distinguishing correction from withdrawal. A correction could repair an inaccurate label while leaving the reported argument intact. A withdrawal notice, by contrast, warned readers against treating the article as dependable evidence. Neither category could be determined by counting how many characters had been changed. Replacing one numerical sign might affect an entire conclusion, while replacing a misspelled institutional name might affect none. Meridian therefore required the responsible editor to describe the significance of the change before choosing the public notice, rather than letting the publishing software choose a category from the size of an edit.

### C

Preserving an earlier version did not mean recommending it. The journal retained the original file in its archive, but anyone opening it saw a prominent link to the current notice. The corrected article, in turn, pointed back to the earlier version. This arrangement supported historical inspection without making the two files look equally suitable for present use. They made the relationship visible within the journal's own service, which was a narrower and more defensible promise.

### D

Timing presented a separate problem. An author could report an error on Monday, an editor could approve a correction on Wednesday, and the revised file could become available on Friday. Recording only Friday concealed the interval during which the journal knew about the problem. Recording only Monday implied that readers could already see the correction. The journal kept all three dates and described the event attached to each. This was not intended to suggest that approval should always take two days; it prevented one date from being asked to represent three different stages.

### E

Some changes originated outside the author team. Meridian accepted such reports through a public form and a private email address. However, receiving a report did not automatically establish that the complaint was correct. The editors logged its arrival, requested the relevant evidence, and kept the allegation separate from their eventual decision. The system needed to acknowledge that someone had raised a question without presenting an unexamined accusation as a finding.

### F

The authority to change a published article remained with designated editors. Production staff could repair a broken download link or restore a missing image file, but they could not silently alter a reported result while doing so. The technician could flag it and preserve the evidence; an editor then had to decide whether the content required correction. Technical ability to replace a file was deliberately separated from responsibility for approving a change to the scholarly record.

### G

The journal initially proposed a single status label for each article. Its research librarian objected that this would combine several independent questions. An article might have complete files, a published correction, unresolved criticism, and no independent replication. Marking it simply “checked” would leave readers unsure which question had been checked. The revised record used separate fields for file availability, editorial action, and the existence of further investigations. These fields did not calculate a final reliability score. They prevented a positive finding in one limited area from being mistaken for an endorsement of everything else.

### H

Corrections also had to reach readers who did not return to the article's webpage. Libraries, citation services, and subject databases often stored their own descriptive records. Meridian sent structured notices to those services and recorded whether they acknowledged receipt. An acknowledgement proved that a message had reached a service, not that every downstream copy had already changed. The journal avoided reporting universal propagation from a handful of successful receipts. Its public description identified the services contacted and left the remaining distribution of copies as an acknowledged limit.

### I

A corrected figure could create new obstacles if only the visual file was replaced. A reader using a screen reader might still encounter the old description, and a researcher copying values from the accompanying table might obtain the old numbers. The production checklist therefore bound the figure, its description, and any table presenting the same values into one correction package. Staff compared the package before publication. They were checking whether the representations agreed, rather than treating the presence of a newly uploaded image as sufficient evidence that the correction was complete.

### J

The editors resisted another convenient measure: the number of corrections issued by each author. That count could not distinguish careless work from a willingness to report and repair mistakes promptly. Meridian did not claim that correction counts had no possible research use. It declined to turn them directly into a ranking of individual researchers. Assessing such a measure would require an explicitly defined question and additional evidence, rather than an assumption that fewer notices always indicated better work.

### K

Readers sometimes asked whether a correction invalidated articles that had cited the original paper. The answer depended on what those later articles had used. A study that relied on an altered measurement might need reconsideration; a historical discussion citing only the original title might not. The journal could list known citing works and describe the corrected element, but it could not infer the effect on every later argument from a citation link alone. A connection between documents identified a place to investigate, not an automatic verdict about the connected document.

### L

For the journal's internal review, each proposed procedure was tried against deliberately constructed examples. One example changed a figure but left its description untouched. Another altered a conclusion while retaining an innocent-looking file label. The team asked whether the process detected the inconsistency and stopped publication. Passing these exercises demonstrated behaviour under those particular conditions. It did not establish that all possible editorial failures had been anticipated. 

### M

Maintaining this history did not require copying the entire archive every time one file changed. Meridian stored content under identifiers derived from the file bytes and recorded which article version referred to each object. Identical objects could be shared by several records, while distinct older objects remained recoverable. This reduced unnecessary duplication without declaring history disposable. The system's practical test was a successful reconstruction of a selected earlier version, rather than a large directory size or an impressive count of backup jobs that had never been restored.

### N

No technical record resolved the question of permission. An article could be easy to download and still contain an image licensed only for a particular publication. A correction could introduce a replacement figure whose reuse conditions differed from those of the original. Meridian recorded these conditions alongside the relevant versions instead of treating public accessibility as a general licence. The editorial and technical checks could confirm what had been published and how it had changed; they could not grant rights that the journal itself did not hold.

### O

After a year, the editors described their service as a record of accountable changes, not a machine that certified truth. The system made editorial actions more inspectable and reduced several known ways of confusing versions. It still depended on expert judgment, cooperation from external services, and readers attending to the notices provided. That qualified description was less marketable than promising a permanently reliable publication. It was also more useful: it identified the work the journal could demonstrate, while leaving the validity of particular scientific claims open to the wider process of inquiry.

### 3：C

C双向引用及旧版警示并存；M内容寻址存储未描述此读者警示。

### 4：D

D分别报错、审批、公开三时点；其他段未讨论日期。

### 5：L

L人为错误例与仅证明这些条件下行为；G分字段不当测试通过。

### 6：N

N区分访问与授权且新版图的权利可不同；其他段不授版权。

### 7：B

B对改单字数与结论影响分开；O总述可核范围非具体通知分类。

### 8：J

J指更正数不能直接评价研究者；K是引用链对后来论证的影响。

### 9：I

I图、文字替代及数据表成包；H对外分发非表示形式一致。

### 10：M
