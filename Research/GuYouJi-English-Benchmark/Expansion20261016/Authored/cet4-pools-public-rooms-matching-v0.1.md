# 长篇段落信息匹配：cet4-pools-public-rooms-matching

## 学生题面

阅读整篇文章，为十个陈述选择信息所在段落A—K。同一段可以重复使用，也可不用；不是每项只用一次。

本文是虚构案例；不代表真实机构研究或实测结论。

### A

The town of Bellmere has three swimming pools, but its newest public survey began with a question that had little to do with swimming: where could someone wait without buying anything? The question came from a parent who regularly accompanied her teenage son to training. She could enter the pool building without a ticket, yet she felt that sitting in its entrance hall required an explanation. The council used her comment to begin examining how the buildings served people who never entered the water. These interviews provide the setting for the fictional case described here.

### B

The council's first records offered a misleadingly simple picture. An electronic gate counted everyone who passed from the changing rooms to the pool. It did not count grandparents sitting upstairs, students meeting after lessons, or people asking the receptionist for local information. Managers had treated gate totals as the number of people using each building. The survey team separated these figures rather than adding an estimated percentage to them. From that point, its reports used two headings: swimmers recorded at the gate and other visitors observed in public rooms. Neither total could stand in for the other.

### C

One pool had a cafe overlooking the water, while another had only a row of seats near the entrance. Interviewers initially assumed that the cafe offered the better waiting area. Several parents disagreed. They explained that they could afford an occasional drink but did not want to make a purchase every time their children trained. Staff therefore placed a small notice at the cafe entrance saying that using its tables did not require an order. The change addressed uncertainty about permission, rather than the price of coffee or the comfort of the chairs.

### D

Communication created a different obstacle for people attending their first swimming lesson. Their booking email listed the lesson time but did not explain that changing took place before it. Some arrived at the stated time still wearing outdoor clothes, then felt embarrassed about joining late. The booking team added a short explanation of what the start time meant. It also offered a printable arrival checklist. This did not move lessons earlier or lengthen the swimming period; it supplied information that experienced visitors had previously taken for granted.

### E

Quiet space could not be provided simply by lowering every sound in the building. Swimming instruction required clear calls, and the water area naturally produced echoes. At the central pool, staff instead identified a room away from the changing-room corridor. They kept its door closed and placed a sign outside explaining its purpose. Visitors could wait there before returning to a busy public area. The room was available throughout normal opening hours, rather than only during a special quiet session, and anyone needing a break could use it without describing a medical condition.

### F

The survey also challenged the belief that an empty building at midday was an unsuccessful one. At one pool, an adult beginner group deliberately used a less crowded period. Its members valued being able to practise without a large audience. Filling every remaining lane with another activity would have increased the head count while reducing that benefit. The manager kept the beginner period and described its purpose in the monthly report. A smaller attendance figure now had an explanation, making it harder to confuse limited numbers with an unwanted service.

### G

Not every request could be accepted. A local club wanted permanent storage beside the entrance for its equipment. Reception staff supported the club's work but opposed that location because large boxes would narrow the accessible route to the lift. The club and manager eventually agreed on a locked cupboard farther inside the building. The decision was recorded as a change of location, not a refusal to support the club. Keeping this distinction in the meeting notes mattered when volunteers later asked why their original proposal had disappeared.

### H

Collecting comments required more than putting a questionnaire on a website. The council left short paper cards at reception and arranged a weekly telephone period for people who preferred speaking. Staff entered the comments into the same system but kept a record of how each had arrived. They did not describe the online responses as the views of the whole town. Nor did they assume that every paper response came from an older person. The different routes were ways of making participation possible, not reliable labels for the people using them.

### I

One suggestion appeared in several messages: visitors wanted a direct bus from the eastern neighbourhood to the central pool. The pool manager could report the demand and provide the bus operator with training schedules, but she could not alter public transport routes herself. The council's reply identified the transport team responsible for the decision and stated that no new service had been approved. This was less attractive than announcing that the problem had been solved. It nevertheless prevented an improvement to a pool building from being presented as control over the wider town.

### J

To see whether information was improving, receptionists kept a temporary list of repeated arrival questions. The number of questions about changing before lessons fell after the booking email was revised. That result supported a narrow conclusion: the explanation appeared to address that particular misunderstanding. It did not establish that all visitors understood every instruction, or that waiting areas were now equally welcoming. The survey team kept the question list separate from its interviews, because each could reveal a different aspect of the experience and neither replaced the other.

### K

At the final meeting, councillors asked for one overall score that would rank the three buildings. The team declined to create it. A pool could have a well-designed waiting room and still be difficult to reach; another could give excellent arrival instructions while lacking quiet space. Combining those features into a single number would hide choices about their relative importance. Instead, the report described specific changes, unresolved requests, and the people responsible for the next decisions. It treated accessibility as a set of practical relationships to examine, rather than a title awarded permanently to a building.

### 1：C

### 2：F

F明确成人初学时段观众少为价值；其他段讨论访客计量或设施，不是人数少的教学理由。

### 3：H

H同时给网上/纸卡/电话及不据渠道猜年龄；其他段没有这一参与渠道与身份推断区别。

### 4：B

B闸机只数泳区，漏掉陪同者等；A是访谈起点，未给计数口径修订。

### 5：I

I只能转交公交需求并明示未批准新线；G是建筑内部可协商的储物位置。

### 6：E

E给远离走廊房间、正常时段和无须说明病情；其余段没有该三项安排。

### 7：D

D说明需提前换衣，未改课时；J是对该修订的窄范围观察，具体原误解和新增说明在D。

### 8：K

K明确拒绝总分与隐藏权重；B分统计口径并非对特征权重的排名问题。

### 9：A

A以陪练家长的无消费等待问题引出非泳客建筑用途；C是后来具体咖啡桌政策，非调查起点。

### 10：G

G储物箱妨碍去电梯的通道，改用内侧柜；其他段未涉及这项位置协商。
