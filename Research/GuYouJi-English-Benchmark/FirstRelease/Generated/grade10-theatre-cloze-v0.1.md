During the first rehearsal, I pressed the button whenever an actor began speaking. The screen showed every line correctly, and I felt (2) ____ of my work. Then a teacher invited three students to sit at the back and watch. One of them asked why the words disappeared so (3) ____. He had not finished reading a line before I moved to the next one.

My first reaction was to say that the actors spoke too fast. But the teacher asked me to watch the play without listening to it. I soon (4) ____ the problem. I had been following the actors' voices, not the audience's reading pace.

I wanted to leave each line on screen for longer, but that created another (5) ____: two characters sometimes spoke before the previous words had gone. Simply slowing every change would not (6) ____.

Instead, I sat with the director and divided the dialogue into shorter units. We removed none of the important words, but we changed where each unit (7) ____. I marked the places in my script and practised the button changes again.

The students returned for a second test. This time, they could follow most of the conversation. Their comments were (8) ____, though: one said that a joke appeared before the actor delivered it, which spoiled the surprise. I (9) ____ that point in the script and delayed the change.

On opening night, I was still nervous. Yet I no longer tried to make the screen look impressive. I concentrated on keeping the words in (10) ____ with the performance. When the audience laughed at the same moment, I felt a quiet sense of (11) ____.

Afterwards, the director thanked me for accepting suggestions rather than (12) ____ my first design. I had started with a task that seemed to require only quick fingers. It turned out to require (13) ____ observation and repeated discussion. The best improvement came when I stopped treating feedback as a personal (14) ____ and began to see it as a way of making the play more (15) ____ to others.
