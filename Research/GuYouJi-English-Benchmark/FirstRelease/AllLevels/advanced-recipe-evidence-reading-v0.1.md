### What a recipe leaves out

Recipes promise an unusually portable form of knowledge. A cook in one place writes down a procedure, and someone elsewhere appears able to repeat it. This apparent transparency makes recipes attractive to historians: here, surely, is an everyday practice preserved in words. Yet the attraction can become a trap. Even an instruction that specifies every ingredient may leave its most important assumptions unstated. The reader may be expected to recognise a texture, anticipate the behaviour of a familiar oven, or know which household member will prepare the ingredients. Such omissions need not indicate careless writing. They may reveal what a particular audience already knew. To treat a recipe as a complete record of a meal is therefore to confuse a proposal for action with an account of everything that made the action possible. My concern is not that recipes tell us nothing, but that their apparent completeness can discourage the questions they deserve.

The physical survival of a recipe adds another layer of uncertainty. A stain beside a paragraph may suggest that a page was used near food, but it cannot establish how often that particular dish was made. A clean page, conversely, may have been read aloud while somebody else cooked. Handwritten additions are valuable, although they may belong to a later owner whose circumstances differed from those of the original reader. Reprinted editions present a related difficulty. A familiar instruction can remain unchanged while fuel, utensils and the organisation of domestic work alter around it. These are reasons to examine the history of a document, not reasons to discard it. The evidence acquires significance through its relation to other evidence; its authority does not simply increase with the number of measurements printed on the page.

Consider the contradictory accounts of a savoury pudding discussed in the second chapter. A printed recipe specifies a generous quantity of salt, a household notebook recommends much less, and a later recollection insists that the dish was never salty. It would be tempting to select whichever statement makes the most coherent story. Instead, I begin by asking what each source could establish on its own. The printed version may indicate an expectation circulated among readers; the notebook may record an experiment; the recollection may express a contrast with meals eaten elsewhere. None of these possibilities settles what went into one particular pudding. Describing those limits before combining the sources prevents an attractive narrative from becoming its own evidence. Experimental cooking can then test whether an interpretation is workable, but the success of a modern reconstruction cannot prove that a past cook followed the same procedure.

Our descriptive vocabulary also requires care. An old recipe called simple is not necessarily one that a present reader could prepare quickly. Its writer may have counted only the work performed at the final stage, taking for granted the labour of somebody who had already ground, chopped or washed the ingredients. Equally, a modern preference for precise quantities can make an instruction look defective when its intended readers needed a different kind of guidance. This does not require us to abandon present concerns about convenience or fairness. It requires us to distinguish those concerns from the criteria by which a particular instruction was originally judged. Otherwise, the historian's vocabulary quietly supplies the answer to a question that has not yet been asked. A recipe's description of itself is evidence about an audience and its expectations, rather than a timeless verdict on the effort it demands.

This book consequently moves between printed instructions, household records and accounts of meals. It does not attempt to recover a single authentic version of every dish, nor does it offer a catalogue of successful reconstructions. One especially poorly documented area is the informal conversation around a meal: who suggested a change, whose objection mattered, and how those reactions affected the next attempt. These interactions merit further investigation even when no neat sequence of revised recipes survives. Written versions remain indispensable, but they are only one part of an exchange involving people who may never have written anything down. Reading a recipe historically means following that exchange as far as the evidence permits, while keeping its missing parts visible. The familiar appearance of an instruction is a useful invitation to inquiry; it is not a guarantee that the practice behind it has been fully recorded.
