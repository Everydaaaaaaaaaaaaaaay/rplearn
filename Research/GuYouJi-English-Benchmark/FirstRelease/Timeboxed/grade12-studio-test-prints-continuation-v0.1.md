## 学生题面

阅读材料，根据内容和两个给定段首续写两段，使之构成完整短文。续写词数约150词。

On the last morning in my father's old photography studio, my brother and I disagreed about a pile of faded test prints. He wanted to finish packing before the van arrived. I wanted to look through every picture, although I could not have explained what I was hoping to find.

Our father had retired and moved to a smaller home. He had asked us to bring his cameras and a few albums, then clear the studio for its next owner. As children, we had spent many afternoons there, waiting behind the curtain while customers sat under the bright lights. I remembered the smell of paper and the soft click that came just before somebody's ordinary face became a photograph.

Most of the frames were already empty. On one wall, pale rectangles showed where portraits had hung for years. The room looked less like a place that had been loved than a shop that had been closed in a hurry. My brother said that was natural. “A wall is only a wall,” he said, lifting another box. “The pictures can come with us.”

I knew he was right, but the faded prints still bothered me. Some showed a hand, the corner of a chair, or a face turned away from the camera. None looked like the polished portraits my father displayed in the window. Why had he kept so many imperfect attempts? I put a few aside rather than throwing them into the paper bin.

My brother carried the first load downstairs and said he would take the boxes to our father's new home. He would be back before the van arrived. I stayed to pack the last cupboard. Inside, behind two empty cases, I found a flat board covered with a cloth. A note in my father's handwriting was attached to it: “Keep this with the test prints. Ask me if you don't understand.”

I called him. He sounded pleased that I had found it. “Those people chose more than the pictures you saw in the window,” he said. “Sometimes the imperfect ones mattered most. Look at what they wrote.” I turned back towards the covered board. For the first time that morning, the pile on the table seemed less like something left unfinished and more like a conversation I had not yet heard.

I lifted the cloth and found a row of carefully labelled envelopes. Inside were notes from customers, each attached to a test print. One woman had chosen the picture of a hand because it showed her father's familiar way of holding his hat. Another liked a blurred smile that the camera had almost missed. I read the names, then matched the notes to the photographs on the table. Instead of packing them immediately, I placed a few on the wall, with their stories underneath.

By the time my brother returned, the empty wall no longer looked abandoned. He stopped in the doorway and read the first note. Neither of us spoke for a moment. Then he put down the empty box and helped me photograph the small display. We would still clear the room, but we could take this record to our father and ask him about the other pictures. I packed the envelopes carefully. The studio was closing; the conversation did not have to end.
