## 学生题面

阅读短文，根据短文内容回答问题。

For a long time, I thought that good students never needed to ask questions. If I did not understand an instruction, I would wait and watch what everyone else did. Sometimes I copied the first step correctly, but I still had no idea why we were doing it. I was afraid that a simple question would make me look less clever than my classmates.

This became a real problem during a group project. We had to plan a small garden for an empty corner of the school. I was responsible for drawing the plan, but I did not understand the size written on the teacher's sheet. Instead of asking, I guessed. My drawing looked neat, yet the path was much too wide. When the group tried to add the flower beds, there was hardly any space left.

I felt embarrassed and wanted to hide the drawing. My partner Sam stopped me. “I couldn't understand that number either,” he said. “Let's ask together.” The teacher explained that the number showed the whole length, not the width of the path. Another group heard the answer and thanked us. They had been confused about the same thing.

That afternoon, I realised that asking a question could help other people as well as myself. A question did not mean I had stopped thinking. It could show exactly where my thinking needed more information. Sam and I corrected the drawing, and our group could continue.

I began to make small changes. Before each meeting, I wrote down one thing I understood and one thing I was still unsure about. I tried to say what I had already checked before asking for help. When somebody else asked a question, I listened instead of deciding that it was too easy. Often, the answer helped me notice something I had missed.

I still prefer to work things out on my own first. However, I no longer use silence to hide a problem. Our finished garden plan is on the classroom wall. When I look at it, I remember not only the flowers and paths but also the question that made the plan possible.
