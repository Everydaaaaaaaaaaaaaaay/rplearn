### Choosing a Question You Can Investigate

An interesting research question is not always a manageable one. Before a school project begins, students need to consider both what they want to learn and how they could obtain evidence. The following steps offer a way to narrow an idea without losing its purpose.

#### Start with a question that matters to you

**[1]**, so begin by writing down something you would like to understand better. If you wonder why a particular classroom stays cooler than another, your concern about comfort can provide a starting point. At this stage, you do not need a detailed plan or a promise that you will find a particular answer.

#### **[2]**

Once you have chosen a topic, specify what you would compare. For example, one student may want to compare temperatures in two rooms at the same time rather than record a vague impression of which room feels comfortable. The comparison should make clear what information would count as evidence.

#### Check whether your evidence would answer it

A method is useful only if its results are relevant to the question. **[3]**. A temperature record collected at different times, for instance, might mix a difference between rooms with a difference between mornings and afternoons. Merely collecting more numbers would not remove that uncertainty.

#### Check what you need to collect the evidence

After choosing a method, find out whether the necessary resources are available. **[4]**, ask your science teacher how the equipment can be borrowed and used safely. If a project requires permission rather than equipment, contact the person responsible before collecting the information. These are two different needs, so do not treat asking permission as a substitute for checking a device.

**[5]**. Make a brief plan showing when you will collect the information and how you will compare the results. You may discover that your first question needs to be narrower. That is a useful revision, not a reason to hide evidence that fails to support your original expectation.
