## 学生区

<!-- passage:start -->
For several months, the transport office in Bellford received complaints that evening buses were either crowded or almost empty. Some passengers blamed the small number of services. Others argued that residents simply preferred their cars. Both explanations sounded reasonable, but the office lacked information about when people actually wanted to travel.

Its first survey asked residents which departure times they would use. The most popular answer was six o'clock. Officials therefore added a bus at that time, expecting it to attract passengers from several existing services. Yet after four weeks, the new bus still carried few people. The office initially interpreted this as evidence that the survey had been unreliable.

A closer look suggested a different problem. Many respondents had chosen six because it was the nearest option to their preferred time. The questionnaire offered departures at five, six and seven, while several workplaces finished at six fifteen. Those workers could not reach the stop before the new bus left. Their answers had described a convenient-looking category rather than a journey they could actually make.

The office then invited residents to enter their finishing time and the minutes needed to reach a stop. This form took longer to complete, so fewer people responded. However, the answers showed that a departure around six forty might serve a group missed by the earlier timetable. A four-week trial of that departure attracted more passengers than the six o'clock service had, although the office could not tell whether the improvement would continue in winter.

The experience did not establish that longer questionnaires are always better. Some residents still found the new form confusing, and people without internet access were poorly represented. The office decided to keep paper forms available and review the timetable after the season changed. Its main lesson was narrower: before treating a popular survey answer as a demand for a particular service, planners should check whether the available answers reflect the choices people face in daily life.

1. What information did the transport office initially lack?
   A. How much money each resident spent on a car.
   B. Which bus company employed the most drivers.
   C. When residents actually wanted to make journeys.
   D. Why every existing evening bus was crowded.
2. Why did some workers fail to use the new six o'clock bus?
   A. Their working day ended after its departure.
   B. They had not been informed of the new service.
   C. They refused to answer the first questionnaire.
   D. Their workplace provided free transport instead.
3. What was one disadvantage of the second survey?
   A. It prevented residents from using paper forms later.
   B. It recorded departure categories instead of finishing times.
   C. It proved that the first survey's respondents were dishonest.
   D. It attracted fewer responses because completion took longer.
4. What remained uncertain after the later bus trial?
   A. Whether the original questionnaire included three times.
   B. Whether the increased use would continue during winter.
   C. Whether the office had added the six o'clock service.
   D. Whether some workplaces finished at six fifteen.
5. What lesson does the author draw from this experience?
   A. Every survey should contain as many questions as possible.
   B. Digital surveys can represent all local residents equally.
   C. Survey options should reflect people's practical choices.
   D. Popular departure times always predict successful services.
