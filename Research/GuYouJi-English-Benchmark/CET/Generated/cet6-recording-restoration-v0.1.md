## 学生区

<!-- passage:start -->
The Northbridge Sound Archive is considering whether to use a new computer tool to reduce noise in its collection of old interviews. Several recordings contain a continuous mechanical hum, and visitors often struggle to hear the speakers. Supporters of the tool regard a clearer recording as an obvious improvement: if words become easier to recognise, they argue, more people will be able to use the collection. Some critics, however, describe any computer alteration as a threat to the historical record. In their view, even apparently unwanted sounds may reveal how an interview was recorded, and removing them could leave listeners with a misleading impression of the original event.

These positions identify real concerns, but neither tells the archive what should happen to a particular recording. A test arranged by its committee illustrates the difficulty. The same software made one interview much clearer while weakening a quiet speaker's words in another. The committee did not conclude that the tool was either universally helpful or useless. Instead, it asked staff to document changes and compare each edited version with its original. The quality of a process, it suggested, should be judged by the information it preserves and makes accessible, rather than by whether it belongs to a fashionable category.

Preservation and access also need not depend on the same file. The archive proposes retaining an unchanged copy of every recording and offering edited listening copies where they help. A note would identify any changes, and visitors could request the original. This arrangement would not settle every disagreement about restoration, but it would make a crucial distinction visible: an accessible copy is an interpretation produced for a purpose, rather than a replacement for the surviving evidence. The committee also supports testing simpler methods, including adjustments to playback volume and written transcripts, since a sophisticated tool may offer little benefit when the main difficulty is unfamiliar vocabulary.

The proposed policy still has limitations. Staff cannot assume that a label will explain every alteration, and producing accurate transcripts requires time and careful checking. Nor does keeping an original guarantee that all future visitors will choose to consult it. Nevertheless, these practical questions deserve more attention than a contest between those who describe every digital change as progress and those who reject all change as damage. The archive's priority should be to identify what listeners need, examine what each method removes or adds, and explain those choices honestly. Agreement on a single preferred technology is less useful than a procedure that allows decisions to be questioned and revised.

1. What is the concern of some critics of computer alteration?
   A. Visitors will stop using all interviews in the collection.
   B. Every original recording has already become impossible to hear.
   C. Staff will replace interviews with newly invented historical stories.
   D. Removing sounds may change how listeners understand the original event.
2. What did the committee learn from its test?
   A. The same tool can affect different recordings differently.
   B. The tool improves every recording when used by experienced staff.
   C. Quiet speech should always be removed together with background noise.
   D. Edited recordings should be judged only by their software category.
3. Why would the archive keep original files alongside edited copies?
   A. To avoid checking the accuracy of any written transcripts.
   B. To let visitors compare an interpretation with the surviving evidence.
   C. To require every visitor to listen to both versions before leaving.
   D. To show that unchanged recordings are always easiest to understand.
4. Why does the committee support trying simpler methods?
   A. It has proved that computer noise reduction never preserves speech.
   B. It plans to publish all recordings without information about changes.
   C. A complex tool may fail to address the listener's main difficulty.
   D. Volume adjustments eliminate the need to keep original recordings.
5. What does the author consider most useful for the archive?
   A. A flexible procedure for examining and explaining particular choices.
   B. Agreement that every digital change represents historical progress.
   C. A rule that visitors must accept the newest available technology.
   D. A competition to identify the only method suitable for every recording.
