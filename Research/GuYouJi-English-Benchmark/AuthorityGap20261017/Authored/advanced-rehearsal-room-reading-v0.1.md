# 高级：排练室里的协作与时间

## 学生题面

阅读完整的五段虚构书序，为全部六题各选一个最佳答案。判断应依据本文的论证、语气和限定范围，不把书中虚构案例当作现实研究事实。

The concert programme has a reassuring economy: a work, a composer and a list of performers. It gives no indication of the conversations that made a particular performance possible. When I began visiting the imaginary Bell Street Orchestra for this book, I expected rehearsal to fill that gap by revealing how musicians corrected mistakes. It did, but not in the sense I had anticipated. Notes that everyone could already play occupied much of the available time. A phrase would be repeated without any detectable error having preceded it. The purpose was often to discover what the players wanted the phrase to become. My original picture of rehearsal as a workshop in which an accurate object is gradually assembled proved inadequate. Accuracy mattered, but it could not decide among several convincing ways of giving a passage shape. The difficulty was less a shortage of correct notes than the need to establish a shared intention.

During one session, a conductor asked for a lighter ending. The violins made it quieter; the cellos shortened it. Neither response was absurd, yet together they produced an ending nobody wanted. The conductor's next instruction was not more technically exact. She asked the players to imagine a question left unfinished. This time their choices converged. It would be easy to conclude that imagery is superior to technical vocabulary. In a later session the same image caused confusion, while a precise indication of duration resolved the problem. An instruction works through what particular people take it to mean at a particular moment. Its apparent elegance on the page tells us surprisingly little about that transaction. I therefore describe what followed an instruction as carefully as I record the words themselves. Otherwise, a memorable remark can become evidence of successful communication simply because it is memorable.

This attention to exchanges changes who appears to be directing the work. The conductor remains conspicuous, but a principal player may quietly propose a way of releasing a note, and the rest of a section may alter its sound before the conductor speaks. Some of these interventions are invitations; others have the force of instructions. A transcript that places every spoken sentence in chronological order can still miss the distinction, since the authority of a suggestion may depend on who makes it and who responds. My descriptions consequently follow small sequences of action rather than assigning each decision to the person whose voice was loudest. This is not a claim that the orchestra secretly runs without leadership. It is an attempt to show how formal leadership is exercised through a network of contributions that an account centred on the conductor alone would leave invisible.

The word efficient recurred whenever managers discussed rehearsal time. In their schedules, efficiency meant getting through a specified amount of music within the hours available. Players sometimes used the same word for the opposite-looking activity: spending a long time on one passage so that a shared understanding could be used elsewhere. Neither meaning automatically discredits the other. A rehearsal may produce insight and still overrun its budget; it may meet its timetable while leaving an unresolved disagreement to emerge on stage. The interesting question is which result a particular speaker is counting. Treating efficiency as an agreed measure would conceal that question beneath a familiar compliment. In this book I keep the competing uses visible, including occasions when musicians and managers found a practical compromise without arriving at a single definition. Agreement about what to do next did not always require agreement about what counted as a good use of time.

The chapters that follow move from established orchestral sections to groups assembled for a single project. The latter expose a question that the Bell Street sessions could not answer: how do players with no continuing relationship decide which unspoken conventions they can safely assume? Long-serving colleagues can rely on habits whose origins nobody remembers. Temporary colleagues have to discover the limits of such reliance while the clock is already running. This is the part of the inquiry for which I still have too few accounts from the players themselves, and it deserves more sustained attention. My aim is not to recommend one ideal rehearsal method or to celebrate everything that happens away from the audience. Some discussions are unproductive. Rather, I want to make the choices inside rehearsal audible enough for us to understand why a polished public result may depend on a process that was neither smooth nor unanimous.
