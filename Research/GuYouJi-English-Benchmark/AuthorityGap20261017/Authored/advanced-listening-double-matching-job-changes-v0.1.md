### 音频1完整原创脚本

S1: I used to organise corporate events, which meant being away whenever the children had a school holiday. I didn't mind the journeys themselves, but I couldn't keep asking my partner to rearrange everything at short notice. A college offered me a facilities role with a proper weekly timetable, and that decided it. The pay was a little lower, so money certainly wasn't the attraction. What I hadn't expected was how much publicity came with the job. When a room reopens, it's my photograph in the newsletter, not just the building. Everyone says that shows appreciation, and perhaps it does. I'd still rather people enjoyed the space without knowing who arranged it. I'm learning to smile for the camera, but it hasn't become a pleasure.

S2: For years I wrote reports about housing services without ever meeting anyone who used them. The reports weren't pointless, but I wanted to be there when a small change actually made somebody's day easier. That's why I took a front-desk job at a housing charity, despite friends telling me I was throwing away a comfortable career. It certainly isn't a better-paid one. There's plenty to learn, and some conversations are difficult. The great comfort is that I can always call the duty adviser. Nobody treats a request for help as a sign that I shouldn't be doing the job. I don't have to pretend I can solve everything on my own, and that makes it possible to concentrate on the person in front of me.

S3: I was the sales administrator in a furniture company. The targets could be demanding, but I was good at the job and wasn't trying to get away from them. Outside work I'd spent years repairing wooden instruments, mostly for friends. Eventually I realised that the care I put into a tiny repair was the part of the week I looked forward to. When a repair workshop advertised a position, I applied. I still have no wish to run the business. What matters is that when I suggest a different way of supporting an instrument during a repair, they actually try it. I'm the newest person there, but my observations don't disappear because someone senior has spoken. That feels better than having an impressive title.

S4: The laboratory paid well and the team were friendly. The difficulty was that every adjustment to a routine had to follow a procedure written elsewhere, even when we could see a better way. I moved to a small testing cooperative because I wanted some responsibility for choosing how the work was organised. I certainly got that. We discuss everything, including purchases I would once have signed off in a morning. I support the principle, but by the third meeting about a perfectly ordinary piece of equipment, I begin to wish somebody would just decide. I'm not planning to go back, and I wouldn't want a manager simply to impose an answer. I do hope we'll become quicker at reaching one together.

S5: My aunt asked whether I'd take over her printing shop when she retired. I considered it, but the job I eventually chose was with a mobile exhibition company. After ten years driving between sites for a delivery firm, I wanted to stay in one place. That's the whole reason I accepted their workshop post; the title sounded less exciting than my old one, but I could walk home every evening. I expected the move to cost me money. The basic wage was lower, yet the regular overtime and a qualification allowance have taken my monthly earnings above what I used to bring home. I hadn't counted on that. I'm not suddenly wealthy, but it's a welcome surprise after all the calculations I'd made before accepting.
