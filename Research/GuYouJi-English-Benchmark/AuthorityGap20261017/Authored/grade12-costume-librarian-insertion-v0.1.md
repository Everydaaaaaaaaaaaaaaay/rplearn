This fictional portrait follows Bea, who looks after a costume cupboard used by several neighbourhood theatre groups. People often imagine that her work consists of hanging clothes in rows. Bea says the harder part is preserving enough information for a garment to be borrowed, used and returned without a new argument at every stage.

The labels originally showed only size and colour. A long coat borrowed for a performance came back with a removable button missing. The next group thought the coat had always been incomplete. (1) ____ Borrowers could compare the garment with that record before taking responsibility for it.

Photographs helped, but they did not explain which changes were allowed. One group wanted to shorten a skirt for a child actor. (2) ____ A temporary fold was acceptable; cutting the fabric was not. Bea made the difference explicit before the scissors appeared, rather than treating a reasonable question as evidence of carelessness.

Some costumes were popular enough to receive overlapping requests. Bea did not settle these by giving the loudest caller priority. (3) ____ When a clash remained, she contacted both groups about alternative dates or garments. A visible arrangement could be discussed; a private favour could not be checked by anyone else.

Near the end of a busy season, she noticed that borrowers regularly arrived after the building's closing time. Repeating the closing hour had not solved the problem. (4) ____ Once the final collection was moved earlier, fewer people were left with a garment they had no agreed way to return that evening.

Bea kept a small notebook of such adjustments. It included rejected ideas as well as rules still in use. (5) ____ A future helper would need to understand why a rule existed before deciding whether a new situation called for a change. The cupboard was organised, but its organisation was never meant to become an unexplained tradition.
