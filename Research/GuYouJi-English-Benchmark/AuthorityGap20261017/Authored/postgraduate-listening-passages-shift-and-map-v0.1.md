## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

R: The following fictional account concerns a small translation office trying a more flexible working day. Its manager initially offered everybody the same freedom to choose a starting time. She expected the arrangement to be simple because each translator could work independently for much of the day. Within a week, however, urgent queries were accumulating late in the afternoon, when several experienced staff had already left. The difficulty was not that those people were working fewer hours. Their hours no longer overlapped with the period when their advice was most often needed. The manager could have required everyone to return to the old schedule. Instead, she asked the team to identify tasks that genuinely needed simultaneous availability and tasks that did not. They agreed on a shorter shared period, with separate arrangements for an urgent query outside it. Flexibility remained possible for most individual work. The revised arrangement was less identical for every role, but the reasons for the differences were written down and reviewed with the staff affected. After a month, fewer queries waited until the next day. The office did not announce that flexible hours had increased translation quality; it had measured waiting, not the accuracy of the finished work. The manager says the useful lesson was about coordination. Giving everyone the same choice had looked fair on paper, but it had hidden unequal dependencies between tasks. A workable agreement had to recognise those dependencies without assuming that every hour of every person's work needed to occur at the same time.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

R: For the fictional map collector Martin Vale, a map's most interesting feature is not always the accuracy of its roads. He began by collecting maps used by his own family. One had a pencil circle around a campsite that was never reached. Another carried a shopping list on the back. These marks could seem to damage the printed object, but Martin saw them as evidence of how a map entered somebody's life. When a local gallery proposed an exhibition, its first plan was to display only the cleanest maps, arranged by publication date. Martin suggested adding the marked copies and a short explanation of what was known about their use. He did not claim that every pencil mark could be interpreted. Some were anonymous, and a crossed-out route did not reveal whether a journey had been cancelled or merely changed. The exhibition kept those uncertainties visible. Visitors could compare the formal route printed on the page with the personal record added afterwards. To avoid overwhelming the display, the gallery chose a few well-documented examples rather than filling every wall. A separate catalogue listed the remaining maps without pretending they had all been equally researched. Martin was pleased when one visitor said she had never thought about the unused side of a map before. For him, collecting had become less about rescuing perfect objects and more about keeping the traces of imperfect use understandable. He still cared whether a road was correctly drawn. He simply refused to treat that as the only question a map could invite.
