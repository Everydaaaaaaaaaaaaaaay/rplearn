# 高级：临时花园的时间与归属

## 学生题面

阅读完整的五段虚构书序，为全部六题各选一个最佳答案。判断应依据本文的论证、语气和限定范围，不把书中虚构案例当作现实研究事实。

A temporary garden is often described as a welcome pause before something more serious happens to a piece of land. The phrase seems modest, but it has consequences. In the fictional city of Lanton, whose neighbourhood gardens provide the setting for this book, a pause can last longer than the childhood of a person who plays there. Calling the garden temporary does not make the relationships formed in it temporary in the same way. Nor does it tell us which of its effects will disappear when the site closes. My starting point is the mismatch between an administrative description and a lived duration. I am not suggesting that every short lease conceals a promise of permanence. I am asking what becomes harder to notice when the expected end of an activity is allowed to determine how seriously we take everything that happens before that end.

The first garden I visited had been praised for its openness. There was no membership fee and its gate stood open throughout the day. Yet the people who decided what to plant generally met on weekday mornings, when several regular visitors were at work. One visitor could therefore enter whenever she wished at weekends without ever taking part in a planting decision. The organisers had not deliberately excluded her, and moving meetings to the evening would have inconvenienced somebody else. This does not make the praise entirely false. It makes it incomplete. Access to a place, access to a conversation and influence over a decision are different opportunities, although publicity can fold them into a single attractive word. When later chapters describe a garden as open, they specify which of these opportunities is actually available and to whom.

It is tempting to compare the gardens by how much unused land they bring into use. That comparison answers a legitimate planning question, but it would be an eccentric guide to the experiences described here. A small plot beside a bus stop became a place where two neighbours who had avoided each other began sharing tools. A much larger site remained divided into self-contained beds whose gardeners rarely spoke. Neither story establishes the superiority of one size or arrangement. The second garden supported people who valued working undisturbed, which was also an achievement. I have therefore organised the central chapters around recurring situations, such as asking for a tool or resolving a disagreement about a path, rather than placing the sites in a league table. Comparison remains possible, but the terms of comparison should emerge from the question being asked, not from the convenience of a readily available total.

Closure brings these differences into unusually sharp focus. When the East Yard garden ended, its organisers moved the tool shed and divided the remaining plants among volunteers. Their inventory treated most of the garden as successfully recovered. Some former visitors told a different story: the convenient place to stop on the way home was gone, even though every spade had survived. Both accounts can be accurate. A portable collection of objects and a particular arrangement of everyday journeys are not the same thing. The distinction also complicates the familiar request that a new developer provide an equivalent space elsewhere. Equivalence is not established by matching the old garden's area alone. That does not make relocation pointless; it means that deciding what should be carried forward is itself a negotiation, rather than a calculation whose answer is already contained in the site plan.

Most accounts of temporary gardens finish at the moment when the land changes use. I have tried to follow the participants further, asking which acquaintances continued, which practices moved and which simply ceased. For people who stopped coming before closure, however, my account is much thinner. Their absence cannot safely be read as satisfaction, indifference or hostility, and finding ways to include their experiences remains an important task. The book consequently ends with an incomplete picture rather than a verdict on whether temporary gardens are good or bad. A garden can offer something worthwhile without delivering every benefit claimed for it. Taking that achievement seriously requires a more demanding description of what people were able to do, over what period and under whose conditions, than the cheerful statement that an empty site was briefly made green.
