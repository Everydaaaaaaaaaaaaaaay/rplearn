## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

W: The management team seems pleased with the four-day schedule trial. They say output stayed level even though people were here on fewer days. Are we ready to make it permanent?

M: I wouldn't jump to that conclusion. The trial involved the design team, whose work can often be arranged around projects. The customer support team still needs someone available every weekday.

W: Couldn't support staff simply take different days off? That would maintain coverage.

M: Possibly. But their handover time would increase, and we haven't included that in the figures. I'm not against the schedule. I just think we've tested one version of it in one department.

W: The staff questionnaire was positive too. Nearly everyone on the trial said they wanted to continue.

M: That matters, but remember they volunteered. People who thought the arrangement would be awkward could choose to stay outside it. We shouldn't assume the same response from everyone.

W: So you think the result is useless?

M: Not at all. It shows that this team could maintain its recorded output for eight weeks. That's worth knowing. It doesn't show what happens when a project runs late or when someone takes annual leave. The trial happened during a relatively quiet period.

W: What would you propose? Another questionnaire?

M: A second trial that includes a support group and a busier period. We should record handover hours as well as completed work, and let people report problems without having to defend their original choice.

W: That will delay the announcement the director wants to make next month.

M: We can announce the promising result now and explain what still needs testing. A cautious statement isn't the same as a negative one.

W: All right. I'll revise the briefing so the questionnaire is presented as feedback from volunteers, and ask the support manager to help design the next stage.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

M: We've received an offer from a volunteer group to produce subtitles for the local history interviews. It could help us release the whole series sooner.

W: That's welcome, but have they seen how much work is involved? We have twelve interviews, and some speakers use local expressions that are difficult even for our editors.

M: They've translated material for community events before. I thought we could divide the interviews among them and do a quick check at the end.

W: Experience is useful, but independent versions could use different names for the same places. Viewers might think two speakers were talking about different locations when they weren't.

M: Would a shared list of names solve that?

W: It would help. I'd also begin with one short interview, reviewed jointly by a volunteer and our editor. That would let us agree on how much speech to shorten for a readable subtitle without changing its meaning.

M: I'm concerned we'll spend more time organizing than we save. The first episode is supposed to appear in three weeks.

W: Then subtitle that episode first. The broadcast date doesn't require every interview to be ready at once. If we rush twelve versions now, correcting inconsistent choices later could take longer.

M: Fair enough. What about crediting the volunteers? Their coordinator asked whether individual names could appear.

W: Certainly, with their agreement. But a credit shouldn't imply that a volunteer has approved every editorial change we make afterwards. We need to show the final version to the person who worked on it.

M: And if somebody can't finish on time?

W: We should have a clear handover arrangement, rather than quietly removing their name or expecting another unpaid person to work through the night.

M: I'll discuss a one-interview pilot with the coordinator, including the names list and how final versions are approved. We can set a realistic schedule for the rest after that.
