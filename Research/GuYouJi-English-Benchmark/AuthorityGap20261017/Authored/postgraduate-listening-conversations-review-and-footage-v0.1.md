## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

W: My review of the new history book is far too long. The editor asked for eight hundred words, and I have nearly twice that. I could shorten it by removing the examples.

M: First decide what judgement the review is making. Some examples may be doing essential work. If they show why you disagree with a claim, removing them could leave an accusation without support.

W: The book argues that the town’s old market was planned entirely by one official. I found that it mentions local traders elsewhere, but never connects their decisions to that argument.

M: Then that gap is worth explaining with a precise example. You do not need to summarise every chapter to make it clear. Readers can understand a focused criticism without receiving a miniature version of the whole book.

W: I also wrote that the author had ignored local traders. That is stronger than what I can actually show, since she does discuss them in two chapters.

M: Change the wording. Your criticism concerns the connection between her evidence and her central explanation, not the complete absence of those people. Being precise will make the review shorter and fairer.

W: What about the paragraph praising the photographs? I genuinely think they are useful, but it reads like a separate subject.

M: Keep it if you can explain their relevance to the book’s argument or the reader’s use of it. Otherwise, a brief sentence may be enough. Balance does not require equal space for every positive and negative feature.

W: So I should cut the chapter-by-chapter summary, retain the market example, and narrow the claim about the traders. I had been treating length as a problem of removing words at random.

M: Exactly. Once the purpose is clear, you can choose what the review needs. Send me the revised version before polishing every sentence; there is no point perfecting material you may decide not to include.

录音问题：Why does the man caution against removing all examples?

### 第2段完整原创脚本

M: We have permission to use the interview clips in the student documentary, but two contributors are asking how they will be credited. I assumed the same line at the end would be enough.

W: It depends on what the line says. Someone who appears in an interview and someone who provides an old photograph have made different contributions. A general thanks may not explain which material came from whom.

M: One contributor has asked to remain unnamed on screen, even though we can use her voice. I thought those wishes contradicted each other.

W: They need not. Permission to include a voice is not automatically permission to publish a name. Record her preferences clearly and make sure the credit does not identify her indirectly through a description she has not approved.

M: The photograph came from another contributor’s family collection. He owns that copy, but he does not know who took it.

W: Then say what is known about the source and do not invent a photographer’s name. Ownership of a copy does not by itself settle every question about reproduction. Ask the project supervisor what checks are needed before release.

M: We had hoped to finish tonight. Could we leave the photograph in the draft and mark the query for the supervisor?

W: Yes, in the internal working version. But do not treat the marked draft as a public release. The question has to be resolved, or the image replaced or removed, before publication.

M: I will prepare a material list showing each clip or image, its source, the agreed use and the credit request. That should make the differences easier to check.

W: Good. Also check that the final exported film uses the agreed credits. An accurate list does not help if an earlier credit line accidentally remains in the version people see.

M: I will make that the final export check, rather than assuming the notes automatically control the film.
