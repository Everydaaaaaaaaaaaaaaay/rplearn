### The instrument that kept its scars

In a fictional instrument workshop, Lena was asked to prepare an old violin for a town concert. Its owner wanted two things: a reliable instrument for a young player and an object that still showed where it had been. The demands sounded compatible until Lena examined a dark mark near the edge. Removing it would make the violin look more uniform, but nobody could tell her whether it was accidental damage or a trace of an earlier repair.

(1) ____

The pencilled sketch settled that narrow question. The mark was already visible before the current owner acquired the violin, so his memory of a recent accident could not explain it. It did not reveal who had made it or why. Lena left those questions open and turned to the quite different problem of whether the instrument could be played comfortably.

A replacement bridge could be fitted without altering the mark. Lena made two trial versions and invited the young player, Arun, to compare them. She deliberately withheld her own preference, since she wanted to hear what he noticed rather than whether he could recognise an expert's recommendation.

(2) ____

The reversal was important: a difference first described as the character of the violin now followed the accessory fitted to it. Arun still preferred one arrangement, but the explanation for his preference had changed. Lena kept his comments beside the measurements. Neither the numerical record nor the player's description could answer every question the other raised.

The owner was impatient with this cautious procedure. With the concert approaching, he asked Lena to choose the brightest-sounding arrangement and stop discussing the alternatives. Arun, however, would have to play a much quieter piece before the final ensemble, and he was worried that a choice made for one moment would constrain the rest of his performance.

(3) ____

That compromise protected the change between the two pieces without pretending that the disagreement had disappeared. The workshop would prepare both arrangements, and Arun would make the final choice at rehearsal. For Lena, the owner's wish to impress an audience deserved consideration, but it could not substitute for the needs of the person holding the instrument.

A visitor who watched the work suggested a short film for the concert programme. He proposed a dramatic sequence in which the old violin emerged from darkness, lost its imperfections and returned to life. Lena liked the opportunity to explain the work, but the proposed transformation was precisely the story her treatment of the surface refused to tell.

(4) ____

This less spectacular sequence made the retained mark visible in the final shot. Viewers could compare it with the opening image and see that restoration had not meant making every trace vanish. The film no longer offered an effortless triumph, yet its account of what had changed was more accurate and, to Lena, more interesting.

After the concert, the owner asked whether the workshop could advertise the evening as proof that the repair had improved the violin's sound. The instrument had been played in a different hall by a player whose technique was developing rapidly. The enthusiastic response was welcome; its cause was another matter.

(5) ____

The wording disappointed the visitor, who thought it lacked a memorable claim. Lena declined to strengthen it. She had accepted responsibility for a particular repair, not acquired a licence to assign every subsequent success to it. An honest account could describe the work and the response while admitting that they did not establish the proposed causal link.

Arun returned some weeks later with a smaller request. He wanted a copy of Lena's working notes, including the trial arrangement he had rejected. He expected his own preferences to change, and a clean summary of the final choice would give him little help in understanding that change.

(6) ____

Those annotated copies became the beginning of a continuing conversation. The instrument remained playable, but the record of its treatment remained revisable. Lena had initially been asked to reconcile appearance with performance. She came to think that preserving the reasons for a decision mattered as much as preserving the decision's visible result.

### 待插入的完整段落 A—G（有一段不用）

**A**

She offered instead to record a series of comparisons: the surface before and after cleaning, the two bridges side by side, and the trials that had led to a preference. There would be no shot in which the mark disappeared. The camera would follow a process of choosing, rather than stage the disappearance of age.

**B**

She gave him the full record, distinguishing measurements from tentative judgements and dating each entry. Alongside the discarded proposal, she left space for his later comments. It was not an instruction to return to that proposal, but a way of keeping its reasons available when circumstances changed.

**C**

First she laid a recent photograph beside a much older drawing found in the case. The photograph showed the mark clearly; the drawing included it too, faintly pencilled next to the same corner. Comparing the two images could establish how long the feature had been present without settling what it meant.

**D**

The concert had originally been planned for an outdoor stage. A forecast of rain persuaded the organisers to book the hall instead, although the larger audience they anticipated never materialised. Several unused tickets were later offered to pupils at a neighbouring school, whose teacher had helped arrange transport.

**E**

Her proposed account said only that the violin had been prepared for use and had been warmly received at the concert. It made no comparison with its earlier sound. Establishing that stronger claim would have required a different kind of comparison from the one the evening had provided.

**F**

Instead of settling the dispute by choosing a single sound for the whole evening, Lena suggested a reversible plan. They would try both bridges during rehearsal and leave enough time to change between the quiet piece and the ensemble. Arun could then judge each arrangement in the situation for which it was intended.

**G**

Arun first called the instrument sharp and restless, then described it as rounded and calm. When Lena exchanged the bridges and asked him to repeat the passages, those descriptions changed places. She had also kept the order of the musical passages constant, so that the change was not simply a response to different music.

## 作者核对

### 1：C

C承上检验不明痕迹，明确比较照片与旧画，下文The pencilled sketch和已有痕迹才能承接。A是影片工序，B是工作记录交付，D是换场天气，E是演后宣传，F是两演奏情境折衷，G是桥测试；均不提供旧画早已画出该痕迹的局部证据。

### 2：G

G接two trial versions并交换琴桥，同时固定乐段；下文reversal和followed the accessory对应描述随桥调换。C比较痕迹图像，F虽也有两桥但提出未来排练而未报告描述颠倒，A/B/D/E分别是影片、记录、场地、宣传，不能承接已发生反转。

### 3：F

F同时满足安静独奏与合奏，以可逆两桥方案承接分歧；下文That compromise、between the two pieces和prepare both逐项回扣。G是先前测试报告非此处排练方案；A/B/C/D/E都不协调这两首曲子的演奏选择。

### 4：A

A用过程比较替代消除年龄痕迹的影片神话，末句明确不让痕迹消失；下文less spectacular sequence、final shot和opening image回扣。C虽然也谈图像，但无拍摄方案与最终镜头；B/D/E/F/G均非此处影片设计。

### 5：E

E给出克制的演后表述，不宣称声音较以前改善，下文The wording和declined to strengthen it有明确先行内容。D仅叙场地史而未提出任何宣传措辞；A/B/C/F/G均不能承接该因果声明争议。

### 6：B

B回应索取完整旧笔记，区分测量与暂时判断并留出评论空间，下文Those annotated copies和continuing conversation回扣。A是给观众的影片而非给演奏者的全记录；C/D/E/F/G都没有交付笔记及后续批注。
