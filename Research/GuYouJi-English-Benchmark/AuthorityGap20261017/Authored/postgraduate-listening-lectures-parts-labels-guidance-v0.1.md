## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

R: Imagine a workshop making replacement handles for old cupboards. At first, every handle is measured and shaped separately. This takes time, and the owner suggests creating one standard design. Many cupboards do indeed use the same distance between screw holes, so a common part could make some repairs quicker. But one maker points out that the distance is not the only condition. A door may have a different thickness, or a projecting edge may leave less room for a hand. A handle that fits the holes can still be awkward to use. The workshop does not abandon standard parts. Instead, it lists the conditions under which the common design is suitable and the observations needed before selecting it. Measurements that fall outside those conditions lead to a different part or a custom repair. This makes the standard a useful option rather than a claim that all cupboards are secretly identical. The next difficulty concerns the order form. Customers may not know which measurements matter, and a request for technical details can produce confident guesses. The workshop adds a simple way to request a check rather than forcing every customer to enter a number. A missing measurement is then marked as missing, not converted automatically into the most common value. The distinction matters because a guessed value can travel through an efficient production system without attracting further attention. The workshop also keeps the reason for a custom part beside its dimensions. A future maker can see whether the difference solved a real clearance problem or merely reflected an earlier maker's preference. Standardisation, in this example, is valuable because it makes repeated work manageable. Its value does not depend on denying variation. The stronger design is the one that makes the boundary of the standard clear enough for people to recognise when they need something else.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

R: In a fictional museum exercise, students are asked to describe the colours in a woven cloth. The first group produces a confident list: red, blue and brown. The second writes that the border appears reddish under the gallery lamp but more purple beside the window. It would be easy to declare the second group more accurate simply because its description is longer. The instructor resists that conclusion. First ask what the description is for. If a visitor is trying to identify the cloth among three exhibits, a short colour contrast may be enough. If a conservator is recording a possible change in the material, the lighting conditions become important. These tasks demand different kinds of information. The instructor is not suggesting that any colour word is as useful as any other. A description still has to be connected to what was observed. But the words should be assessed against the job they are intended to do, rather than against the assumption that more detail always means more care. The class then tries an audio description. A listener cannot look back at the previous sentence as easily as a reader can look back on a page. Repeating a key location, such as along the lower border, may therefore be useful rather than clumsy. On a written label, the same repetition might be unnecessary because the reader can pause and compare the sentences. The exercise ends with two versions instead of a single supposedly perfect description: a brief identification for a visitor and a more conditional observation record for specialist follow-up. The versions do not compete to be the longest. They make their purpose and conditions clearer. What the students learn is a way of asking questions about description. Who will use these words, in what situation, and for what decision? Those questions can reveal why a difference in wording is necessary without turning every difference into a disagreement about the object itself.

### 第3段完整原创脚本

R: Consider a fictional heritage walk that offers recorded directions. Its writer initially turns the printed leaflet into a script without changing the wording. On paper, a reader can see a map, scan the headings and return to the previous paragraph. In a recording, those supports are not automatically present. A sentence beginning after that may refer to a step the listener has already forgotten. The team asks volunteers to follow the spoken route without the leaflet. Several stop at a junction because the recording describes two turns before telling them which landmark to look for. The writer moves the landmark description earlier, so listeners can identify the place before receiving the action. This is a change in the order of information, not merely a slower delivery of the same sentences. Another problem appears when two routes share a short opening. A listener choosing the shorter walk still hears details belonging only to the longer one. The team makes the point of separation explicit and provides a separate continuation for each route. It does not ask every listener to remember which later sentences should be ignored. During a second trial, the shorter version is easier to follow. Yet the team keeps a written alternative for visitors who prefer it or cannot use the recording. The spoken route is an additional means of access, not evidence that the leaflet has become unnecessary. Finally, the team records a version date and the names of landmarks that need checking when the site changes. A temporary barrier can make an accurate direction out of date. The finished recording is therefore accompanied by a maintenance note, even though that note is not read aloud at every stop. The lesson is that moving information between media is a design task. It requires attention to what a person can notice, remember and control in the new situation, as well as care that the directions remain connected to the place they describe.
