# 高二：熟悉之后如何解释

## 学生题面

阅读完整短文，按各题所问范围选择一个最佳答案，保留段落划分。

In this fictional advice column, a science-club organiser describes a familiar frustration. After mastering a new instrument, she gives newcomers a quick explanation and is surprised when they cannot repeat the procedure. She remembers the correct sequence so easily that its difficult transitions no longer seem to require explanation. The problem is not an unwillingness to help; she has lost sight of what knowing less was like.

The columnist calls this “the disappearing staircase”. Each step remains necessary for the beginner, but the experienced person can mentally travel from the bottom to the top without noticing it. This is a difference in available knowledge, rather than evidence that either person is more intelligent. It can arise even when the explanation uses simple words: an ordinary word such as “reset” may conceal several operations that only the speaker already knows.

Good intentions alone will not expose every missing step. Asking “Do you understand?” may produce a polite yes from a newcomer who does not yet know what to ask. In the account, one volunteer discovered this when a pupil copied his final result but could not repeat the process with a second example. The successful copy had shown the ability to follow that particular demonstration, not an independent grasp of all its choices.

The columnist does not recommend returning every conversation to its simplest possible starting point. That would waste time and could make a knowledgeable listener feel ignored. Instead, the speaker needs some way of discovering the listener's present starting point. The next section of the column will therefore turn from describing the missing staircase to practical ways of finding which of its steps a particular learner still needs.
