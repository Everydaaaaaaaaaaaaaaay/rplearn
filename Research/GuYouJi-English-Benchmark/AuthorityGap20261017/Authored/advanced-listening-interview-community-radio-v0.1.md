You hear an interview with Amy Reed, a community radio producer, and Jo Lin, a presenter.

1. What does Amy particularly value about working at the station?
   A. The absence of any formal process for choosing stories.
   B. The possibility of responding promptly to ideas from the community.
   C. The freedom to broadcast every story suggested by a listener.
   D. The opportunity to avoid predictable subjects altogether.

2. What did Jo's first experience of presenting change?
   A. Her belief that good presenting required a consistently assured performance.
   B. Her preference for asking questions rather than editing recordings.
   C. Her opinion that a presenter should avoid questions of personal interest.
   D. Her confidence that volunteering would be an easy route into broadcasting.

3. How did Amy approach the story about the rehearsal room?
   A. She removed local details to make the dispute more widely relevant.
   B. She allowed official statements to determine which questions were asked.
   C. She used a recognisable issue to explore the specific situation more effectively.
   D. She presented a general argument instead of interviewing the people involved.

4. What do Amy and Jo disagree about when editing an interview?
   A. Whether a broadcast should offer a worthwhile answer before an item ends.
   B. Whether a longer version should ever be made available online.
   C. Whether guests should be allowed to complete their stories.
   D. How much of a speaker's hesitation it is useful to retain.

5. What do both speakers suggest about listener feedback?
   A. A strongly worded message should override the team's editorial judgement.
   B. It is useful evidence whose meaning needs to be interpreted with care.
   C. The number of messages accurately represents how much a programme matters.
   D. A lack of immediate response usually indicates a topic should be abandoned.

6. What is Jo's main point about the successful last-minute replacement?
   A. An unplanned conversation is generally more effective than a prepared interview.
   B. An engineer is usually a more engaging guest than a recognised expert.
   C. Established preparation and trust made an improvised idea possible.
   D. Listener recordings can remove the need to schedule future programmes.

## 作者核对

### 1：B

她重视从发现话题到作决定路径短、邻里谈话下周可成节目，B。A仍有选择决定；C明说不是每个conversation上播；D不以意外本身为留任原因。

### 2：A

被临时推上场后停止模仿永远自信的声线，接纳紧张，A。B未说从此不爱剪辑；C她问真正想知的题是好转因；D入站原本求剪辑，没有easyroute信念。

### 3：C

找共享空间分配的共通问题以问具体人物更好，同时保留声音细节，C。A明确保留untidy details；B未赋官方主导；D不想讲公平讲座、仍采访人物。

### 4：D

Jo觉得寻找用词的停顿助信任，Amy担心像掉线，D。A/B/C无明确对立，分别是Amy主张、可用做法、两人认可的故事完整性。

### 5：B

Jo把信息当复审机会不是命令，Amy补沉默不等无讨论，B。A反于message isn't vote；C/D夜班节目反例均否定。

### 6：C

录制备用材料争取思考时间、长期信任使临场方案可试，C。A她明否准备不需，也无一般优越；B未比较工程师与专家；D定期栏目仍需策划，不是不用排节目。

### 音频1完整原创脚本

I: Today we're behind the scenes at a community radio station. My guests are producer Amy Reed and presenter Jo Lin. Amy, people imagine local radio as a place where anything can happen. Is that its main attraction for you?

G1: The surprises can be lovely, but they aren't why I stay. I like knowing that a conversation in a shop can turn into a useful programme the following week. At a larger station I once spent three months trying to get a simple neighbourhood story approved. Here, the person who notices a subject can also do something about it. That doesn't mean we put every conversation on air. It means the route from an idea to a decision is short enough for people to keep suggesting things.

I: Jo, you joined as a volunteer. Were you aiming to become a presenter?

G2: I wanted to edit recordings. Talking into a microphone sounded dreadful. Then our regular presenter lost her voice and I was the only person who knew that week's material. I got through the hour by asking the guest questions I actually wanted answered. I wouldn't recommend being thrown in like that, but it stopped me trying to imitate the bright, endlessly confident voice I thought radio required. I still get nervous. I'm just no longer trying to sound as though I don't.

I: Amy, how do you decide whether a local story will work beyond the people directly involved?

G1: I look for a question someone elsewhere might share. We covered a dispute about a rehearsal room, for instance. The room itself meant nothing to most listeners, but deciding who gets access to a shared space is something many people recognise. We kept the particular voices and the untidy details. I didn't want to turn it into a lecture on fairness. The wider question was there to help us ask better questions of those particular people.

G2: And sometimes the apparently minor details are what make you listen. The person who kept a spare key in a biscuit tin told you more about how the room had been run than an official statement did. We didn't plan that moment. We heard it because we left enough time for the guest to finish the story.

I: There must be pressure to keep everything short.

G1: There is. A conversation can wander beautifully and still need an ending. We sometimes move the longer version online. But I don't want the broadcast to feel like a set of advertisements for things you have to listen to somewhere else. If it raises a question, it should give you something worth having before the next item starts.

G2: I'd keep more of the hesitation than Amy does. You can hear someone searching for the right word, and that can be the point at which you trust them. Amy hears the same pause and worries that the listener thinks the connection has failed. We usually find a compromise, but our first instincts are different.

I: How much attention do you pay to messages from listeners?

G2: A great deal, though a message isn't a vote. One listener wrote that a guest had sounded unsure of herself. I listened again and thought she'd been careful about a complicated question. We didn't tell future guests to sound more certain. We did ask whether our introduction had made clear why uncertainty was appropriate. Feedback is a chance to reconsider a choice, rather than an instruction to undo it.

G1: I'd add that the people who write aren't the whole audience. We heard almost nothing after a programme about night workers. Months later, a nurse told me several colleagues had been discussing it during their breaks. Silence in the inbox doesn't necessarily mean silence everywhere else. That was a useful correction to my habit of judging an hour by the next morning's messages.

I: You recently had to replace a planned live interview at the last minute. What happened?

G1: Our guest was stranded on a train. We had a short recorded item ready, but it wouldn't fill the whole gap. Jo suggested asking the engineer about the sounds he hears while walking to work. It could have been a disaster. Instead, he described the city with such care that listeners started sending their own recordings. We've made a regular feature from it.

G2: I don't think the lesson is that preparation is unnecessary. I could try that because the recorded item gave us a few minutes to think, and I trusted the engineer to tell me if the idea wasn't working. The spontaneous part depended on a lot of ordinary preparation and a relationship built over time.

I: Amy Reed and Jo Lin, thank you for joining us.
