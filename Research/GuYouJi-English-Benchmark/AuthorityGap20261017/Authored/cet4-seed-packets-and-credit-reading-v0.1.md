# 四级：种子标签与海报署名两篇阅读

## 学生题面

阅读两篇完整短文。第1—5题依据Passage One，第6—10题依据Passage Two，每题从A—D中选一个最佳答案，完成全部十题。人物、机构及事件均为虚构。

### Passage One

When volunteers at a fictional community garden opened a donated box, they found several packets of flower seeds without dates. A note on top said that the seeds came from a garden that had won a local prize. Some volunteers wanted to print the prize-winning garden's name on their own display, suggesting that the donated seeds would produce equally impressive flowers.

Lena, who kept the donation records, asked a simpler question first: which seeds had actually come from that garden? The box contained packets from two different households. Their owners had used similar paper envelopes, and the person delivering them had placed everything together. The note described one donation, not necessarily the whole box.

One volunteer proposed planting a few seeds from every packet and waiting to see what grew. Lena agreed that observing the plants might answer some questions about the seeds. It would not, however, establish who had collected them. A successful flower would carry no record of which household had supplied its packet. The group needed to separate the question of what the seeds could do from the question of where they had come from.

They contacted both donors. One still had photographs of the packets before delivery, including a small mark she had drawn on each envelope. These identified three packets. The rest could not be assigned confidently, so the garden recorded their source as unknown. It did not give every packet the identity of the best-documented ones.

The final display described the donation and the uncertainty without promising a prize-winning result. Visitors could read which packets had been identified and how. Lena considered that a more useful account than a cheerful claim that the evidence could not support. The garden could still value the gift, she explained, without presenting an attractive story about it as a fact about every seed.

### Passage Two

A student arts club at the fictional Northmere College asked Ravi to create a poster for an exhibition. He agreed to arrange the words and choose the colours. Another student, Jo, supplied a drawing that would appear in the centre. The club secretary told both that their work would be acknowledged, but no one discussed exactly how the names would be printed.

When the poster appeared, it carried the line “Poster by Ravi”. Jo felt that viewers would naturally assume he had drawn the central image as well. Ravi had not asked for that wording. He had sent a file containing both names to the secretary, who shortened it because the line seemed too long for the available space. Each person had understood the promise of acknowledgement differently.

At their meeting, Ravi suggested simply removing every name. That would avoid giving him credit for Jo's drawing, but Jo objected. Her concern was not that no one should be identified; she wanted the different contributions to be visible. The secretary then proposed a revised line: “Drawing: Jo. Layout: Ravi.” It fitted below the image after a small change to the spacing.

The club replaced the printed posters it could reach and added a correction to the online version. It could not recover every copy already shared by students. Its public note therefore identified which wording had changed instead of claiming that the mistake had disappeared everywhere. Jo accepted that practical limit, while asking the club to use the fuller description in future announcements.

The secretary later added a question to the club's planning form: how should each contribution be credited? This was not a promise that disagreements would never occur again. It made a previously hidden decision available for discussion before publication. The experience had shown that a friendly intention to acknowledge people was less precise than an agreed description of what each person had done.
