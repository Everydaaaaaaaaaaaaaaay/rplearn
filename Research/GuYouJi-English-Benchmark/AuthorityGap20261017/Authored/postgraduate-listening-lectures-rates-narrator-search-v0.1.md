## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

R: Consider an imaginary cooperative that wishes to report participation in its annual meeting. Its first report says that attendance rose from forty people to sixty. That sounds like an improvement, and as a statement about the number in the room it is perfectly clear. But the cooperative also gained new members during the year. It had eighty members at the first meeting and two hundred at the second. The attendance rate therefore fell from fifty per cent to thirty per cent. There is no contradiction between the larger crowd and the lower rate. They answer different questions. The count tells us how many attended. The rate relates that count to a defined membership. Which is more useful depends on what we are trying to understand. If we need to know how many chairs to provide, the count is directly relevant. If we are discussing how widely members were represented, the rate may add information that the count hides. Even then, we should ask who was included in the membership list and whether that definition stayed the same. A percentage does not remove those decisions. Now suppose someone suggests making the report look better by describing the extra twenty attendees but omitting the expanding membership. The problem is not that the count becomes mathematically false. It is that a true statement is being invited to carry an unsupported implication about broader participation. A responsible report can print both measures and say what each describes. It can also discuss why some members did not attend without pretending that their reasons can be read from the percentage alone. The point of this example is to resist the search for one number that settles every argument. Before choosing a measure, state the question. Then make the population and comparison visible enough for another person to see whether the number actually answers it.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

R: In a fictional literature seminar, the class reads a story told through a traveller's notebook. On the first page, the traveller describes a host as unfriendly because he speaks very little. Later, we learn that the host had been trying to protect the traveller from overhearing news she was not ready to receive. Several students say that the first page is simply wrong and should be ignored. The seminar leader asks them to distinguish an observation from the interpretation attached to it. The host did speak little. The traveller's explanation for the silence was uncertain. A narrative can preserve the observation while inviting us to reconsider what it meant. This distinction also changes how we think about the narrator. A mistaken interpretation does not necessarily make every event in the notebook false. Nor does it automatically tell us that the author shares the traveller's first judgement. The author has arranged later information that allows a reader to see beyond it. The class then examines a second moment. The traveller claims to have understood the host immediately, but her earlier entries show repeated confusion. Here the interest lies in the distance between the narrator's later account of herself and the record the story has already given us. We are not required to diagnose the character or accuse a real person of dishonesty. We are being asked to notice how different parts of the narrative qualify one another. One student proposes rewriting the opening so that the traveller understands everything from the start. That would remove the inconsistency, but it would also remove the reader's experience of discovering the limits of the traveller's view. The seminar leader suggests that an apparent weakness in a narrator can be part of the design of a strong narrative. Reading carefully means asking which voice makes a claim, what that voice could know at the time, and how the rest of the story asks us to treat the claim.

### 第3段完整原创脚本

R: Imagine you are designing a search tool for a collection of local photographs. A visitor types the name of a bridge. The collection contains a photograph filed under an older name for the same bridge, so an exact match returns nothing. The first improvement seems obvious: accept both names. That is useful, but it introduces a question about how the result should be presented. If the tool silently replaces every old name with the new one, a researcher may lose information about how a photograph was originally described. The design team therefore keeps the catalogue wording visible and adds the alternative as a searchable link. Searching can become more forgiving without rewriting the historical record. Now consider a query for market. Some photographs show a market square, while others show a shop with market in its name. A high position in the results cannot by itself decide which sense the visitor intended. The interface can offer filters or a short explanation of why a result matched. It should not pretend that the first result is necessarily the answer. Finally, suppose the team judges success only by whether visitors click something. A click may mean that a promising item was found. It may also mean that the brief result description was too vague to reject. The team asks visitors to complete a small locating task and explain whether the item they opened met the stated need. This adds work to the evaluation, but it relates the observed action to the purpose of the search. None of these choices promises a perfect catalogue or an infallible interpretation of every query. The design separates three responsibilities: preserving what the collection says, helping a visitor express a request, and providing enough context to judge a returned item. When those responsibilities are confused, a tool can appear efficient while making the collection harder to understand. When they are distinguished, the team has a clearer way to discuss which problem a particular change is intended to solve.
