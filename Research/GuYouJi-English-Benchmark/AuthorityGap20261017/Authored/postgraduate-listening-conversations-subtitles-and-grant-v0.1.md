## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

W: Our student podcast is adding video, and I have been asked to prepare the subtitles. I thought I could simply use the automatic transcript, but the producer wants me to edit it.

M: The transcript is a starting point. People repeat themselves when speaking, and the viewer has limited time to read. Editing does not mean changing their position; it means deciding which repetitions can disappear without removing a qualification.

W: That is what worries me. One guest says a new scheme will probably help, and then adds that it depends on how it is introduced. If I keep only the first part, the subtitle looks much more confident.

M: Keep the condition. Remove an empty restart if you need space, but do not exchange a cautious claim for a promise. We can also divide a long contribution across two subtitle screens.

W: The producer suggested putting an explanation of the scheme underneath at the same time. Would that solve the problem?

M: It might overload the screen. The subtitle is carrying what the guest says. Background information could appear on a separate card before the interview, clearly marked as the editorial introduction.

W: So the two kinds of text should not look as if they came from the same speaker. I also have a name I cannot catch in the recording.

M: Ask the producer to check it against the guest list. Do not choose the nearest familiar name just because the software supplies one. Leave a query in the working file until it is confirmed.

W: We are due to publish on Thursday. I could finish the easy parts today and send the uncertain name with its time in the recording.

M: That would be useful. I can review the condition we discussed and the layout tomorrow. After that, we should watch the video with the sound off to check whether the text can actually be followed.

W: I will send a draft rather than call the transcript finished. It is a different task from merely producing every word the microphone recorded.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

M: I am applying for the small community research grant. The maximum award would cover our whole project, but I am not sure how to present the budget.

W: Separate the essential work from optional additions. The panel needs to see what can still be done if it offers less than the maximum. Otherwise, a reduced award looks as if it would leave the proposal impossible.

M: The essential part is interviewing former shopkeepers. We also want a printed booklet, although that could wait. Should I list the booklet as if it were included in every version?

W: No. Explain the smaller project honestly. You could prepare an online account with the interviews and a clear record of permission, then seek printing funds later. Do not promise a booklet that a smaller award cannot support.

M: We have a volunteer who can design it without payment. I was going to count that as cash already secured.

W: Describe it as a promised contribution of time, with its conditions. It is valuable, but it is not money you can spend on travel or printing. The panel should be able to distinguish those resources.

M: We also need permission from participants. Is it enough to say that anyone agreeing to an interview has agreed to publication?

W: Those are different decisions. Your proposal should explain how you will ask about recording and public use separately. A person may be willing to talk while wanting a particular story kept private.

M: That will make the procedure longer. The application form allows an attachment, so I could outline it there rather than squeeze everything into the budget box.

W: Exactly. Use the budget for costs and the attachment for the method. Before submission, ask the volunteer to confirm how much time she can offer. A contribution should not become certain merely because it looks useful in a form.

M: I will revise the essential version first, then confirm her offer and write the permission procedure. The larger booklet can remain a clearly labelled possibility.
