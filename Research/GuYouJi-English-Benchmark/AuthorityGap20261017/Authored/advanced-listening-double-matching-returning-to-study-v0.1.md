### 音频1完整原创脚本

S1: I hadn't been planning to study furniture design. Then the project I was employed on ended six months earlier than expected, with enough money left in my contract to keep me going for a while. I suddenly had a stretch of uncommitted time, and the course started the following week. It seemed better than spending every morning looking at the same job advertisements. The first exercises have been enjoyable, but I've looked ahead to the final project and I'm a little uneasy about how quickly the technical demands increase. Everyone says to take one stage at a time. I am doing that; I just hope the later stages don't arrive before I've really understood the earlier ones.

S2: I'd been photographing old railway buildings for years. I could recognise different window shapes, but I couldn't explain why a station had been arranged in one way rather than another. I enrolled on a local-history course because I wanted more than a collection of attractive photographs. There's no job or formal qualification at the end for me. What I'm enjoying most is having to rethink things I used to say with complete confidence. A building I'd called badly planned turns out to have served a purpose I'd never considered. I don't feel cheated when an old explanation falls apart. It means there's a more interesting question to take back to the building next time I visit.

S3: I was asked to lead a small team at work and suddenly found myself presenting figures I hadn't produced. I needed to know what questions to ask before passing them on, so I signed up for an evening statistics course. It wasn't a condition of keeping the job, and I'm not preparing to change careers. I just wanted to do the new part of my current job properly. The reassuring thing has been the other students. People arrive knowing different bits, and nobody seems embarrassed about admitting a gap. Last week I helped someone interpret a chart; ten minutes later she explained a calculation I'd been staring at for half an hour. That exchange makes a difficult evening feel manageable.

S4: I left school before taking the mathematics course I'd wanted to finish. For years I told myself it no longer mattered, but the feeling of having stopped halfway kept returning. This year I finally joined an adult class. I expected to follow a fixed timetable and take whatever arrangements were offered. When caring responsibilities made Tuesday evenings impossible, the tutor let me attend another group and bring my questions to a short online session. I hadn't realised a course could bend around a student's circumstances like that. I'm still doing the same work as everyone else; I haven't been excused from it. It's the way I can reach it that has changed, and that has made staying on the course possible.

S5: The stage-design programme I wanted to enter required a certificate in technical drawing. I'd done plenty of informal work in theatres, but that didn't count as the specific qualification, so I enrolled on a preparatory course. I wouldn't have chosen it simply to fill my evenings. Now I'm glad the assessment asks us to produce and explain a workable plan, rather than write a polished essay about design. I can show what I've learnt by making something another person could actually use. I still have to justify every measurement. It isn't an easy option, but it plays to the way I think, and I feel that the result will represent what I can do.
