# 高三：译文覆盖率能说明什么

## 学生题面

阅读完整短文，按各题所问范围选择一个最佳答案，保留段落划分。

A fictional local history project has proposed a new figure for its annual report. It defines its translation-access share as the number of newly published articles offered in two languages divided by all the articles newly published that year, multiplied by 100. An article counts once in both numerator and denominator, however many pages it contains. Older articles translated during the year are recorded separately and do not enter this particular share.

Suppose the project publishes 40 new articles, of which 18 appear in both languages. Its translation-access share is 45 per cent. The team also translates 12 older articles, but adding those to the numerator while leaving the denominator at 40 would mix two different populations. The larger figure might look encouraging while no longer measuring what the report says it measures.

The proposal's supporters argue that the share draws attention to a publishing choice that total article counts obscure. Yet a reader cannot infer from a rising share that the number of bilingual articles has risen: the total number of new articles may instead have fallen. Nor does the figure establish whether either version is easy to understand. Publishing two difficult versions is different from enabling two groups of readers to use them.

For this reason, the proposed report would print the underlying counts and explain which articles were included. It would place reader feedback beside the percentage rather than turn the two into a single mark. The figure can reveal one aspect of access while leaving important questions open. Used with that restraint, it could make the project more answerable for its choices. Used as a complete certificate of inclusion, it would encourage the very confusion between visible activity and experienced benefit that the report is intended to avoid.
