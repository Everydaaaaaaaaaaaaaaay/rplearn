1. Why does Helen qualify the description of her first stall as an overnight success?
   A. The queue was mostly composed of people waiting for another stall.
   B. The photograph concealed how little work she had received.
   C. The apparent sudden success followed a lengthy period of practical mistakes.
   D. The publicity prevented her from completing repairs on time.

2. What has Ruth learnt to establish when she first discusses a repair?
   A. Which features the customer regards as important to preserve.
   B. Whether the customer is willing to accept her preferred finish.
   C. How much the object would be worth if it were sold.
   D. Whether the object originally belonged to a relative.

3. What point do both speakers make about explaining prices?
   A. Offering an apology makes customers more willing to pay.
   B. The environmental benefits should outweigh any financial objection.
   C. A repair should always be cheaper than replacing the object.
   D. Customers should receive a clear account of the service without emotional pressure.

4. Why is Ruth cautious about using a common brand with other repairers?
   A. It would make sharing transport more expensive.
   B. It could blur responsibility for promises made to customers.
   C. It would prevent her from recommending another stall.
   D. It could cause the market to attract too many visitors.

5. What does Ruth emphasise about her workshops?
   A. They are intended to replace the need for professional repairers.
   B. They should demonstrate the most demanding jobs her business undertakes.
   C. Participants need to understand the limits of what the session prepares them to do.
   D. Their value depends on attracting customers for her full service.

6. How do the speakers' plans for expansion differ?
   A. Ruth is attracted to training a team, while Helen mainly wants relief from her own workload.
   B. Helen hopes to open several stalls, while Ruth intends to work alone.
   C. Ruth wants to stop repairing objects, while Helen wants to stop teaching.
   D. Helen expects growth to improve quality automatically, while Ruth rejects growth entirely.

## 作者核对

### 1：C

她说队伍是在数月错估工时之后形成，照片把过程压成一周末成功，C。A队伍是取她已修物；B工作不少，不是没接活；D之前就错估时间，未说照片宣传造成延误。

### 2：A

她改为问what hope will stay the same，A。B不能以自己的finish代客户意愿；C没有估出售价值；D祖母是说明保留情感痕迹的例子，非每件都要查亲属归属。

### 3：D

Helen说说明工序、预先报价且不反复道歉；Ruth允许客户不因使她失望而被迫接受，D概括共同点。A道歉反让人疑价；B她不做道德劝说；C明确replacement sometimes sensible，未承诺总更便宜。

### 4：B

别人许诺她做不到的finish后投诉会及所有人，故需分清责任，B。A她已共用车，没提增费；C转介未被禁；D客流不是顾虑。

### 5：C

准备简单fittings且说明workshop stops，防以为所有旧灯同样易修，C。A未替代专业；B特意简单；D明确not advertisement for full service。

### 6：A

Helen想帮手以能休假、不想连锁；Ruth想两学徒并长期教，A。B方向相反；C两人均无全停该工作计划；D都不把bigger等better，Ruth也愿增长。

### 音频1完整原创脚本

I: My guests today run small repair businesses. Helen Ward repairs outdoor clothing, while Ruth Mason restores lamps. Helen, people often describe your first market stall as an overnight success. Is that how you remember it?

G1: Not quite. There was a wonderful photograph of a queue outside it, and that became the story. Most of the people were waiting to collect things I'd already repaired. It was encouraging to have work, but I hadn't become a better repairer or a better business owner in a single weekend. For months beforehand I'd been going home with half the jobs unfinished because I'd allowed too little time for each one. The queue was the end of a long period of getting my estimates wrong.

I: Was finding customers the hardest part for you as well, Ruth?

G2: I was lucky. A local shop sent people to me from the start. What I struggled with was discussing a job before accepting it. Someone would say, just make it look new, and I'd nod as if we meant the same thing. Then I'd polish a lamp and discover that the mark I'd removed was exactly what reminded them of their grandmother. I now spend longer asking what they hope will stay the same. It isn't the conversation that delays a repair; it's having the wrong conversation.

I: Do customers ever object to paying for a repair when a replacement is cheap?

G1: Certainly, and sometimes replacement is the sensible choice. I explain what the job involves and give a price before starting. I used to apologise for every estimate, as though charging for careful work were slightly embarrassing. That made people wonder whether I believed my own prices. I still want the service to be affordable, but an apology doesn't pay for the hour at the machine.

G2: I'm learning the same thing. I don't defend every repair as morally superior to buying something new. A customer needs a working lamp, not a speech. If the repair would cost much more than they expected, they should be able to walk away without feeling they've disappointed me.

I: There are several other repairers in the market now. Has that made things difficult?

G1: At first I thought I'd have to protect every customer. In practice, I can't repair every kind of material. Someone who comes with a pair of boots is better served by the stall next door. Sending them there means they trust me when I say a jacket really is a job I can do. The other traders send people back, too. The market is becoming somewhere you visit when something breaks, which helps all of us.

G2: We even share a van for deliveries. There are occasional arguments over whose collection is most urgent, but those are manageable. I would be less happy sharing a single brand name. If someone promised a finish I couldn't provide, the complaint would come to all of us. Cooperation works best when customers can still tell who is responsible for the work.

I: You both run occasional workshops. Does teaching people to repair things reduce your own income?

G1: The people who come usually want to mend a small tear themselves. I'm delighted if they can. They also discover how much is involved in a difficult repair, and they're more likely to bring that sort of job to a professional. I don't keep techniques secret to ensure a queue at my stall.

G2: I enjoy the workshops, although they're not an advertisement for my full service. We use simple fittings prepared for the session; I wouldn't want a beginner to leave thinking every old lamp was equally straightforward. I explain where the workshop stops. That boundary is part of teaching responsibly, even if it makes the class sound less impressive.

I: What would you like your businesses to look like in five years?

G1: I would like another pair of hands, mainly so that I can take a proper holiday. I'm not interested in a chain of stalls. There's a limit to how many jobs I can supervise while still knowing what's happening to each jacket.

G2: I'd like a larger workshop and two apprentices. I enjoy the repairs, but teaching someone over several years appeals to me more than being the only expert in a very full room. Helen and I agree that getting bigger isn't automatically getting better. We differ over how much growing would make our own working lives more satisfying.

I: Helen Ward and Ruth Mason, thank you.
