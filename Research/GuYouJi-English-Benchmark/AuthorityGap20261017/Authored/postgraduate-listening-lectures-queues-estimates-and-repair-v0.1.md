## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

R: Today I want to distinguish the time a service takes from what a customer can understand about that time. Imagine two desks in a public office. At one desk, people receive a numbered ticket but no indication of the queue's progress. At the other, the ticket number being served is clearly displayed, together with a warning that complicated cases may take longer. Suppose the actual waiting times are identical. We should not therefore assume that the two experiences are identical. Information can help someone decide whether to remain seated, ask a question, or return later. That does not mean a display can solve a shortage of staff. It means that uncertainty and delay are separate problems, which may require different responses. In a class exercise, I asked two groups to design improvements for the same imaginary office. One group proposed a faster ticket printer. The other proposed explaining why some customers appeared to be called out of order. Those customers had appointments for a different service, but nobody in the waiting area could see that distinction. The second proposal cost less, although the exercise did not establish that it would always be the better choice. If the office were simply failing to serve enough people, clearer explanations would not remove that failure. The important point is to diagnose what a complaint is about before selecting a response. A complaint that somebody has been treated unfairly may involve a different concern from a complaint about the number of minutes spent waiting. When evaluating a change, ask more than whether customers say they are satisfied. Check the actual delay as well, and ask whether people can explain what will happen next. A display that gives a precise but unreliable estimate may create a new problem: people make plans around a promise the office cannot keep. Where the timing of individual cases varies considerably, a broad indication with an honest explanation of uncertainty may be more useful than an exact-looking countdown. Notice that I am not recommending less accuracy. I am recommending that the apparent precision of the information should match what the service can reasonably know. Good communication should make the limits of a prediction visible, rather than hide them behind a more impressive screen.

### 第2段完整原创脚本

R: Let us consider a common mistake in reading an estimate. A project manager says that a task will probably cost between eight and twelve thousand units. A listener repeats the statement as: it will cost ten thousand. The middle of the range is convenient, but repeating it alone changes the information. It removes both the uncertainty and the conditions on which the estimate depends. In the example we will discuss, a small exhibition team had to choose between hiring a finished display system and constructing one to its own design. The hired system had a fixed fee, but limited flexibility. The custom version had a lower initial estimate, which excluded the possible cost of changing the design after construction began. At the planning meeting, the team compared the fixed hiring price with the lowest custom estimate and concluded that building was clearly cheaper. That comparison did not involve two equivalent promises. One price was a defined service under agreed conditions; the other depended on decisions that were still unsettled. It would have been equally careless to conclude that hiring must always be better because its price was more predictable. Flexibility might have been worth paying for, and some changes could have been avoided by making decisions earlier. What the team needed was a comparison that made those trade-offs explicit. Its coordinator asked the builders to separate the basic construction cost from the cost of likely revisions. She also asked which decisions had to be made before work could begin, and what would happen if those decisions changed. The revised comparison did not tell the team which option to value more. It showed what each option included and where the uncertainty lay. This is why a range should not be treated merely as an inconvenient way of writing a single number. Its width can reflect missing information, genuine variation, or different possible choices. To interpret it, ask what would move the outcome towards either end. An estimate becomes more useful when the reasons for its variation are understood. Simply selecting the number that makes a preferred option look attractive does not make the underlying decision more informed; it only makes the comparison look easier than it is.

### 第3段完整原创脚本

R: In the final part of this talk, I want to examine what it means to repair an object successfully. We often start by asking whether the broken part works again. That is a necessary question, but sometimes an insufficient one. Consider an imaginary university that sends its old laboratory stools to a workshop. The seats are stable after repair, and each stool passes the workshop's strength check. Once they are returned, however, technicians find that several are now slightly too high to fit under the benches where they are normally stored. The repair has solved one problem while creating another in the setting of use. Nobody deliberately ignored the benches. The workshop's order form simply described the damaged joints and contained no information about storage space. This example illustrates why a successful technical test should not automatically be treated as a complete account of usefulness. The test answered the question it was designed to answer. The difficulty lay in assuming it had answered every relevant question. Now imagine a second case. A student asks the same workshop to mend an old wooden box inherited from a relative. The box can be made easier to open by replacing its uneven lid with a new one. Yet the marks on the old lid are part of what the student wants to preserve. Here the disagreement is not about whether the replacement would work mechanically. It is about which features are part of the object's value. The repairer should not assume that a cleaner appearance is necessarily an improvement from the owner's point of view. What follows for decision-making? Before beginning, distinguish the fault to be corrected, the conditions in which the object will be used, and the features the owner wishes to retain. These questions do not imply that every repair is worth attempting at any cost. Sometimes the necessary materials are unavailable, or the proposed change cannot satisfy the stated conditions. In that case, explaining the limitation may be more responsible than promising a complete restoration. The aim is to agree on what success would mean before the object is altered. A repair can then be assessed against that agreement, rather than against a standard that seemed obvious to one person but was never shared by the others.
