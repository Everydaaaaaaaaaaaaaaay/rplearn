## 作者核对

### 第1段完整原创脚本

R: At the fictional Fern Lane Museum, a wall clock used to be described as the oldest object in the building. The description had been copied from an early guidebook and repeated for years. When a volunteer found a purchase receipt, she noticed that the date was much later than the label suggested. The museum did not immediately replace one confident date with another. A clock can contain parts made at different times, and the receipt might record a sale rather than the date of manufacture. The volunteer photographed the receipt and asked a specialist to examine the clock. The resulting label explained what the museum knew about the purchase and which questions remained unanswered. Some visitors missed the simple claim that it was the oldest object. Others said the new account helped them see why dating an object could be difficult. The museum kept the earlier label in its working file instead of destroying it. That way, a future researcher could trace how the description had changed. The volunteer says the most useful discovery was not merely a different date. It was the need to keep the reason for a label available, so that repetition alone would not gradually turn an uncertain statement into an unquestioned fact.

录音问题：What made the volunteer question the clock’s label?

### 第2段完整原创脚本

R: A fictional company offered its staff a choice of lunchtime activities. One option was a short walk led by a volunteer. At first, the organiser measured its success by counting how many people joined. The number increased, but several regular walkers quietly stopped coming. When the organiser asked them why, their answers were practical. The group had begun walking faster in order to return on time, and conversation was becoming difficult for people at the back. The organiser shortened the route instead of asking those people to hurry. She also introduced a pause at one clear meeting point, so nobody had to keep talking while trying to catch up. Attendance did not immediately rise again. However, some former participants returned, and the group could finish within the lunch break at a pace they found comfortable. The organiser kept the attendance count, but stopped treating it as a complete description of the activity. She now asked for comments about the route and pace as well. The change was not presented as medical evidence that walking had improved staff health. It was a response to what this particular group said was preventing them from taking part. A simple count had been useful, but it had not explained the experience behind the number.

### 第3段完整原创脚本

R: When fictional college student Ella moved into a shared house, she found a box of letters addressed to a former resident. They had arrived after he left, and no forwarding address was written on them. Ella first planned to throw them away because most appeared to be advertisements. A housemate suggested checking with the landlord before deciding. The landlord could not give Ella the resident's private address, but agreed to contact him and ask what he wanted done. Two days later, the former resident replied through the landlord. Most envelopes could be recycled, but he was waiting for one letter about a course application. He described the sender and asked whether such an envelope was in the box. Ella found it unopened. Rather than send a photograph of its contents, she arranged to leave the sealed envelope at the letting office, where the resident would collect it. Afterwards, the housemates put a small tray near the door for letters sent to people who no longer lived there. A note explained that the landlord should be contacted before anything was discarded. They did not make a list of former residents' private addresses. The tray gave them a temporary place to keep the letters and a clear next step. It did not turn the current tenants into managers of somebody else's correspondence. Ella was glad she had asked before treating the whole box as rubbish.
