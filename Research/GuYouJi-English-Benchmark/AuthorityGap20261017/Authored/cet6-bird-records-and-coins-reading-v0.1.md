# 六级：鸟类记录与旧硬币两篇阅读

## 学生题面

阅读两篇完整短文。第1—5题依据Passage One，第6—10题依据Passage Two，每题从A—D中选一个最佳答案，完成全部十题。人物、机构、研究、历史案例及事件均为虚构；所需信息全部在题内。

### Passage One

An invented field study on the imaginary island of Emsay begins with an apparently alarming result: volunteers record far fewer sightings of a marsh bird than their predecessors did. A local report describes the difference as evidence that the population has collapsed. The research team does not dismiss that possibility, but asks whether the old and new totals represent comparable opportunities to see the birds. A number written in the same column of two reports need not have been produced by the same procedure.

The earlier volunteers had walked beside the marsh shortly after sunrise throughout spring. The new group, formed after the island's work schedules changed, usually visited in the afternoon and made fewer trips. Its members recorded the birds they saw carefully. The problem was not necessarily dishonesty or carelessness. They had sampled a different set of times with less overall effort. A lower total could reflect fewer birds, fewer occasions for observation, a change in when the birds were visible, or some combination of those factors.

One team member proposes dividing each total by the number of visits. That would address one difference, but not all the others. An average per afternoon visit would still not be directly equivalent to an average per early-morning visit if the chance of observing a bird differed between those periods. Nor could the surviving notes establish exactly how long every historical visit had lasted. The researchers keep the original totals alongside their qualifications, rather than presenting a newly calculated average as if it had repaired every weakness in the comparison.

For the next spring, they arrange visits at both times of day, record their duration and retain observations of conditions that might affect visibility. This design makes the new records more informative about differences within that season. It cannot retroactively supply missing details about earlier years. Even if it shows that afternoon counts tend to be lower, the team will still need care when estimating how much of the historical change that difference explains.

The revised public report therefore distinguishes the fall in recorded sightings from a confirmed fall of the same size in the population. It identifies the latter as a possibility requiring further investigation, not a fact disproved by methodological doubts. The point of questioning the comparison is to improve the basis for action. Uncertainty about what a number represents is a reason to specify the question more carefully, rather than either announce a catastrophe or declare that there is nothing to investigate.

### Passage Two

Consider an imaginary excavation in the former town of Merrow, where researchers find a coin carrying the words “one loaf”. A museum visitor may reasonably ask whether that inscription proves that the coin once bought a loaf of bread. The historian responsible for the display answers that the object offers evidence of a claim about value. Establishing what happened in transactions requires a different kind of evidence. An authority's declared relationship between money and goods is not automatically a record of what every seller accepted.

The coin's date identifies the year its makers intended to place on it. Even if researchers establish that it was produced in that year, the date does not tell them how long it circulated or when it reached the place where it was found. A coin may remain in use, be kept as a souvenir or be moved after losing its earlier function. These are possible histories to investigate, not interchangeable conclusions that can all be announced as facts about this particular object.

A merchant's surviving ledger makes the picture more specific. In one recorded transaction, two such coins were accepted for a loaf during a shortage. That entry supports a claim about an exchange at a stated occasion. It challenges the idea that the inscription always described the transaction rate, but does not prove that the rate was identical throughout the town or throughout the coin's life. The ledger may itself omit informal exchanges that its writer had no reason to record. Bringing documents and objects together can narrow uncertainty without eliminating every question.

An image of wheat on the coin invites another tempting inference: perhaps the surrounding fields produced abundant grain. The historian resists using the image as a harvest report. It might express an official promise, a local identity or a wish for prosperity. Without additional evidence, choosing the most attractive of those interpretations would supply certainty that the design alone could not provide. The image remains historically interesting precisely because researchers can ask why it was selected and how people understood it.

The display consequently places the inscription, the ledger entry and the limits of their relationship beside one another. It does not label the coin useless as evidence, nor present the ledger as a complete account of everyday life. Each source answers some questions more readily than others. A compelling historical account emerges from making their contributions and their limits visible, rather than asking one surviving object to speak for every transaction and every field in a lost town.
