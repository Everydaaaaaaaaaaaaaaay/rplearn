## 学生题面

In this fictional portrait, Inez delivers repaired bicycles from a small workshop to customers who cannot collect them. The journey is not the whole job. She has to make sure that the bicycle arriving at a doorway is the one the customer expects, with the explanation that belongs to its repair.

At first, she memorised the mechanic's comments and repeated them after the ride. On busy days, similar repairs blurred together. (1) ____ The card travelled with the bicycle, while a copy stayed at the workshop. If a customer asked a question she could not answer, Inez wrote it down rather than filling the gap with an impressive guess.

Her route usually crossed a large square. It was the shortest distance on a map, but a market occupied the middle of it twice a week. (2) ____ The extra distance was small, and the predictable arrival mattered more than preserving a route that looked efficient only when the square was empty.

Not every customer was ready when she arrived. Once, a woman asked Inez to leave the bicycle outside an unlocked gate. The shop's rule required a personal handover. (3) ____ The woman chose a later appointment, and the bicycle went back to the workshop for the afternoon. The inconvenience was real, but it did not give Inez permission to change the agreed delivery condition.

When the shop later offered online arrival estimates, Inez asked for a way to update them during a journey. (4) ____ A number that never changed could reassure someone for the wrong reason. She preferred an estimate that honestly reflected a delay to a promise the route could no longer support.

At the end of each week, she showed the workshop the questions customers had asked. (5) ____ The next set of cards included a clearer account of those points. For Inez, a completed delivery meant more than moving a bicycle: it meant improving the connection between a repair and the person who would use it.
