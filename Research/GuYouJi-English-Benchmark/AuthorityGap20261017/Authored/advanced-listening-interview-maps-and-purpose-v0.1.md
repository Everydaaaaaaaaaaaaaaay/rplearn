## 作者核对

### 1：C

Nora先预期软件难，后说coordinates easy、用户关心出口与夜间路径，C概括训练视角未覆盖的优先信息。A与easy对照方向相反；B用户不是索求较低准确性或怀疑测量；D未提车站改建，不能由地图无用推出。

### 2：A

她以decisions a person actually has to make为取舍原则，A。B两周全收导致拥挤而被放弃；C明说没有button代替判断；D稳定性不是所述准则，路径照明与可达性均可能变化。

### 3：D

两同年地图分别为地产与水管服务；新标签问用途，促使看有无，D。A没要求以现代准确性判古图；B她说这比多学术语有用；C同一年且差异来自用途，不是发展阶段。

### 4：B

Nora警惕记住动画却忘历史；Leah对playful bits更宽容，认为先吸引靠近也有价值，B。C两人同意触屏数不等理解；A未讨论自由参观路线；D都不否定展示河流关系的清晰潜力。

### 5：C

投诉说线错，核实发现新锁门与时段，继而检查其他地点，C。A本例线恰正确且无usually依据；B她先核查而非即删；D夜间是例子，不限定可达性仅夜间重要。

### 6：A

两者仍可更改时并行讨论，地图解说互相改变取舍，A。B相反正靠可改；C互相作用没有Nora终审权；D结尾明说不能保证完美，也无受欢迎预测。

### 音频1完整原创脚本

I: Today we're talking about maps in public places. My guests are Nora Ellis, who makes walking maps, and Leah Grant, an exhibition designer. Nora, you trained as a surveyor. Was it a straightforward move into designing maps for visitors?

G1: I assumed the difficult part would be learning the software. In fact, the coordinates were the easy bit. My first map was perfectly accurate and almost useless to someone arriving at the railway station. People wanted to know which exit put them on the right side of a busy road, or whether a path was lit after dark. Neither question had occurred to me at my desk. They weren't asking for less accurate information; they were asking me to notice a different set of things.

I: So did you start putting everything people mentioned onto the page?

G1: For about a fortnight! Then I produced something so crowded that nobody could find the station. I needed a principle for leaving things out. On a walking map, I now give space to decisions a person actually has to make. A cafe may be a useful landmark, but its opening hours belong somewhere else. That still involves judgement. There's no button that tells you which detail a tired visitor will need at a difficult junction.

G2: That sounds familiar. I used to put old maps in exhibitions as if they were transparent windows onto the past. Then we displayed two maps of the same neighbourhood, made in the same year, and visitors thought one must be wrong. One showed property boundaries; the other showed water pipes. It was obvious to us why the streets looked different, but we'd never given people a reason to ask what each map had been made for.

I: How did that change the exhibition?

G2: We kept both maps, but changed the labels. Instead of starting with dates and scale, we asked what job each map could help someone do. People began noticing what was absent, as well as what was drawn. We didn't have to add a lecture about bias. They could see that even a careful map is a selection made for a purpose. That was a more useful discovery than learning another technical term.

I: Exhibitions increasingly let visitors manipulate a map on a screen. Does that help?

G1: It can. In one project, switching between routes helped visitors see how a river divided the town. That relationship was hard to grasp on separate printed sheets. But I get nervous when every feature has to flash or move. You can leave remembering the animation and still have no idea why the bridge was built there.

G2: I'd be a little more generous to the playful bits. Some people won't approach a display until they've seen someone else have fun with it. That first invitation matters, even if it isn't the historical point. Where I agree with Nora is that the invitation needs to lead somewhere. Counting touches on a screen doesn't tell us whether anyone noticed the river.

I: What happens when someone reports a mistake on one of your maps, Nora?

G1: We try to establish what they actually encountered before changing anything. Someone once said we'd put a footpath in the wrong place. The line was right, but a new locked gate made the route unusable in the evening. Fixing the line would have made the map less accurate. We changed the route advice instead, and checked other places where access depended on the time. A small-looking report can reveal a much wider problem.

G2: And an apparent mistake in a historical map can be worth keeping in view. An old street name that looks like a printing error may tell you who was asked to supply the information. Of course, if our own label sends people to the wrong gallery, we correct it. But silently tidying every oddity in an old document would remove some of the evidence.

I: You are now working together on a harbour exhibition. Is Nora supplying the map and Leah arranging it afterwards?

G2: That was the original timetable. Fortunately, we changed it before either of us had done much. We sat down with the visitor route and the draft map together. A detail Nora could make clear with one line would have taken me a wall of explanation; a story I wanted to tell showed her why an apparently minor landing place deserved to be visible.

G1: Yes, the useful conversations happened while both things were still alterable. If we'd treated the map as a finished object to be delivered, we'd probably have spent the last week arguing about its size. Instead we're asking what each part can do for the whole visit. That won't guarantee a perfect exhibition, but it gives us a better question to work on.

I: Nora Ellis and Leah Grant, thank you both.
